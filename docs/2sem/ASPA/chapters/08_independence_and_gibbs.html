9.1 Introduction

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, introduced in the previous chapter, is a general framework for constructing Markov chains with a target stationary distribution. However, the choice of proposal distribution critically affects both computational efficiency and the quality of samples. In this chapter, we explore two important special cases that reveal a fundamental trade-off in MCMC design: global vs. local exploration, and the role of acceptance rates in controlling mixing.

The independence sampler uses a fixed global proposal, independent of the current state. This allows long-distance jumps but requires the proposal to approximate the target well. The Gibbs sampler, by contrast, updates coordinates sequentially using exact full conditional distributions, guaranteeing acceptance but potentially moving slowly through high-dimensional spaces when parameters are correlated. Both methods illustrate how MCMC designers must balance the competing demands of efficient exploration and computational tractability.

9.2 The independence sampler: global proposals

In a random-walk Metropolis algorithm, the proposal distribution depends on the current state: \[ q(x' \mid x) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{\|x' - x\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right). \]

This locality allows the chain to take small, tentative steps and gradually explore the target space. However, if the proposal variance is too small, the chain mixes slowly; if too large, it wastes computation on distant, low-probability regions.

The independence sampler takes a different approach: the proposal is independent of the current state, \[ q(x') = q(x' \mid x), \] depending only on \(x'\). This allows the chain to jump from anywhere to anywhere in a single step, potentially escaping local modes and exploring the target space more globally.

  • Acceptance probability. For a generic Metropolis–Hastings step the acceptance probability is \[ \alpha(x \to x') = \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(x') \, q(x \mid x')}{\pi(x) \, q(x' \mid x)}\right). \] For the independence sampler, \(q(x \mid x') = q(x)\) and \(q(x' \mid x) = q(x')\), so \[ \alpha(x \to x') = \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(x') \, q(x)}{\pi(x) \, q(x')}\right). \] Rearranging in terms of importance weights \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\): \[ \alpha(x \to x') = \min\left(1, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right). \]

The acceptance probability compares the importance weights at the proposed and current states. If \(w(x')\) is larger, the proposal is always accepted. If \(w(x')\) is smaller, it is accepted with probability \(w(x') / w(x)\). When the proposal closely matches the target, weights are nearly constant and acceptance is high.

  • Connection to importance sampling and ESS. The independence sampler inherits its theoretical motivation from importance sampling. In importance sampling, we approximate an expectation \[ E_\pi[f(x)] = \int f(x) \, \pi(x) \, dx \] by sampling from a simpler proposal \(q\) and weighting: \[ \widehat{E_\pi}[f(x)] = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N w(x_i) f(x_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^N w(x_i)}, \quad w(x_i) = \frac{\pi(x_i)}{q(x_i)}. \] The effective sample size (ESS) reflects how much information the weighted samples contain. If weights are unequal—as they are when \(q\) deviates from \(\pi\)—the ESS is smaller than \(N\): \[ \text{ESS} = \frac{1}{\sum_i (w_i / \bar{w})^2}. \] When \(w(x)\) varies widely, many samples receive vanishingly small weight, and the variance of the estimator becomes very large. The independence sampler’s acceptance rate is directly related to the relative variation in weights: a narrow or poorly-matched proposal produces highly variable weights, leading to low acceptance and slow exploration. This is the narrow-proposal failure mode — if the proposal is too concentrated, the chain accepts rarely and gets stuck.

  • When it works well. The key requirement is that \(q\) must approximate \(\pi\) globally. A sufficient ergodicity condition is \[ \pi(x) \leq C \, q(x) \quad \forall x \] for some finite constant \(C > 0\) (analogous to the envelope condition in rejection sampling), which ensures the chain does not get trapped in regions of arbitrarily high relative weight. The independence sampler is best when we have a good global approximation to the posterior — e.g., from a Laplace approximation, variational inference, or other fast approximate methods — and when the target is unimodal or has well-separated modes the proposal can accommodate. It fails when the proposal underestimates the tails or misses modes, so that states with large \(\pi(x)/q(x)\) are rare and hard to reach.

  • Curse of dimensionality. A profound limitation emerges in high dimensions. Suppose \(\log w(x) = \log \pi(x) - \log q(x)\) decomposes as a sum of roughly independent contributions: \[ \log w(x) \approx \sum_{i=1}^d \Delta_i(x_i), \] where each \(\Delta_i\) represents the local mismatch between \(\pi\) and \(q\) in coordinate \(i\). Even if each \(\Delta_i\) is small (order \(O(1)\)), the total deviation is \(\log w(x) = O(d)\), so \(w(x) = \exp(O(d))\). The weights vary exponentially in dimension, and the acceptance rate typically decays like \(P(A) \sim \exp(-cd)\).

Concentration of measure: In high dimensions, probability mass concentrates on thin regions. For a standard Gaussian \(\pi = N(0, I_d)\), the norm \(\|x\|\) concentrates around \(\sqrt{d}\). Even a slightly mis-scaled proposal \(q\) will place its probability mass in regions irrelevant to \(\pi\), causing acceptance to decay exponentially with \(d\).

This is the motivation for local proposals (random-walk Metropolis), gradient-based methods (Hamiltonian MC), and coordinate-wise samplers (Gibbs sampling), which we turn to next.

9.3 The Gibbs sampler: coordinate-wise updates

In many Bayesian models, jointly sampling from \(p(\theta \mid D)\) where \(\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)\) is intractable, but sampling from each full conditional distribution is simple.

The full conditional distribution of \(\theta_i\) is \[ p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D), \] where \(\theta_{-i} = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_{i-1}, \theta_{i+1}, \ldots, \theta_d)\) denotes all other parameters. It is obtained by treating \(\theta_{-i}\) as fixed and proportional to the joint posterior: \[ p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \propto p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i} \mid D). \]

9.3.1 Algorithm: deterministic and random scan

Gibbs sampling iteratively samples each parameter from its full conditional.

Gibbs Sampler Algorithm

Initialize \(\theta^{(0)} = (\theta_1^{(0)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(0)})\).

For \(t = 1, 2, \ldots\):

  1. \(\theta_1^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t-1)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(t-1)}, D)\)
  2. \(\theta_2^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t)}, \theta_3^{(t-1)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(t-1)}, D)\)
  3. \(\vdots\)
  4. \(\theta_d^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_d \mid \theta_1^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_{d-1}^{(t)}, D)\)

One complete cycle of all \(d\) updates constitutes one iteration of the Gibbs sampler.

Variants: The random-scan Gibbs sampler updates coordinates in random order instead of a fixed sequence. For convergence, both deterministic and random scans are ergodic under mild conditions.

  • Gibbs as a special case of Metropolis–Hastings. Gibbs sampling can be viewed as Metropolis–Hastings with the proposal being the full conditional itself. At step \(t\), we propose a new value for \(\theta_i\) by sampling from \[ q(\theta_i' \mid \theta_i^{(t-1)}, \theta_{-i}^{(t)}) = p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D). \] The acceptance probability is \[ \alpha(\theta^{(t-1)} \to \theta^{(t)}) = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta_i^{(t)}, \theta_{-i}^{(t)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t-1)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)}{p(\theta_i^{(t-1)}, \theta_{-i}^{(t-1)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)}\right). \] Using the factorization \(p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i} \mid D) = p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i} \mid D)\), the ratio simplifies: \[ \frac{p(\theta_i^{(t)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i}^{(t)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t-1)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)}{p(\theta_i^{(t-1)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t-1)}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i}^{(t-1)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)} = 1. \] Therefore, \(\alpha = 1\): every Gibbs step is accepted.

Acceptance is guaranteed, but samples are still correlated. The chain does not jump to a new state at each step; instead, it updates coordinates sequentially, and autocorrelation arises because coordinates are strongly dependent.

9.3.2 Detailed balance for Gibbs updates

For the full Gibbs update (one complete sweep of all \(d\) coordinates), detailed balance holds with respect to the joint posterior. We verify that \[ p(\theta \mid D) \, T(\theta \to \theta') = p(\theta' \mid D) \, T(\theta' \to \theta), \] where \(T\) is the transition kernel of one complete sweep.

For a single coordinate update \(T_i\) applied to coordinate \(i\), left-multiply by \(p(\theta \mid D)\) and expand using the factorization: \[\begin{align} p(\theta \mid D) \, T_i(\theta \to \theta') &= p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, \delta(\theta_{-i}' - \theta_{-i})\\ &= p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, \delta(\theta_{-i}' - \theta_{-i}). \end{align}\]

Permuting terms and using \(p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) = p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}', D)\) (since \(\theta_{-i} = \theta_{-i}'\)): \[ = p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, \delta(\theta_{-i}' - \theta_{-i}) = p(\theta' \mid D) \, T_i(\theta' \to \theta). \]

Thus each coordinate-wise update preserves the posterior. Since the full sweep is a composition of such updates, it also satisfies detailed balance.

  • Mixing and slow mixing from correlation. Unlike the independence sampler, Gibbs always accepts. However, this does not imply fast mixing. When parameters are strongly correlated — as in high-dimensional hierarchical models or when the posterior is elongated along a narrow ridge — coordinate-wise updates must take many small steps to explore the space.

Bivariate Gaussian with high correlation

Consider a bivariate normal distribution: \[ \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix} \sim N\left( \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix} \right). \]

The full conditionals are: \[ X \mid Y = y \sim N(\rho y, 1 - \rho^2), \quad Y \mid X = x \sim N(\rho x, 1 - \rho^2). \]

When \(\rho = 0.9\), the posterior concentrates on a narrow diagonal ridge. A Gibbs step first moves horizontally (X-update), then vertically (Y-update), taking many iterations to traverse the ridge. In contrast, updating both coordinates jointly (e.g., via a rotated-coordinate Gibbs or HMC) moves along the ridge in fewer steps.

  • Metropolis-within-Gibbs and block Gibbs. When the full conditional for a component is intractable, we can replace the Gibbs step with a Metropolis–Hastings step; when several coordinates are strongly correlated, we can group them and update them jointly.

Metropolis-within-Gibbs: Replace the Gibbs step for \(\theta_i\) with a Metropolis–Hastings move using a local proposal \(q_i(\theta_i' \mid \theta_i)\). The acceptance probability is \[ \alpha_i(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}) = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, q_i(\theta_i \mid \theta_i')}{p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, q_i(\theta_i' \mid \theta_i)}\right). \]

The chain remains valid (it still has \(p(\theta \mid D)\) as stationary distribution), but some steps are rejected.

Block Gibbs sampling updates multiple coordinates jointly, \[ (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_k) \sim p(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_k \mid \theta_{k+1}, \ldots, \theta_d, D), \] which is effective when joint conditionals are tractable and dramatically reduces autocorrelation in highly correlated subsets.

9.4 Worked example: Gibbs sampling for a bivariate Gaussian

We now demonstrate Gibbs sampling on the bivariate Gaussian with \(\rho = 0.9\) to illustrate slow mixing due to correlation, and show how a simple coordinate rotation accelerates mixing.

9.4.1 Slow mixing at high correlation

Show code 
set.seed(42)

gibbs_bivariate <- function(n_steps, rho, start = c(0, 0)) {
  x <- numeric(n_steps)
  y <- numeric(n_steps)
  x[1] <- start[1]
  y[1] <- start[2]
  cond_sd <- sqrt(1 - rho^2)

  for (t in 2:n_steps) {
    # Update X | Y
    x[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = rho * y[t - 1], sd = cond_sd)
    # Update Y | X
    y[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = rho * x[t], sd = cond_sd)
  }

  cbind(x = x, y = y)
}

rho_high <- 0.9
chain_high <- gibbs_bivariate(n_steps = 10000, rho = rho_high, start = c(-3, 3))

# Diagnostics

9.4.2 Rotated coordinates for faster mixing

The slow mixing arises because the Gibbs updates align with the coordinate axes, while the posterior concentrates along the diagonal. We can decouple the correlation by rotating coordinates. Let \(u = \frac{x + y}{\sqrt{2}}\) and \(v = \frac{x - y}{\sqrt{2}}\). For a standard bivariate Gaussian, the joint distribution of \((u, v)\) is: \[ u \sim N(0, (1 + \rho)), \quad v \sim N(0, (1 - \rho)), \] and \(u\) and \(v\) are independent.

Show code 
gibbs_rotated <- function(n_steps, rho, start = c(0, 0)) {
  # Transform starting point to rotated coordinates
  u_start <- (start[1] + start[2]) / sqrt(2)
  v_start <- (start[1] - start[2]) / sqrt(2)

  u <- numeric(n_steps)
  v <- numeric(n_steps)
  u[1] <- u_start
  v[1] <- v_start

  sd_u <- sqrt(1 + rho)
  sd_v <- sqrt(1 - rho)

  for (t in 2:n_steps) {
    # u and v are now independent, so Gibbs reduces to independent sampling
    u[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = 0, sd = sd_u)
    v[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = 0, sd = sd_v)
  }

  # Transform back to original coordinates
  x <- (u + v) / sqrt(2)
  y <- (u - v) / sqrt(2)

  cbind(x = x, y = y)
}

9.5 Comparison: independence sampler vs. Gibbs sampler vs. random-walk Metropolis

The three samplers embody different philosophies and trade-offs:

Aspect Independence Sampler Gibbs Sampler Random-Walk MH
Proposal \(q(x')\) (global, fixed) \(p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D)\) \(x' \sim N(x, \Sigma)\)
Move type Global jumps anywhere Coordinate-wise updates Local steps
Acceptance MH ratio (often low) Always 1 (deterministic) Variable (tunable)
Main requirement Good global approximation Tractable conditionals Reasonable scale estimate
Fails when \(q\) poorly matches \(\pi\) Strong parameter correlations Proposal scale wrong
Autocorrelation Low if \(q \approx \pi\) High if correlated structure Moderate (tunable)
Dimension scaling Exponential decay \(\sim \exp(-cd)\) Polynomial (depends on correlation) Exponential decay
Typical use Approximate posteriors Conjugate/hierarchical models Flexible fallback

Remark. In practice, these are rarely used in isolation. Modern samplers often combine ideas:

  • Metropolis-within-Gibbs: Use Gibbs for tractable coordinates, MH for intractable ones.
  • Block Gibbs: Group correlated coordinates and sample jointly to reduce autocorrelation.
  • Adaptive proposals: Learn a proposal from preliminary runs (adaptive MCMC).
  • HMC within Gibbs: Combine gradient-based efficient exploration with block structure.

The choice depends on the model structure, the availability of efficient samplers for conditionals, and computational constraints.

9.6 Summary

The independence sampler represents a global exploration strategy: it can jump anywhere but only works well if the proposal closely approximates the target. Its acceptance probability directly reflects the importance weights, making it a bridge between importance sampling and MCMC. However, the exponential degradation of weights in high dimensions—a manifestation of concentration of measure—limits its applicability to high-dimensional problems.

The Gibbs sampler exploits model structure by updating parameters from their full conditionals. Every step is accepted, eliminating the rejection waste of Metropolis algorithms. However, when parameters are strongly correlated, the chain mixes slowly because coordinate-wise moves struggle to explore elongated posterior geometries efficiently. Rotation to decorrelated coordinates, block updates, or auxiliary variable methods can dramatically improve performance.

Both methods highlight the central tension in MCMC: acceptance rates, exploration speed, and computational tractability are not independent. A low acceptance rate signals a poorly matched proposal; a high rate (even 100%) does not guarantee fast mixing if the effective step size is small. Successful MCMC practice requires diagnosing mixing, adapting proposals, and sometimes redesigning the parameterization to align with the posterior structure.

9.7 Exercises

(Written test, 15 June 2026 — Q6.) A discrete-time Markov chain has state space \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) and transition matrix \[T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - a - b & a & b \\ b & 1 - a - b & a \\ a & b & 1 - a - b \end{pmatrix},\] where \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), and \(a + b \leq 1\).

  1. Find the stationary distribution \(\pi = (\pi_0, \pi_1, \pi_2)\).
  2. State the detailed-balance equations for this chain.
  3. Derive the condition on \(a\) and \(b\) under which detailed balance can hold.

Solution. We are asked to find the stationary vector of a cyclic \(3 \times 3\) transition matrix, write out its detailed-balance equations, and identify when detailed balance can hold.

(a) Stationary distribution. A stationary distribution satisfies \(\pi T = \pi\) with \(\sum_i \pi_i = 1\). Inspecting \(T\) we see that every column also sums to \(1\): column \(j\) contains one occurrence of each of \(\{1-a-b,\,a,\,b\}\), whose sum is \(1\). A matrix whose rows and columns both sum to \(1\) is doubly stochastic, and for any doubly stochastic \(T\) on \(n\) states the uniform vector is invariant, because \[(\pi T)_j = \sum_i \tfrac{1}{n} T(i,j) = \tfrac{1}{n} \sum_i T(i,j) = \tfrac{1}{n}.\] Hence \(\pi = (\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})\).

(b) Detailed-balance equations. Detailed balance (the reversibility condition invoked throughout the chapter, e.g. in § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates) is \(\pi_i T(i,j) = \pi_j T(j,i)\) for every pair \(i \neq j\). With \(\pi_i = 1/3\) this simplifies to the symmetry conditions \[T(0,1)=T(1,0),\quad T(1,2)=T(2,1),\quad T(0,2)=T(2,0).\]

(c) Condition on \(a,b\). Reading off entries: \(T(0,1)=a\) and \(T(1,0)=b\); \(T(1,2)=a\) and \(T(2,1)=b\); \(T(0,2)=b\) and \(T(2,0)=a\). All three equations reduce to \(a=b\). Thus detailed balance holds iff \(a=b\). When \(a\neq b\) there is a net cyclic probability flux \(a-b\) around \(0\to 1\to 2\to 0\): the chain is stationary but not reversible — exactly the pathology anticipated by the exercise on cyclic chains below.

Cross-reference: § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates (reversibility definition \(\pi(x)T(x\to y) = \pi(y)T(y\to x)\)) and the doubly-stochastic argument for uniform stationarity.

Two-state Markov chain with \(T(0 \to 1) = \alpha\) and \(T(1 \to 0) = \beta\), where \(\alpha, \beta \in (0, 1)\). Find the stationary distribution \(\pi\) in closed form and verify detailed balance.

Solution. We are asked to find the stationary distribution of a two-state Markov chain with off-diagonal transition probabilities \(\alpha,\beta\) and to check detailed balance.

Set-up. The full transition matrix is \[T = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\alpha & \alpha \\ \beta & 1-\beta \end{pmatrix}.\] A stationary distribution \(\pi = (\pi_0, \pi_1)\) satisfies \(\pi T = \pi\), i.e. \[\pi_0(1-\alpha) + \pi_1 \beta = \pi_0, \qquad \pi_0 \alpha + \pi_1 (1-\beta) = \pi_1.\] Both equations reduce to the single balance condition \[\pi_0 \alpha = \pi_1 \beta. \tag{$\ast$}\] Combining with the normalisation \(\pi_0 + \pi_1 = 1\) we solve \[\pi_0 = \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}, \qquad \pi_1 = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}.\]

Detailed balance. The reversibility condition from § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates is \(\pi_i T(i \to j) = \pi_j T(j \to i)\) for all \(i \neq j\). Here there is a single off-diagonal pair to check: \[\pi_0 T(0\to 1) = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot \alpha = \frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot\beta = \pi_1 T(1\to 0). \checkmark\] Equation \((\ast)\) is identical to detailed balance, so the two-state chain is always reversible whenever \(\alpha, \beta \in (0,1)\) — this is a special feature of \(n=2\): there is only one pair of states and hence no room for a cyclic flux like the one flagged in the note following \(§\) Detailed balance.

Cross-reference: § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates (reversibility \(\pi(x)T(x\to y) = \pi(y)T(y\to x)\)).

Three-state birth-death chain on \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) with \(T(i \to i+1) = p\) and \(T(i \to i-1) = q\) (where defined), self-loops chosen so each row sums to one. Find the stationary distribution in terms of the ratio \(r = p/q\).

Solution. We must find the stationary distribution of a three-state birth-death chain in which each interior state can move up with probability \(p\) or down with probability \(q\) (with self-loops making the rows sum to \(1\)), expressed via \(r = p/q\).

Why we may use detailed balance directly. In a birth-death chain the only non-self-loop transitions connect neighbouring states \(i\) and \(i \pm 1\). The graph of allowed transitions is a tree (a path), so it contains no cycles, and any stationary distribution of such a chain is automatically reversible — because the pairwise fluxes across each cut of the path must balance separately. This lets us apply the detailed-balance recursion from § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates rather than solving the full linear system \(\pi T = \pi\).

Recursion. For each pair \((i,i+1)\) the detailed-balance equation \(\pi_i T(i \to i+1) = \pi_{i+1} T(i+1 \to i)\) becomes \[\pi_i \, p = \pi_{i+1} \, q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_{i+1} = \frac{p}{q}\, \pi_i = r\,\pi_i.\] Iterating from \(i=0\): \[\pi_1 = r\,\pi_0, \qquad \pi_2 = r\,\pi_1 = r^2\,\pi_0.\]

Normalisation. Requiring \(\pi_0 + \pi_1 + \pi_2 = 1\) gives \(\pi_0(1 + r + r^2) = 1\), so \[\pi = \frac{1}{1 + r + r^2}\,(1,\, r,\, r^2).\] When \(r < 1\) the distribution is peaked at \(0\); when \(r > 1\) it is peaked at \(2\); when \(r = 1\) we recover the uniform stationary distribution.

Cross-reference: § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates (reversibility for nearest-neighbour chains on a path graph).

Construct a 3-state Markov chain that admits a stationary distribution but does not satisfy detailed balance. (Hint: try a deterministic cycle \(0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0\).) Verify both claims explicitly.

Solution. We must exhibit a 3-state Markov chain that has a stationary distribution but violates detailed balance, then verify each claim explicitly.

Construction. Take the deterministic cyclic shift \[T(i, i+1 \bmod 3) = 1, \qquad T(i,j) = 0 \text{ otherwise},\] so \[T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Stationarity. The rows sum to \(1\) (stochasticity) and the columns also sum to \(1\): each column has exactly one entry equal to \(1\). Hence \(T\) is doubly stochastic, and by the same doubly-stochastic argument used elsewhere in this chapter the uniform distribution is invariant: \[(\pi T)_j = \sum_i \tfrac{1}{3} T(i,j) = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 1 = \pi_j.\]

Failure of detailed balance. The condition \(\pi_i T(i,j) = \pi_j T(j,i)\) from § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates requires the pairwise fluxes to match. But \[\pi_0 T(0,1) = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{3}, \qquad \pi_1 T(1,0) = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 0 = 0,\] so the flux \(0 \to 1\) is \(1/3\) whereas the reverse flux \(1 \to 0\) is \(0\). There is a strictly positive net probability current \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) around the cycle \(0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0\).

Interpretation. The chain is stationary because the net current into every state equals the net current out — the incoming flux from the cyclic predecessor \(i-1\) exactly balances the outgoing flux to the successor \(i+1\). But this global “circulation” balance is weaker than the pairwise detailed-balance condition; the chain is not time-reversible. This is precisely the counter-example anticipated by the note following the derivation of detailed balance in the chapter body.

Cross-reference: § Detailed balance for Gibbs updates (definition of reversibility and the distinction from mere stationarity).

Independence sampler with target \(\pi(x) = \text{Beta}(2, 2)\) on \((0,1)\) and proposal \(q(x) = \text{Uniform}(0,1)\). Write the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability \(\alpha(x \to x')\) in terms of \(x\), \(x'\), and identify the smallest constant \(M\) that would make every proposal accepted in a rejection sampler.

Solution. The exercise asks for the independence-sampler acceptance probability when the target is \(\pi = \mathrm{Beta}(2,2)\) and the proposal is \(q = \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\), and the smallest envelope constant that would make rejection sampling accept every proposal.

Densities. The Beta\((\alpha,\beta)\) density with \(\alpha=\beta=2\) is \[\pi(x) = \frac{\Gamma(4)}{\Gamma(2)^2}\, x^{2-1}(1-x)^{2-1} = 6\,x(1-x), \qquad x \in (0,1),\] and \(q(x) = 1\) on \((0,1)\).

Importance weight. Directly from the definition \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\) introduced in § The independence sampler: global proposals we get \[w(x) = 6\,x(1-x).\]

Acceptance probability. By the independence-sampler acceptance formula derived in that same section, \[\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{6\,x'(1-x')}{6\,x(1-x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{x'(1-x')}{x(1-x)}\right\}.\] The constant \(6\) cancels — as it must for any independence sampler, since acceptance depends only on ratios of unnormalised weights.

Smallest envelope constant. For rejection sampling with envelope \(Mq(x) \geq \pi(x)\) everywhere, the smallest admissible \(M\) is \[M = \sup_{x \in (0,1)} \frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)} = \sup_{x \in (0,1)} 6\,x(1-x).\] The function \(x(1-x)\) is maximised at \(x = 1/2\) (set derivative \(1-2x = 0\)) with value \(1/4\), so \[M = 6 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{2}.\] Equivalently, the envelope condition \(\pi \leq C q\) from § The independence sampler holds with \(C = 3/2 < \infty\), so this sampler is uniformly ergodic.

Cross-reference: § The independence sampler: global proposals (acceptance formula \(\alpha = \min\{1, w(x')/w(x)\}\) and envelope condition \(\pi \leq Cq\)).

Independence sampler with target \(\pi = \text{Exp}(1)\) and proposal \(q = \text{Exp}(2)\). Compute the importance weight \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\) and the acceptance ratio \(\alpha(x \to x') = \min\{1, w(x')/w(x)\}\).

Solution. We must compute the importance weight and acceptance ratio for an independence sampler with target \(\pi = \mathrm{Exp}(1)\) and proposal \(q = \mathrm{Exp}(2)\).

Densities. The rate-\(\lambda\) exponential has density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) on \([0,\infty)\). Hence \[\pi(x) = e^{-x}, \qquad q(x) = 2\,e^{-2x}, \qquad x \geq 0.\]

Importance weight. By the definition \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\) from § The independence sampler: global proposals, \[w(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{2\,e^{-2x}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-x + 2x} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{x}.\]

Acceptance ratio. By the independence-sampler acceptance formula in the same section, \[\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x'}}{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x}}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, e^{x' - x}\right\}.\] Proposals to larger states are always accepted, whereas proposals to smaller states are accepted with probability \(e^{x'-x}\).

Pathology: unbounded weights. Because the proposal has rate \(2 > 1\) (heavier decay than the target), its tail is lighter than \(\pi\)’s. Formally \(w(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}e^{x} \to \infty\) as \(x \to \infty\), so no finite envelope \(C\) makes \(\pi \leq Cq\) hold on all of \([0,\infty)\). The envelope condition of § The independence sampler is violated, and the chain is not uniformly ergodic: whenever it wanders into a large \(x\) its acceptance probability for the next proposal \(x' \sim q\) (which typically has small \(x'\)) is \(e^{x'-x} \ll 1\), and the chain stalls — exactly the narrow-proposal / light-tail failure mode discussed in the chapter.

Cross-reference: § The independence sampler: global proposals (importance-weight definition, acceptance formula, envelope condition, and the “narrow-proposal failure mode” paragraph).

Show that an independence sampler with proposal \(q = \pi\) accepts every move and therefore generates i.i.d. samples from \(\pi\).

Solution. We must show that an independence sampler whose proposal \(q\) equals the target \(\pi\) accepts every move and therefore generates i.i.d. samples from \(\pi\).

Importance weight. From the definition \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\) in § The independence sampler: global proposals, setting \(q = \pi\) gives \[w(x) = \frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)} = 1\] for every \(x\) in the support.

Acceptance probability. By the independence-sampler acceptance formula \[\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{1}{1}\right\} = 1,\] for every pair \((x, x')\). Every proposal is accepted deterministically.

I.i.d. samples. The transition kernel of an independence sampler is \[T(x \to x') = q(x')\,\alpha(x \to x') + \delta(x'-x)\bigl(1 - \bar\alpha(x)\bigr),\] where \(\bar\alpha(x) = \int q(y)\alpha(x\to y)\,dy\). With \(\alpha \equiv 1\) we get \(\bar\alpha(x) = \int q(y)\,dy = 1\), so the delta-rejection piece vanishes and \[T(x \to x') = q(x') = \pi(x').\] The next state is drawn from \(\pi\) regardless of the current state — that is exactly the statement that the successive draws are independent and identically distributed as \(\pi\). Combined with the envelope bound \(w \leq 1 < \infty\) (the strongest possible case of the condition in § The independence sampler), this shows why \(q = \pi\) is the ideal — indeed unattainable — target for independence-sampler design.

Cross-reference: § The independence sampler: global proposals (weight definition, acceptance formula, envelope condition \(\pi \leq Cq\)).

Explain qualitatively why an independence sampler whose proposal \(q\) has lighter tails than the target \(\pi\) tends to get stuck in the tails of \(\pi\). Express your answer in terms of the importance weight \(w(x)\).

Solution. We are asked to explain qualitatively, in terms of the importance weight \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\), why an independence sampler whose proposal \(q\) has lighter tails than the target \(\pi\) tends to get stuck in the tails of \(\pi\).

Weight blows up in the tail. “Light tails for \(q\)” means \(q(x)\) decays faster than \(\pi(x)\) as \(|x|\to\infty\). Therefore the importance weight introduced in § The independence sampler: global proposals, \[w(x) = \frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)},\] diverges as \(|x| \to \infty\). The envelope condition \(\pi \leq C\,q\) from the same section cannot hold with any finite \(C\), so the sampler is not uniformly ergodic.

Consequence for acceptance. Suppose by some rare fluctuation the chain reaches a tail state \(x\) with \(w(x)\) very large. A fresh proposal \(x' \sim q\) typically comes from the bulk of \(q\) (which is close to the mode of \(\pi\)), so \(w(x') = O(1)\), much smaller than \(w(x)\). By the independence-sampler acceptance formula from that section, \[\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} \approx \frac{w(x')}{w(x)} \ll 1.\] Almost every proposal is rejected, and the chain remains at \(x\) for many steps.

Sticking pathology. The expected holding time at \(x\) is roughly \(1/\bar\alpha(x)\), where \(\bar\alpha(x) = \int q(y)\alpha(x\to y)\,dy \approx E_q[w]/w(x) \to 0\) as \(w(x) \to \infty\). This is precisely the narrow-proposal failure mode diagnosed in the chapter body: because \(w\) is unbounded, the chain “sticks” in the tail, producing extreme autocorrelation and biased Monte Carlo estimates. It is the MCMC analogue of the pathology in importance sampling where a single sample with huge \(w\) dominates the sum \(\sum_i w(x_i) f(x_i) / \sum_i w(x_i)\) and destroys the effective sample size (see the ESS discussion in the same section).

Cross-reference: § The independence sampler: global proposals (weight definition, envelope condition \(\pi \leq Cq\), ESS and “narrow-proposal failure mode”).

Gibbs sampler for a bivariate Gaussian \((X, Y) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\) with \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Derive the full conditionals \(p(x \mid y)\) and \(p(y \mid x)\) and give a Gibbs sweep update rule.

Solution. We must derive the two full conditionals of the centred bivariate Gaussian with correlation \(\rho\) and write down one Gibbs sweep.

Full-conditional definition. By the definition of a full conditional in § The Gibbs sampler: coordinate-wise updates, \[p(x \mid y) \propto p(x, y),\] treating \(y\) as fixed. The joint log density of \((X,Y) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\) with \(\Sigma = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) is (up to a constant in \(x\)) \[\log p(x, y) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,(x, y)\, \Sigma^{-1} (x, y)^\top + \text{const}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}.\] Expanding the quadratic form, \[-\tfrac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(x^2 - 2\rho x y + y^2\bigr).\]

Completing the square in \(x\). Dropping terms independent of \(x\), \[\log p(x\mid y) = -\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(x^2 - 2\rho x y\bigr) + \text{const} = -\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(x - \rho y\bigr)^2 + \text{const}.\] Reading off the Gaussian form, \[X \mid Y = y \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho y,\, 1 - \rho^2\bigr).\] By symmetry of the joint distribution under swapping the roles of \(X\) and \(Y\), \[Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho x,\, 1 - \rho^2\bigr).\] (Equivalently, from the standard formula \(E[X\mid Y] = \Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}Y = \rho Y\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X\mid Y) = 1 - \rho\cdot\rho = 1 - \rho^2\).)

One Gibbs sweep. By the Gibbs Sampler Algorithm theorem in § Algorithm: deterministic and random scan, one iteration is \[x^{(t)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho\, y^{(t-1)},\, 1-\rho^2\bigr), \qquad y^{(t)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho\, x^{(t)},\, 1-\rho^2\bigr).\] Note that the conditional variance \(1-\rho^2\) shrinks as \(|\rho|\to 1\) — this is exactly the mechanism behind the slow mixing shown in the worked example on the bivariate Gaussian.

Cross-reference: § The Gibbs sampler: coordinate-wise updates (full-conditional definition) and § Algorithm: deterministic and random scan (Gibbs sweep).

Beta–Binomial hierarchical model: \(x \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)\), \(\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\). Derive the full conditional \(p(\theta \mid x, \alpha, \beta)\) and identify it as a Beta distribution.

Solution. By Bayes’ rule, \[p(\theta \mid x, \alpha, \beta) \propto p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta) \propto \theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x}\cdot \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1} = \theta^{\alpha + x - 1}(1-\theta)^{\beta + n - x - 1}.\] This is the kernel of a Beta distribution, so \[\theta \mid x, \alpha, \beta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha + x,\, \beta + n - x).\] Conjugacy makes the Gibbs update a closed-form Beta draw.

Two-component Gaussian mixture: \(X_i \mid Z_i = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, 1)\) with \(Z_i \in \{1, 2\}\) and \(P(Z_i = 1) = w\). Given \(\mu_1, \mu_2, w\) and the data, write the full conditional \(p(z_i = 1 \mid x_i)\) (the E-step of EM, but in the Gibbs context).

Solution. By Bayes’ rule, with \(\phi(x;\mu) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^2\}\), \[p(z_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \frac{w\,\phi(x_i;\mu_1)}{w\,\phi(x_i;\mu_1) + (1-w)\,\phi(x_i;\mu_2)}.\] Cancelling the common \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) factor gives the explicit form \[p(z_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \frac{w\,\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i-\mu_1)^2\}}{w\,\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i-\mu_1)^2\} + (1-w)\,\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i-\mu_2)^2\}}.\] The Gibbs update samples \(z_i\) from this Bernoulli; the EM E-step uses the same expression as a soft responsibility.

Show that the Gibbs sampler is a special case of Metropolis–Hastings in which the proposal for component \(k\) is the full conditional \(\pi(x_k \mid x_{-k})\), and that the corresponding acceptance probability is always \(1\).

Solution. Take the MH proposal \(q(x_k' \mid x_k, x_{-k}) = \pi(x_k' \mid x_{-k})\). The MH ratio factorises as \[\frac{\pi(x_k', x_{-k})\,\pi(x_k \mid x_{-k})}{\pi(x_k, x_{-k})\,\pi(x_k' \mid x_{-k})} = \frac{\pi(x_k'\mid x_{-k})\pi(x_{-i})\,\pi(x_k\mid x_{-i})}{\pi(x_k\mid x_{-i})\pi(x_{-i})\,\pi(x_k'\mid x_{-i})} = 1,\] using \(\pi(x_k, x_{-k}) = \pi(x_k \mid x_{-k})\pi(x_{-k})\). Hence \(\alpha(x \to x') = \min\{1, 1\} = 1\) for every Gibbs step.

Block Gibbs. Suppose \((\theta_1, \theta_2)\) have posterior correlation \(\rho\) close to \(1\). Sketch (no calculator) why coordinate-wise Gibbs mixes slowly and why updating \((\theta_1, \theta_2)\) jointly fixes the problem.

Solution. When \(\rho \approx 1\) the posterior concentrates on a narrow diagonal ridge. Each axis-aligned Gibbs step has conditional variance \(1-\rho^2 \ll 1\), so the chain inches along the ridge by small horizontal/vertical moves and the effective step along the ridge is \(O(\sqrt{1-\rho^2})\). The block update \[(\theta_1, \theta_2) \sim p(\theta_1, \theta_2 \mid \text{rest}, D)\] draws from the joint conditional, which has full variance along the ridge direction. One block step accomplishes what hundreds of coordinate-wise steps would require, eliminating the dependence on \(\rho\).

Reversibility from detailed balance. Verify that if a Markov chain satisfies \(\pi(x) T(x, y) = \pi(y) T(y, x)\) for all states \(x, y\), then \(\pi\) is stationary. Is the converse true?

Solution. Summing detailed balance over \(x\): \[\sum_x \pi(x) T(x,y) = \sum_x \pi(y) T(y,x) = \pi(y) \sum_x T(y,x) = \pi(y),\] so \((\pi T)(y) = \pi(y)\) and \(\pi\) is stationary. The converse is false: the deterministic cyclic chain \(0\to 1 \to 2 \to 0\) has \(\pi = (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)\) stationary but violates detailed balance.

Trivial chain. A Markov chain has transition kernel \(T(x, y) = \pi(y)\) for every \(x\) (i.e. each step is an independent draw from \(\pi\)). Verify detailed balance and compute the integrated autocorrelation time of any well-behaved test function.

Solution. Detailed balance: \(\pi(x)T(x,y) = \pi(x)\pi(y) = \pi(y)\pi(x) = \pi(y)T(y,x)\). Successive states are i.i.d. from \(\pi\), so for any test function \(f\) with finite variance the lag-\(k\) autocorrelation is \(\rho_k = 0\) for all \(k \geq 1\), and \[\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \tfrac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k = \tfrac{1}{2}.\] This is the minimum possible value — i.i.d. sampling is the gold standard.

(Define and explain.) Define the independence sampler. Write its acceptance probability in terms of the importance weights \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\). Explain its connection to importance sampling.

Solution. The independence sampler is a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm whose proposal \(q(x' \mid x) = q(x')\) does not depend on the current state. The MH ratio collapses to \[\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\,\frac{\pi(x')\,q(x)}{\pi(x)\,q(x')}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\,\frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\},\] the ratio of importance weights \(w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)\). It is the MCMC analogue of importance sampling: \(q\) plays the role of a global proposal that should approximate \(\pi\), and the acceptance step (rather than weighting) corrects the mismatch. Ergodicity requires the envelope bound \(w(x) \leq C < \infty\).

(Define and explain.) Define the Gibbs sampler. Show that every Gibbs step is accepted, i.e. the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance ratio equals \(1\) when the proposal is the full conditional.

Solution. The Gibbs sampler updates parameters \(\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)\) cyclically by sampling each coordinate from its full conditional \[\theta_i^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D).\] Viewed as MH with proposal \(q(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}) = p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D)\), the acceptance ratio factorises via \(p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i}) = p(\theta_i\mid\theta_{-i})p(\theta_{-i})\): \[\frac{p(\theta_i',\theta_{-i})\,p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i})}{p(\theta_i,\theta_{-i})\,p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i})} = \frac{p(\theta_i'\mid\theta_{-i})p(\theta_i\mid\theta_{-i})}{p(\theta_i\mid\theta_{-i})p(\theta_i'\mid\theta_{-i})} = 1.\] Hence \(\alpha = 1\) — every Gibbs proposal is accepted.

(Compare and contrast.) For a target with strongly correlated coordinates, compare the mixing behaviour of coordinate-wise Gibbs, block Gibbs, and random-walk Metropolis. State which one you would prefer in this situation and why.

Solution.

  • Coordinate-wise Gibbs: always accepts but takes \(O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{1-\rho^2}\right)\) steps to cross the posterior ridge — slow when \(|\rho| \to 1\).
  • Random-walk MH with proposal covariance \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{prop}}\): also slow unless \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{prop}}\) is tuned to match the elongated posterior covariance \(\Sigma\); an isotropic proposal wastes effort across the ridge.
  • Block Gibbs: samples \((\theta_1,\theta_2)\) jointly from the exact joint conditional, removing the dependence on \(\rho\) entirely.

Preferred: block Gibbs, because it eliminates the bottleneck at its source. If the joint conditional is intractable, the next best option is random-walk MH with a preconditioned proposal \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{prop}} \propto \Sigma\) (or HMC).

(Define and explain.) Define integrated autocorrelation time. Explain in one sentence why grouping (blocking) strongly correlated parameters in a Gibbs sampler lowers it.

Solution. For a stationary chain and test function \(f\) with lag-\(k\) autocorrelation \(\rho_k\), \[\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \tfrac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k,\] so that the effective sample size is \(N_{\mathrm{eff}} = N/(2\tau_{\mathrm{int}})\). Blocking strongly correlated coordinates lets one update move along the posterior ridge in a single step (instead of many small axis-aligned moves), which collapses the slow tail of \(\rho_k\) and therefore reduces \(\tau_{\mathrm{int}}\).

## Introduction

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, introduced in the previous chapter, is a general framework for constructing Markov chains with a target stationary distribution. However, the choice of proposal distribution critically affects both computational efficiency and the quality of samples. In this chapter, we explore two important special cases that reveal a fundamental trade-off in MCMC design: global vs. local exploration, and the role of acceptance rates in controlling mixing.

The **independence sampler** uses a fixed global proposal, independent of the current state. This allows long-distance jumps but requires the proposal to approximate the target well. The **Gibbs sampler**, by contrast, updates coordinates sequentially using exact full conditional distributions, guaranteeing acceptance but potentially moving slowly through high-dimensional spaces when parameters are correlated. Both methods illustrate how MCMC designers must balance the competing demands of efficient exploration and computational tractability.

## The independence sampler: global proposals

In a random-walk Metropolis algorithm, the proposal distribution depends on the current state:
$$
q(x' \mid x) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{\|x' - x\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right).
$$

This locality allows the chain to take small, tentative steps and gradually explore the target space. However, if the proposal variance is too small, the chain mixes slowly; if too large, it wastes computation on distant, low-probability regions.

The **independence sampler** takes a different approach: the proposal is **independent of the current state**,
$$
q(x') = q(x' \mid x),
$$
depending only on $x'$. This allows the chain to jump from anywhere to anywhere in a single step, potentially escaping local modes and exploring the target space more globally.

- **Acceptance probability.** For a generic Metropolis–Hastings step the acceptance probability is
$$
\alpha(x \to x') = \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(x') \, q(x \mid x')}{\pi(x) \, q(x' \mid x)}\right).
$$
For the independence sampler, $q(x \mid x') = q(x)$ and $q(x' \mid x) = q(x')$, so
$$
\alpha(x \to x') = \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(x') \, q(x)}{\pi(x) \, q(x')}\right).
$$
Rearranging in terms of **importance weights** $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$:
$$
\alpha(x \to x') = \min\left(1, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right).
$$

::::: {.note}
The acceptance probability compares the importance weights at the proposed and current states. If $w(x')$ is larger, the proposal is always accepted. If $w(x')$ is smaller, it is accepted with probability $w(x') / w(x)$. When the proposal closely matches the target, weights are nearly constant and acceptance is high.
:::::

- **Connection to importance sampling and ESS.** The independence sampler inherits its theoretical motivation from **importance sampling**. In importance sampling, we approximate an expectation
$$
E_\pi[f(x)] = \int f(x) \, \pi(x) \, dx
$$
by sampling from a simpler proposal $q$ and weighting:
$$
\widehat{E_\pi}[f(x)] = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N w(x_i) f(x_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^N w(x_i)}, \quad w(x_i) = \frac{\pi(x_i)}{q(x_i)}.
$$
The **effective sample size (ESS)** reflects how much information the weighted samples contain. If weights are unequal—as they are when $q$ deviates from $\pi$—the ESS is smaller than $N$:
$$
\text{ESS} = \frac{1}{\sum_i (w_i / \bar{w})^2}.
$$
When $w(x)$ varies widely, many samples receive vanishingly small weight, and the variance of the estimator becomes very large. The independence sampler's acceptance rate is directly related to the relative variation in weights: a narrow or poorly-matched proposal produces highly variable weights, leading to low acceptance and slow exploration. This is the **narrow-proposal failure mode** — if the proposal is too concentrated, the chain accepts rarely and gets stuck.

- **When it works well.** The key requirement is that **$q$ must approximate $\pi$ globally**. A sufficient ergodicity condition is
$$
\pi(x) \leq C \, q(x) \quad \forall x
$$
for some finite constant $C > 0$ (analogous to the envelope condition in rejection sampling), which ensures the chain does not get trapped in regions of arbitrarily high relative weight. The independence sampler is best when we have a good global approximation to the posterior — e.g., from a **Laplace approximation**, **variational inference**, or other fast approximate methods — and when the target is unimodal or has well-separated modes the proposal can accommodate. It fails when the proposal underestimates the tails or misses modes, so that states with large $\pi(x)/q(x)$ are rare and hard to reach.

- **Curse of dimensionality.** A profound limitation emerges in high dimensions. Suppose $\log w(x) = \log \pi(x) - \log q(x)$ decomposes as a sum of roughly independent contributions:
$$
\log w(x) \approx \sum_{i=1}^d \Delta_i(x_i),
$$
where each $\Delta_i$ represents the local mismatch between $\pi$ and $q$ in coordinate $i$. Even if each $\Delta_i$ is small (order $O(1)$), the total deviation is $\log w(x) = O(d)$, so $w(x) = \exp(O(d))$. The weights vary **exponentially** in dimension, and the acceptance rate typically decays like $P(A) \sim \exp(-cd)$.

::::: {.definition}
**Concentration of measure**: In high dimensions, probability mass concentrates on thin regions. For a standard Gaussian $\pi = N(0, I_d)$, the norm $\|x\|$ concentrates around $\sqrt{d}$. Even a slightly mis-scaled proposal $q$ will place its probability mass in regions irrelevant to $\pi$, causing acceptance to decay exponentially with $d$.
:::::

This is the motivation for **local proposals** (random-walk Metropolis), **gradient-based methods** (Hamiltonian MC), and **coordinate-wise samplers** (Gibbs sampling), which we turn to next.

## The Gibbs sampler: coordinate-wise updates

In many Bayesian models, jointly sampling from $p(\theta \mid D)$ where $\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)$ is intractable, but sampling from each **full conditional distribution** is simple.

::::: {.definition}
The **full conditional distribution** of $\theta_i$ is
$$
p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D),
$$
where $\theta_{-i} = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_{i-1}, \theta_{i+1}, \ldots, \theta_d)$ denotes all other parameters. It is obtained by treating $\theta_{-i}$ as fixed and proportional to the joint posterior:
$$
p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \propto p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i} \mid D).
$$
:::::

### Algorithm: deterministic and random scan

**Gibbs sampling** iteratively samples each parameter from its full conditional.

::::: {.theorem}
**Gibbs Sampler Algorithm**

Initialize $\theta^{(0)} = (\theta_1^{(0)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(0)})$.

For $t = 1, 2, \ldots$:

1. $\theta_1^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t-1)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(t-1)}, D)$
2. $\theta_2^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t)}, \theta_3^{(t-1)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(t-1)}, D)$
3. $\vdots$
4. $\theta_d^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_d \mid \theta_1^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_{d-1}^{(t)}, D)$

One complete cycle of all $d$ updates constitutes one iteration of the Gibbs sampler.

**Variants**: The **random-scan Gibbs sampler** updates coordinates in random order instead of a fixed sequence. For convergence, both deterministic and random scans are ergodic under mild conditions.
:::::

- **Gibbs as a special case of Metropolis–Hastings.** Gibbs sampling can be viewed as Metropolis–Hastings with the proposal being the full conditional itself. At step $t$, we propose a new value for $\theta_i$ by sampling from
$$
q(\theta_i' \mid \theta_i^{(t-1)}, \theta_{-i}^{(t)}) = p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D).
$$
The acceptance probability is
$$
\alpha(\theta^{(t-1)} \to \theta^{(t)}) = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta_i^{(t)}, \theta_{-i}^{(t)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t-1)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)}{p(\theta_i^{(t-1)}, \theta_{-i}^{(t-1)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)}\right).
$$
Using the factorization $p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i} \mid D) = p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i} \mid D)$, the ratio simplifies:
$$
\frac{p(\theta_i^{(t)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i}^{(t)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t-1)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)}{p(\theta_i^{(t-1)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t-1)}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i}^{(t-1)} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i^{(t)} \mid \theta_{-i}^{(t)}, D)} = 1.
$$
Therefore, $\alpha = 1$: **every Gibbs step is accepted**.

::::: {.note}
Acceptance is guaranteed, but samples are still **correlated**. The chain does not jump to a new state at each step; instead, it updates coordinates sequentially, and autocorrelation arises because coordinates are strongly dependent.
:::::

### Detailed balance for Gibbs updates

For the full Gibbs update (one complete sweep of all $d$ coordinates), detailed balance holds with respect to the joint posterior. We verify that
$$
p(\theta \mid D) \, T(\theta \to \theta') = p(\theta' \mid D) \, T(\theta' \to \theta),
$$
where $T$ is the transition kernel of one complete sweep.

For a single coordinate update $T_i$ applied to coordinate $i$, left-multiply by $p(\theta \mid D)$ and expand using the factorization:
\begin{align}
p(\theta \mid D) \, T_i(\theta \to \theta') &= p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, \delta(\theta_{-i}' - \theta_{-i})\\
&= p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, \delta(\theta_{-i}' - \theta_{-i}).
\end{align}

Permuting terms and using $p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) = p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}', D)$ (since $\theta_{-i} = \theta_{-i}'$):
$$
= p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, p(\theta_{-i} \mid D) \, p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, \delta(\theta_{-i}' - \theta_{-i}) = p(\theta' \mid D) \, T_i(\theta' \to \theta).
$$

Thus each coordinate-wise update preserves the posterior. Since the full sweep is a composition of such updates, it also satisfies detailed balance.

- **Mixing and slow mixing from correlation.** Unlike the independence sampler, Gibbs always accepts. However, this does not imply fast mixing. When parameters are strongly correlated — as in high-dimensional hierarchical models or when the posterior is elongated along a narrow ridge — coordinate-wise updates must take many small steps to explore the space.

::::: {.example}
**Bivariate Gaussian with high correlation**

Consider a bivariate normal distribution:
$$
\begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix} \sim N\left( \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix} \right).
$$

The full conditionals are:
$$
X \mid Y = y \sim N(\rho y, 1 - \rho^2), \quad Y \mid X = x \sim N(\rho x, 1 - \rho^2).
$$

When $\rho = 0.9$, the posterior concentrates on a narrow diagonal ridge. A Gibbs step first moves horizontally (X-update), then vertically (Y-update), taking many iterations to traverse the ridge. In contrast, updating both coordinates jointly (e.g., via a rotated-coordinate Gibbs or HMC) moves along the ridge in fewer steps.
:::::

- **Metropolis-within-Gibbs and block Gibbs.** When the full conditional for a component is intractable, we can replace the Gibbs step with a Metropolis–Hastings step; when several coordinates are strongly correlated, we can group them and update them jointly.

::::: {.definition}
**Metropolis-within-Gibbs**: Replace the Gibbs step for $\theta_i$ with a Metropolis–Hastings move using a local proposal $q_i(\theta_i' \mid \theta_i)$. The acceptance probability is
$$
\alpha_i(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}) = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, q_i(\theta_i \mid \theta_i')}{p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D) \, q_i(\theta_i' \mid \theta_i)}\right).
$$

The chain remains valid (it still has $p(\theta \mid D)$ as stationary distribution), but some steps are rejected.
:::::

**Block Gibbs sampling** updates multiple coordinates jointly,
$$
(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_k) \sim p(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_k \mid \theta_{k+1}, \ldots, \theta_d, D),
$$
which is effective when joint conditionals are tractable and dramatically reduces autocorrelation in highly correlated subsets.

## Worked example: Gibbs sampling for a bivariate Gaussian

We now demonstrate Gibbs sampling on the bivariate Gaussian with $\rho = 0.9$ to illustrate slow mixing due to correlation, and show how a simple coordinate rotation accelerates mixing.

### Slow mixing at high correlation

```{r}
#| label: lst-gibbs-bivariate-high-corr
#| lst-cap: "Gibbs bivariate high corr"
#| fig-cap: "Gibbs sampling trace plots for a bivariate Gaussian with ρ = 0.9. The X-trace (top) shows high autocorrelation; the Y-trace (middle) is even slower due to dependence on X. The scatter plot (bottom) reveals the narrow diagonal structure."

set.seed(42)

gibbs_bivariate <- function(n_steps, rho, start = c(0, 0)) {
  x <- numeric(n_steps)
  y <- numeric(n_steps)
  x[1] <- start[1]
  y[1] <- start[2]
  cond_sd <- sqrt(1 - rho^2)

  for (t in 2:n_steps) {
    # Update X | Y
    x[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = rho * y[t - 1], sd = cond_sd)
    # Update Y | X
    y[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = rho * x[t], sd = cond_sd)
  }

  cbind(x = x, y = y)
}

rho_high <- 0.9
chain_high <- gibbs_bivariate(n_steps = 10000, rho = rho_high, start = c(-3, 3))

# Diagnostics

samples_high <- chain_high[(burn_in + 1):nrow(chain_high), ]

par(mfrow = c(3, 1), mar = c(4, 4, 3, 1))

# Trace plots
plot(chain_high[, "x"], type = "l", lwd = 0.8, col = "#d62728",
     main = paste0("Gibbs trace (X), rho = ", rho_high),
     xlab = "Iteration", ylab = "X")
abline(v = burn_in, col = "grey", lty = 2)

plot(chain_high[, "y"], type = "l", lwd = 0.8, col = "#1f77b4",
     main = "Gibbs trace (Y)", xlab = "Iteration", ylab = "Y")
abline(v = burn_in, col = "grey", lty = 2)

# Scatter of posterior samples
plot(samples_high[, "x"], samples_high[, "y"], pch = 16, cex = 0.3,
     col = rgb(0, 0, 0, 0.5), xlab = "X", ylab = "Y",
     main = "Posterior samples after burn-in")
```

### Rotated coordinates for faster mixing

The slow mixing arises because the Gibbs updates align with the coordinate axes, while the posterior concentrates along the diagonal. We can decouple the correlation by rotating coordinates. Let $u = \frac{x + y}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $v = \frac{x - y}{\sqrt{2}}$. For a standard bivariate Gaussian, the joint distribution of $(u, v)$ is:
$$
u \sim N(0, (1 + \rho)), \quad v \sim N(0, (1 - \rho)),
$$
and $u$ and $v$ are **independent**.

```{r}
#| label: lst-gibbs-rotated
#| lst-cap: "Gibbs rotated"
#| fig-cap: "Gibbs sampling in rotated coordinates. With ρ = 0.9, rotated coordinates decouple the posterior into independent components, yielding much lower autocorrelation and faster mixing."

gibbs_rotated <- function(n_steps, rho, start = c(0, 0)) {
  # Transform starting point to rotated coordinates
  u_start <- (start[1] + start[2]) / sqrt(2)
  v_start <- (start[1] - start[2]) / sqrt(2)

  u <- numeric(n_steps)
  v <- numeric(n_steps)
  u[1] <- u_start
  v[1] <- v_start

  sd_u <- sqrt(1 + rho)
  sd_v <- sqrt(1 - rho)

  for (t in 2:n_steps) {
    # u and v are now independent, so Gibbs reduces to independent sampling
    u[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = 0, sd = sd_u)
    v[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = 0, sd = sd_v)
  }

  # Transform back to original coordinates
  x <- (u + v) / sqrt(2)
  y <- (u - v) / sqrt(2)

  cbind(x = x, y = y)
}

chain_rotated <- gibbs_rotated(n_steps = 10000, rho = rho_high, start = c(-3, 3))
samples_rotated <- chain_rotated[(burn_in + 1):nrow(chain_rotated), ]

par(mfrow = c(3, 1), mar = c(4, 4, 3, 1))

# Trace plots for rotated coordinates
plot(chain_rotated[, "x"], type = "l", lwd = 0.8, col = "#2ca02c",
     main = "Rotated Gibbs trace (X after rotation)",
     xlab = "Iteration", ylab = "X")
abline(v = burn_in, col = "grey", lty = 2)

plot(chain_rotated[, "y"], type = "l", lwd = 0.8, col = "#ff7f0e",
     main = "Rotated Gibbs trace (Y after rotation)",
     xlab = "Iteration", ylab = "Y")
abline(v = burn_in, col = "grey", lty = 2)

# Scatter plot
plot(samples_rotated[, "x"], samples_rotated[, "y"], pch = 16, cex = 0.3,
     col = rgb(0, 0, 0, 0.5), xlab = "X", ylab = "Y",
     main = "Posterior samples (rotated Gibbs)")

par(mfrow = c(1, 1))
```

## Comparison: independence sampler vs. Gibbs sampler vs. random-walk Metropolis

The three samplers embody different philosophies and trade-offs:

| **Aspect** | **Independence Sampler** | **Gibbs Sampler** | **Random-Walk MH** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Proposal** | $q(x')$ (global, fixed) | $p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D)$ | $x' \sim N(x, \Sigma)$ |
| **Move type** | Global jumps anywhere | Coordinate-wise updates | Local steps |
| **Acceptance** | MH ratio (often low) | Always 1 (deterministic) | Variable (tunable) |
| **Main requirement** | Good global approximation | Tractable conditionals | Reasonable scale estimate |
| **Fails when** | $q$ poorly matches $\pi$ | Strong parameter correlations | Proposal scale wrong |
| **Autocorrelation** | Low if $q \approx \pi$ | High if correlated structure | Moderate (tunable) |
| **Dimension scaling** | Exponential decay $\sim \exp(-cd)$ | Polynomial (depends on correlation) | Exponential decay |
| **Typical use** | Approximate posteriors | Conjugate/hierarchical models | Flexible fallback |

::::: {.remark}
In practice, these are rarely used in isolation. Modern samplers often combine ideas:

- **Metropolis-within-Gibbs**: Use Gibbs for tractable coordinates, MH for intractable ones.
- **Block Gibbs**: Group correlated coordinates and sample jointly to reduce autocorrelation.
- **Adaptive proposals**: Learn a proposal from preliminary runs (adaptive MCMC).
- **HMC within Gibbs**: Combine gradient-based efficient exploration with block structure.

The choice depends on the model structure, the availability of efficient samplers for conditionals, and computational constraints.
:::::

## Summary

The **independence sampler** represents a global exploration strategy: it can jump anywhere but only works well if the proposal closely approximates the target. Its acceptance probability directly reflects the importance weights, making it a bridge between importance sampling and MCMC. However, the exponential degradation of weights in high dimensions—a manifestation of concentration of measure—limits its applicability to high-dimensional problems.

The **Gibbs sampler** exploits model structure by updating parameters from their full conditionals. Every step is accepted, eliminating the rejection waste of Metropolis algorithms. However, when parameters are strongly correlated, the chain mixes slowly because coordinate-wise moves struggle to explore elongated posterior geometries efficiently. Rotation to decorrelated coordinates, block updates, or auxiliary variable methods can dramatically improve performance.

Both methods highlight the central tension in MCMC: acceptance rates, exploration speed, and computational tractability are not independent. A low acceptance rate signals a poorly matched proposal; a high rate (even 100%) does not guarantee fast mixing if the effective step size is small. Successful MCMC practice requires diagnosing mixing, adapting proposals, and sometimes redesigning the parameterization to align with the posterior structure.

*(Written test, 15 June 2026 — Q6.)* A discrete-time Markov chain has state space $\{0, 1, 2\}$ and transition matrix
$$T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - a - b & a & b \\ b & 1 - a - b & a \\ a & b & 1 - a - b \end{pmatrix},$$
where $a > 0$, $b > 0$, and $a + b \leq 1$.

a. Find the stationary distribution $\pi = (\pi_0, \pi_1, \pi_2)$.
b. State the detailed-balance equations for this chain.
c. Derive the condition on $a$ and $b$ under which detailed balance can hold.
:::

::: {.solution}
We are asked to find the stationary vector of a cyclic $3 \times 3$ transition matrix, write out its detailed-balance equations, and identify when detailed balance can hold.

**(a) Stationary distribution.** A stationary distribution satisfies $\pi T = \pi$ with $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$. Inspecting $T$ we see that every *column* also sums to $1$: column $j$ contains one occurrence of each of $\{1-a-b,\,a,\,b\}$, whose sum is $1$. A matrix whose rows *and* columns both sum to $1$ is **doubly stochastic**, and for any doubly stochastic $T$ on $n$ states the uniform vector is invariant, because
$$(\pi T)_j = \sum_i \tfrac{1}{n} T(i,j) = \tfrac{1}{n} \sum_i T(i,j) = \tfrac{1}{n}.$$
Hence $\pi = (\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$.

**(b) Detailed-balance equations.** Detailed balance (the reversibility condition invoked throughout the chapter, e.g. in *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates*) is $\pi_i T(i,j) = \pi_j T(j,i)$ for every pair $i \neq j$. With $\pi_i = 1/3$ this simplifies to the symmetry conditions
$$T(0,1)=T(1,0),\quad T(1,2)=T(2,1),\quad T(0,2)=T(2,0).$$

**(c) Condition on $a,b$.** Reading off entries: $T(0,1)=a$ and $T(1,0)=b$; $T(1,2)=a$ and $T(2,1)=b$; $T(0,2)=b$ and $T(2,0)=a$. All three equations reduce to $a=b$. Thus detailed balance holds **iff** $a=b$. When $a\neq b$ there is a net cyclic probability flux $a-b$ around $0\to 1\to 2\to 0$: the chain is stationary but *not* reversible — exactly the pathology anticipated by the exercise on cyclic chains below.

**Cross-reference:** *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* (reversibility definition $\pi(x)T(x\to y) = \pi(y)T(y\to x)$) and the doubly-stochastic argument for uniform stationarity.
:::

::: {.exercise}

Two-state Markov chain with $T(0 \to 1) = \alpha$ and $T(1 \to 0) = \beta$, where $\alpha, \beta \in (0, 1)$. Find the stationary distribution $\pi$ in closed form and verify detailed balance.
:::

::: {.solution}
We are asked to find the stationary distribution of a two-state Markov chain with off-diagonal transition probabilities $\alpha,\beta$ and to check detailed balance.

**Set-up.** The full transition matrix is
$$T = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\alpha & \alpha \\ \beta & 1-\beta \end{pmatrix}.$$
A stationary distribution $\pi = (\pi_0, \pi_1)$ satisfies $\pi T = \pi$, i.e.
$$\pi_0(1-\alpha) + \pi_1 \beta = \pi_0, \qquad \pi_0 \alpha + \pi_1 (1-\beta) = \pi_1.$$
Both equations reduce to the single balance condition
$$\pi_0 \alpha = \pi_1 \beta. \tag{$\ast$}$$
Combining with the normalisation $\pi_0 + \pi_1 = 1$ we solve
$$\pi_0 = \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}, \qquad \pi_1 = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}.$$

**Detailed balance.** The reversibility condition from *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* is $\pi_i T(i \to j) = \pi_j T(j \to i)$ for all $i \neq j$. Here there is a single off-diagonal pair to check:
$$\pi_0 T(0\to 1) = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot \alpha = \frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot\beta = \pi_1 T(1\to 0). \checkmark$$
Equation $(\ast)$ is *identical* to detailed balance, so the two-state chain is always reversible whenever $\alpha, \beta \in (0,1)$ — this is a special feature of $n=2$: there is only one pair of states and hence no room for a cyclic flux like the one flagged in the note following $§$ Detailed balance.

**Cross-reference:** *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* (reversibility $\pi(x)T(x\to y) = \pi(y)T(y\to x)$).
:::

::: {.exercise}

Three-state birth-death chain on $\{0, 1, 2\}$ with $T(i \to i+1) = p$ and $T(i \to i-1) = q$ (where defined), self-loops chosen so each row sums to one. Find the stationary distribution in terms of the ratio $r = p/q$.
:::

::: {.solution}
We must find the stationary distribution of a three-state birth-death chain in which each interior state can move up with probability $p$ or down with probability $q$ (with self-loops making the rows sum to $1$), expressed via $r = p/q$.

**Why we may use detailed balance directly.** In a birth-death chain the only non-self-loop transitions connect neighbouring states $i$ and $i \pm 1$. The graph of allowed transitions is a *tree* (a path), so it contains no cycles, and any stationary distribution of such a chain is automatically reversible — because the pairwise fluxes across each cut of the path must balance separately. This lets us apply the detailed-balance recursion from *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* rather than solving the full linear system $\pi T = \pi$.

**Recursion.** For each pair $(i,i+1)$ the detailed-balance equation $\pi_i T(i \to i+1) = \pi_{i+1} T(i+1 \to i)$ becomes
$$\pi_i \, p = \pi_{i+1} \, q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_{i+1} = \frac{p}{q}\, \pi_i = r\,\pi_i.$$
Iterating from $i=0$:
$$\pi_1 = r\,\pi_0, \qquad \pi_2 = r\,\pi_1 = r^2\,\pi_0.$$

**Normalisation.** Requiring $\pi_0 + \pi_1 + \pi_2 = 1$ gives $\pi_0(1 + r + r^2) = 1$, so
$$\pi = \frac{1}{1 + r + r^2}\,(1,\, r,\, r^2).$$
When $r < 1$ the distribution is peaked at $0$; when $r > 1$ it is peaked at $2$; when $r = 1$ we recover the uniform stationary distribution.

**Cross-reference:** *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* (reversibility for nearest-neighbour chains on a path graph).
:::

::: {.exercise}

Construct a 3-state Markov chain that admits a stationary distribution but does *not* satisfy detailed balance. (Hint: try a deterministic cycle $0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0$.) Verify both claims explicitly.
:::

::: {.solution}
We must exhibit a 3-state Markov chain that has a stationary distribution but violates detailed balance, then verify each claim explicitly.

**Construction.** Take the deterministic cyclic shift
$$T(i, i+1 \bmod 3) = 1, \qquad T(i,j) = 0 \text{ otherwise},$$
so
$$T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Stationarity.** The rows sum to $1$ (stochasticity) and the columns also sum to $1$: each column has exactly one entry equal to $1$. Hence $T$ is doubly stochastic, and by the same doubly-stochastic argument used elsewhere in this chapter the uniform distribution is invariant:
$$(\pi T)_j = \sum_i \tfrac{1}{3} T(i,j) = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 1 = \pi_j.$$

**Failure of detailed balance.** The condition $\pi_i T(i,j) = \pi_j T(j,i)$ from *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* requires the pairwise fluxes to match. But
$$\pi_0 T(0,1) = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{3}, \qquad \pi_1 T(1,0) = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 0 = 0,$$
so the flux $0 \to 1$ is $1/3$ whereas the reverse flux $1 \to 0$ is $0$. There is a strictly positive net probability current $\tfrac{1}{3}$ around the cycle $0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0$.

**Interpretation.** The chain is stationary because the *net* current into every state equals the net current out — the incoming flux from the cyclic predecessor $i-1$ exactly balances the outgoing flux to the successor $i+1$. But this global "circulation" balance is weaker than the pairwise detailed-balance condition; the chain is not time-reversible. This is precisely the counter-example anticipated by the *note* following the derivation of detailed balance in the chapter body.

**Cross-reference:** *§ Detailed balance for Gibbs updates* (definition of reversibility and the distinction from mere stationarity).

::: {.exercise}

Independence sampler with target $\pi(x) = \text{Beta}(2, 2)$ on $(0,1)$ and proposal $q(x) = \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. Write the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability $\alpha(x \to x')$ in terms of $x$, $x'$, and identify the smallest constant $M$ that would make every proposal accepted in a *rejection* sampler.
:::

::: {.solution}
The exercise asks for the independence-sampler acceptance probability when the target is $\pi = \mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ and the proposal is $q = \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$, and the smallest envelope constant that would make rejection sampling accept every proposal.

**Densities.** The Beta$(\alpha,\beta)$ density with $\alpha=\beta=2$ is
$$\pi(x) = \frac{\Gamma(4)}{\Gamma(2)^2}\, x^{2-1}(1-x)^{2-1} = 6\,x(1-x), \qquad x \in (0,1),$$
and $q(x) = 1$ on $(0,1)$.

**Importance weight.** Directly from the definition $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$ introduced in *§ The independence sampler: global proposals* we get
$$w(x) = 6\,x(1-x).$$

**Acceptance probability.** By the independence-sampler acceptance formula derived in that same section,
$$\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{6\,x'(1-x')}{6\,x(1-x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{x'(1-x')}{x(1-x)}\right\}.$$
The constant $6$ cancels — as it must for any independence sampler, since acceptance depends only on ratios of unnormalised weights.

**Smallest envelope constant.** For rejection sampling with envelope $Mq(x) \geq \pi(x)$ everywhere, the smallest admissible $M$ is
$$M = \sup_{x \in (0,1)} \frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)} = \sup_{x \in (0,1)} 6\,x(1-x).$$
The function $x(1-x)$ is maximised at $x = 1/2$ (set derivative $1-2x = 0$) with value $1/4$, so
$$M = 6 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{2}.$$
Equivalently, the envelope condition $\pi \leq C q$ from *§ The independence sampler* holds with $C = 3/2 < \infty$, so this sampler is uniformly ergodic.

**Cross-reference:** *§ The independence sampler: global proposals* (acceptance formula $\alpha = \min\{1, w(x')/w(x)\}$ and envelope condition $\pi \leq Cq$).
:::

::: {.exercise}

Independence sampler with target $\pi = \text{Exp}(1)$ and proposal $q = \text{Exp}(2)$. Compute the importance weight $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$ and the acceptance ratio $\alpha(x \to x') = \min\{1, w(x')/w(x)\}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
We must compute the importance weight and acceptance ratio for an independence sampler with target $\pi = \mathrm{Exp}(1)$ and proposal $q = \mathrm{Exp}(2)$.

**Densities.** The rate-$\lambda$ exponential has density $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ on $[0,\infty)$. Hence
$$\pi(x) = e^{-x}, \qquad q(x) = 2\,e^{-2x}, \qquad x \geq 0.$$

**Importance weight.** By the definition $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$ from *§ The independence sampler: global proposals*,
$$w(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{2\,e^{-2x}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-x + 2x} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{x}.$$

**Acceptance ratio.** By the independence-sampler acceptance formula in the same section,
$$\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x'}}{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x}}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, e^{x' - x}\right\}.$$
Proposals to larger states are always accepted, whereas proposals to smaller states are accepted with probability $e^{x'-x}$.

**Pathology: unbounded weights.** Because the proposal has rate $2 > 1$ (heavier decay than the target), its tail is *lighter* than $\pi$'s. Formally $w(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}e^{x} \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$, so no finite envelope $C$ makes $\pi \leq Cq$ hold on all of $[0,\infty)$. The envelope condition of *§ The independence sampler* is *violated*, and the chain is not uniformly ergodic: whenever it wanders into a large $x$ its acceptance probability for the next proposal $x' \sim q$ (which typically has small $x'$) is $e^{x'-x} \ll 1$, and the chain stalls — exactly the *narrow-proposal / light-tail failure mode* discussed in the chapter.

**Cross-reference:** *§ The independence sampler: global proposals* (importance-weight definition, acceptance formula, envelope condition, and the "narrow-proposal failure mode" paragraph).
:::

::: {.exercise}

Show that an independence sampler with proposal $q = \pi$ accepts every move and therefore generates i.i.d.\ samples from $\pi$.
:::

::: {.solution}
We must show that an independence sampler whose proposal $q$ equals the target $\pi$ accepts every move and therefore generates i.i.d.\ samples from $\pi$.

**Importance weight.** From the definition $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$ in *§ The independence sampler: global proposals*, setting $q = \pi$ gives
$$w(x) = \frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)} = 1$$
for every $x$ in the support.

**Acceptance probability.** By the independence-sampler acceptance formula
$$\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{1}{1}\right\} = 1,$$
for every pair $(x, x')$. Every proposal is accepted deterministically.

**I.i.d. samples.** The transition kernel of an independence sampler is
$$T(x \to x') = q(x')\,\alpha(x \to x') + \delta(x'-x)\bigl(1 - \bar\alpha(x)\bigr),$$
where $\bar\alpha(x) = \int q(y)\alpha(x\to y)\,dy$. With $\alpha \equiv 1$ we get $\bar\alpha(x) = \int q(y)\,dy = 1$, so the delta-rejection piece vanishes and
$$T(x \to x') = q(x') = \pi(x').$$
The next state is drawn from $\pi$ regardless of the current state — that is exactly the statement that the successive draws are *independent* and identically distributed as $\pi$. Combined with the envelope bound $w \leq 1 < \infty$ (the strongest possible case of the condition in *§ The independence sampler*), this shows why $q = \pi$ is the ideal — indeed unattainable — target for independence-sampler design.

**Cross-reference:** *§ The independence sampler: global proposals* (weight definition, acceptance formula, envelope condition $\pi \leq Cq$).
:::

::: {.exercise}

Explain qualitatively why an independence sampler whose proposal $q$ has lighter tails than the target $\pi$ tends to get stuck in the tails of $\pi$. Express your answer in terms of the importance weight $w(x)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
We are asked to explain qualitatively, in terms of the importance weight $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$, why an independence sampler whose proposal $q$ has lighter tails than the target $\pi$ tends to get stuck in the tails of $\pi$.

**Weight blows up in the tail.** "Light tails for $q$" means $q(x)$ decays faster than $\pi(x)$ as $|x|\to\infty$. Therefore the importance weight introduced in *§ The independence sampler: global proposals*,
$$w(x) = \frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)},$$
diverges as $|x| \to \infty$. The envelope condition $\pi \leq C\,q$ from the same section cannot hold with any finite $C$, so the sampler is *not* uniformly ergodic.

**Consequence for acceptance.** Suppose by some rare fluctuation the chain reaches a tail state $x$ with $w(x)$ very large. A fresh proposal $x' \sim q$ typically comes from the bulk of $q$ (which is close to the mode of $\pi$), so $w(x') = O(1)$, much smaller than $w(x)$. By the independence-sampler acceptance formula from that section,
$$\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\, \frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\} \approx \frac{w(x')}{w(x)} \ll 1.$$
Almost every proposal is *rejected*, and the chain remains at $x$ for many steps.

**Sticking pathology.** The expected holding time at $x$ is roughly $1/\bar\alpha(x)$, where $\bar\alpha(x) = \int q(y)\alpha(x\to y)\,dy \approx E_q[w]/w(x) \to 0$ as $w(x) \to \infty$. This is precisely the *narrow-proposal failure mode* diagnosed in the chapter body: because $w$ is unbounded, the chain "sticks" in the tail, producing extreme autocorrelation and biased Monte Carlo estimates. It is the MCMC analogue of the pathology in importance sampling where a single sample with huge $w$ dominates the sum $\sum_i w(x_i) f(x_i) / \sum_i w(x_i)$ and destroys the effective sample size (see the ESS discussion in the same section).

**Cross-reference:** *§ The independence sampler: global proposals* (weight definition, envelope condition $\pi \leq Cq$, ESS and "narrow-proposal failure mode").
:::

::: {.exercise}

Gibbs sampler for a bivariate Gaussian $(X, Y) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Derive the full conditionals $p(x \mid y)$ and $p(y \mid x)$ and give a Gibbs sweep update rule.
:::

::: {.solution}
We must derive the two full conditionals of the centred bivariate Gaussian with correlation $\rho$ and write down one Gibbs sweep.

**Full-conditional definition.** By the definition of a full conditional in *§ The Gibbs sampler: coordinate-wise updates*,
$$p(x \mid y) \propto p(x, y),$$
treating $y$ as fixed. The joint log density of $(X,Y) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ is (up to a constant in $x$)
$$\log p(x, y) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,(x, y)\, \Sigma^{-1} (x, y)^\top + \text{const}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Expanding the quadratic form,
$$-\tfrac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(x^2 - 2\rho x y + y^2\bigr).$$

**Completing the square in $x$.** Dropping terms independent of $x$,
$$\log p(x\mid y) = -\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(x^2 - 2\rho x y\bigr) + \text{const} = -\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigl(x - \rho y\bigr)^2 + \text{const}.$$
Reading off the Gaussian form,
$$X \mid Y = y \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho y,\, 1 - \rho^2\bigr).$$
By symmetry of the joint distribution under swapping the roles of $X$ and $Y$,
$$Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho x,\, 1 - \rho^2\bigr).$$
(Equivalently, from the standard formula $E[X\mid Y] = \Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}Y = \rho Y$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X\mid Y) = 1 - \rho\cdot\rho = 1 - \rho^2$.)

**One Gibbs sweep.** By the *Gibbs Sampler Algorithm* theorem in *§ Algorithm: deterministic and random scan*, one iteration is
$$x^{(t)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho\, y^{(t-1)},\, 1-\rho^2\bigr), \qquad y^{(t)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho\, x^{(t)},\, 1-\rho^2\bigr).$$
Note that the conditional variance $1-\rho^2$ shrinks as $|\rho|\to 1$ — this is exactly the mechanism behind the slow mixing shown in the worked example on the bivariate Gaussian.

**Cross-reference:** *§ The Gibbs sampler: coordinate-wise updates* (full-conditional definition) and *§ Algorithm: deterministic and random scan* (Gibbs sweep).
:::

::: {.exercise}

Beta–Binomial hierarchical model: $x \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)$, $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$. Derive the full conditional $p(\theta \mid x, \alpha, \beta)$ and identify it as a Beta distribution.
:::

::: {.solution}
By Bayes' rule,
$$p(\theta \mid x, \alpha, \beta) \propto p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta) \propto \theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x}\cdot \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1} = \theta^{\alpha + x - 1}(1-\theta)^{\beta + n - x - 1}.$$
This is the kernel of a Beta distribution, so
$$\theta \mid x, \alpha, \beta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha + x,\, \beta + n - x).$$
Conjugacy makes the Gibbs update a closed-form Beta draw.
:::

::: {.exercise}

Two-component Gaussian mixture: $X_i \mid Z_i = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, 1)$ with $Z_i \in \{1, 2\}$ and $P(Z_i = 1) = w$. Given $\mu_1, \mu_2, w$ and the data, write the full conditional $p(z_i = 1 \mid x_i)$ (the E-step of EM, but in the Gibbs context).
:::

::: {.solution}
By Bayes' rule, with $\phi(x;\mu) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^2\}$,
$$p(z_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \frac{w\,\phi(x_i;\mu_1)}{w\,\phi(x_i;\mu_1) + (1-w)\,\phi(x_i;\mu_2)}.$$
Cancelling the common $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ factor gives the explicit form
$$p(z_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \frac{w\,\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i-\mu_1)^2\}}{w\,\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i-\mu_1)^2\} + (1-w)\,\exp\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i-\mu_2)^2\}}.$$
The Gibbs update samples $z_i$ from this Bernoulli; the EM E-step uses the same expression as a soft responsibility.

::: {.exercise}

Show that the Gibbs sampler is a special case of Metropolis–Hastings in which the proposal for component $k$ is the full conditional $\pi(x_k \mid x_{-k})$, and that the corresponding acceptance probability is always $1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Take the MH proposal $q(x_k' \mid x_k, x_{-k}) = \pi(x_k' \mid x_{-k})$. The MH ratio factorises as
$$\frac{\pi(x_k', x_{-k})\,\pi(x_k \mid x_{-k})}{\pi(x_k, x_{-k})\,\pi(x_k' \mid x_{-k})} = \frac{\pi(x_k'\mid x_{-k})\pi(x_{-i})\,\pi(x_k\mid x_{-i})}{\pi(x_k\mid x_{-i})\pi(x_{-i})\,\pi(x_k'\mid x_{-i})} = 1,$$
using $\pi(x_k, x_{-k}) = \pi(x_k \mid x_{-k})\pi(x_{-k})$. Hence $\alpha(x \to x') = \min\{1, 1\} = 1$ for every Gibbs step.
:::

::: {.exercise}

Block Gibbs. Suppose $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ have posterior correlation $\rho$ close to $1$. Sketch (no calculator) why coordinate-wise Gibbs mixes slowly and why updating $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ jointly fixes the problem.
:::

::: {.solution}
When $\rho \approx 1$ the posterior concentrates on a narrow diagonal ridge. Each axis-aligned Gibbs step has conditional variance $1-\rho^2 \ll 1$, so the chain inches along the ridge by small horizontal/vertical moves and the effective step *along* the ridge is $O(\sqrt{1-\rho^2})$. The block update
$$(\theta_1, \theta_2) \sim p(\theta_1, \theta_2 \mid \text{rest}, D)$$
draws from the joint conditional, which has full variance along the ridge direction. One block step accomplishes what hundreds of coordinate-wise steps would require, eliminating the dependence on $\rho$.
:::

::: {.exercise}

Reversibility from detailed balance. Verify that if a Markov chain satisfies $\pi(x) T(x, y) = \pi(y) T(y, x)$ for all states $x, y$, then $\pi$ is stationary. Is the converse true?
:::

::: {.solution}
Summing detailed balance over $x$:
$$\sum_x \pi(x) T(x,y) = \sum_x \pi(y) T(y,x) = \pi(y) \sum_x T(y,x) = \pi(y),$$
so $(\pi T)(y) = \pi(y)$ and $\pi$ is stationary. The converse is **false**: the deterministic cyclic chain $0\to 1 \to 2 \to 0$ has $\pi = (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$ stationary but violates detailed balance.
:::

::: {.exercise}

Trivial chain. A Markov chain has transition kernel $T(x, y) = \pi(y)$ for every $x$ (i.e.\ each step is an independent draw from $\pi$). Verify detailed balance and compute the integrated autocorrelation time of any well-behaved test function.
:::

::: {.solution}
Detailed balance: $\pi(x)T(x,y) = \pi(x)\pi(y) = \pi(y)\pi(x) = \pi(y)T(y,x)$. Successive states are i.i.d. from $\pi$, so for any test function $f$ with finite variance the lag-$k$ autocorrelation is $\rho_k = 0$ for all $k \geq 1$, and
$$\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \tfrac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k = \tfrac{1}{2}.$$
This is the minimum possible value — i.i.d.\ sampling is the gold standard.
:::

::: {.exercise}

*(Define and explain.)* Define the independence sampler. Write its acceptance probability in terms of the importance weights $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$. Explain its connection to importance sampling.
:::

::: {.solution}
The **independence sampler** is a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm whose proposal $q(x' \mid x) = q(x')$ does not depend on the current state. The MH ratio collapses to
$$\alpha(x \to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\,\frac{\pi(x')\,q(x)}{\pi(x)\,q(x')}\right\} = \min\!\left\{1,\,\frac{w(x')}{w(x)}\right\},$$
the ratio of importance weights $w(x) = \pi(x)/q(x)$. It is the MCMC analogue of importance sampling: $q$ plays the role of a global proposal that should approximate $\pi$, and the acceptance step (rather than weighting) corrects the mismatch. Ergodicity requires the envelope bound $w(x) \leq C < \infty$.
:::

::: {.exercise}

*(Define and explain.)* Define the Gibbs sampler. Show that every Gibbs step is accepted, i.e.\ the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance ratio equals $1$ when the proposal is the full conditional.
:::

::: {.solution}
The **Gibbs sampler** updates parameters $\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)$ cyclically by sampling each coordinate from its full conditional
$$\theta_i^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, D).$$
Viewed as MH with proposal $q(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}) = p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i}, D)$, the acceptance ratio factorises via $p(\theta_i, \theta_{-i}) = p(\theta_i\mid\theta_{-i})p(\theta_{-i})$:
$$\frac{p(\theta_i',\theta_{-i})\,p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i})}{p(\theta_i,\theta_{-i})\,p(\theta_i' \mid \theta_{-i})} = \frac{p(\theta_i'\mid\theta_{-i})p(\theta_i\mid\theta_{-i})}{p(\theta_i\mid\theta_{-i})p(\theta_i'\mid\theta_{-i})} = 1.$$
Hence $\alpha = 1$ — every Gibbs proposal is accepted.
:::

::: {.exercise}

*(Compare and contrast.)* For a target with strongly correlated coordinates, compare the mixing behaviour of coordinate-wise Gibbs, block Gibbs, and random-walk Metropolis. State which one you would prefer in this situation and why.
:::

::: {.solution}
- **Coordinate-wise Gibbs**: always accepts but takes $O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{1-\rho^2}\right)$ steps to cross the posterior ridge — slow when $|\rho| \to 1$.
- **Random-walk MH with proposal covariance $\Sigma_{\mathrm{prop}}$**: also slow unless $\Sigma_{\mathrm{prop}}$ is tuned to match the elongated posterior covariance $\Sigma$; an isotropic proposal wastes effort across the ridge.
- **Block Gibbs**: samples $(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ jointly from the exact joint conditional, removing the dependence on $\rho$ entirely.

**Preferred**: block Gibbs, because it eliminates the bottleneck at its source. If the joint conditional is intractable, the next best option is random-walk MH with a preconditioned proposal $\Sigma_{\mathrm{prop}} \propto \Sigma$ (or HMC).
:::

::: {.exercise}

*(Define and explain.)* Define integrated autocorrelation time. Explain in one sentence why grouping (blocking) strongly correlated parameters in a Gibbs sampler lowers it.
:::

::: {.solution}
For a stationary chain and test function $f$ with lag-$k$ autocorrelation $\rho_k$,
$$\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \tfrac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k,$$
so that the effective sample size is $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = N/(2\tau_{\mathrm{int}})$. Blocking strongly correlated coordinates lets one update move along the posterior ridge in a single step (instead of many small axis-aligned moves), which collapses the slow tail of $\rho_k$ and therefore reduces $\tau_{\mathrm{int}}$.
:::
