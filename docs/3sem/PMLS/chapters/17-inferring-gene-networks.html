17  Inferring gene interaction networks by maximum entropy

Chapter 16 started from known regulatory interactions and predicted the dynamics. In practice the problem is reversed: we measure the expression of thousands of genes in many samples or single cells, and want to infer who regulates whom. This chapter treats network inference as a statistical-physics inverse problem. The maximum-entropy principle gives the least biased model that reproduces the measured means and correlations. For Gaussian data the inferred interaction matrix is the inverse of the covariance matrix, which separates direct interactions from indirect correlations. For binarised single-cell data, a kinetic (asymmetric) Ising model learned from time-ordered cells yields directed, signed networks able to predict the effect of perturbations.

17.1 Gene expression data

A typical dataset is a matrix of counts \(n_{it}\): the number of mRNA reads of gene \(i = 1, \dots, N\) in sample (or cell) \(t = 1, \dots, T\). Before analysis, raw counts are normalised for sequencing depth (\(\sum_in_{it}\)) and gene length \(L_i\), and log-transformed: \[x_{it} = \log\frac{n_{it}}{L_i\,\sum_jn_{jt}} \quad\text{(up to constants)}.\] Bulk RNA-seq averages millions of cells per sample. Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) measures individual cells, revealing heterogeneity and transitions between states, but each cell is measured only once (it is destroyed). Dynamics must be reconstructed from snapshots, e.g. by ordering cells along a pseudotime of differentiation.

From the data we can estimate the means \(\langle x_i\rangle\) and the pairwise correlations \(\langle x_ix_j\rangle\). Ideally we would like the full joint distribution \(p(x_1, \dots, x_N)\), but with \(N \sim 10^4\) genes and \(T \ll\) the number of possible states, it cannot be estimated directly. A principled proxy is needed.

17.2 The maximum-entropy principle

Among all distributions consistent with what we know, choose the one that assumes nothing else: the one maximising the entropy \(S[\rho] = -\sum_{\mathbf x}\rho(\mathbf x)\ln\rho(\mathbf x)\) (Jaynes, 1957). Imposing normalisation, the means and the second moments, with Lagrange multipliers \(\nu\), \(\psi_i\) and \(\lambda_{ij}\), \[\mathcal S = S[\rho] - \nu\Bigl(\sum\rho - 1\Bigr) - \sum_i\psi_i\Bigl(\sum\rho\,x_i - \langle x_i\rangle\Bigr) - \sum_{i\leq j}\lambda_{ij}\Bigl(\sum\rho\,x_ix_j - \langle x_ix_j\rangle\Bigr).\] Setting \(\delta\mathcal S/\delta\rho = 0\): \[\rho(\mathbf x) = \frac1Z\exp\Bigl(-\boldsymbol\psi\cdot\mathbf x - \frac12\mathbf x^\top M\mathbf x\Bigr), \qquad M_{ij} = \lambda_{ij} + \lambda_{ji}.\]

17.2.1 Continuous expression levels

For continuous \(x\) the maximum-entropy distribution is a multivariate Gaussian. Completing the square, \(\mathbf y = \mathbf x + M^{-1}\boldsymbol\psi\), it is centred at \(\langle\mathbf x\rangle = -M^{-1}\boldsymbol\psi\) with covariance \[C_{ij} = \langle x_ix_j\rangle - \langle x_i\rangle\langle x_j\rangle = (M^{-1})_{ij} .\] So the interaction matrix is the inverse of the covariance matrix (the precision matrix): \[M = C^{-1} .\] The model has \(N + N(N + 1)/2\) parameters, exactly the number of constraints, and the “Hamiltonian” \(H = \frac12\mathbf x^\top M\mathbf x + \boldsymbol\psi\cdot\mathbf x\) reads \(M_{ij}\) as the effective interaction between genes \(i\) and \(j\).

Correlation is not interaction. If gene \(A\) regulates \(B\) and \(B\) regulates \(C\), then \(A\) and \(C\) are correlated although they do not interact directly. Biologists often build “co-expression networks” by thresholding correlations, which mixes direct and indirect effects. The inverse covariance removes the indirect contributions: \(M_{ij}\) is proportional to the partial correlation of \(i\) and \(j\) given all other genes, and is zero when \(i\) and \(j\) are conditionally independent.

17.2.2 The undersampled regime

With \(T < N\) samples the empirical covariance matrix has rank at most \(T\) and cannot be inverted. Lezon et al. (2006) used the spectral decomposition \[C = \sum_{k=1}^N\omega_k\,\mathbf v^{(k)}\mathbf v^{(k)\top},\] keeping only the \(T - 1\) non-zero eigenvalues and defining the pseudo-inverse \(M = \sum_{k:\omega_k > 0}\omega_k^{-1}\mathbf v^{(k)}\mathbf v^{(k)\top}\). Regularised alternatives (ridge, the graphical lasso with an \(\ell_1\) penalty that favours sparse \(M\)) are used in practice.

17.2.3 Are pairwise interactions enough?

The maximum-entropy model can be extended with three-body terms, \(H \supset \sum T_{ijk}x_ix_jx_k\). Applied to time series of yeast gene expression during the metabolic cycle, the distribution of inferred pairwise couplings is broad, while that of triplet couplings is narrowly centred on zero (Lezon et al., 2006): pairwise interactions dominate. Significance is assessed with a null model: shuffling the data table independently for each gene destroys correlations while preserving single-gene statistics, and couplings within the null distribution are discarded. The inferred networks show a hub-dominated structure, with modules matching functional categories (translation, transcription, cell division, mitochondria, ribosome biogenesis).

17.3 Binary expression and the Ising model

For many purposes it is enough to know whether a gene is “on” or “off”. Binarising each gene’s expression relative to a threshold (e.g. half its maximum), \[s_i(t) = \begin{cases}+1 & x_i(t) \geq \frac12\max_tx_i\\ -1 & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\] the maximum-entropy distribution with fixed means and pairwise correlations is the Ising model of Chapter 12, \[P(\mathbf s) = \frac1Z\exp\Bigl(\sum_ih_is_i + \sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\Bigr),\] with symmetric couplings. Symmetric couplings cannot represent the direction of regulation (\(A\) activates \(B\) but not vice versa) and are learned from equal-time statistics only.

17.4 A kinetic Ising model for single-cell data

Ordering single cells along pseudotime turns snapshots into an effective time series. A kinetic (asymmetric) Ising model then describes how each gene’s state depends on the previous state of the others (Glauber dynamics, as in Chapter 10): \[p\bigl(s_i(t + dt) \mid \mathbf s(t)\bigr) = \frac{\gamma\,dt}{2}\Bigl[1 + s_i(t + dt)\tanh\theta_i(t)\Bigr] + (1 - \gamma\,dt)\,\delta_{s_i(t + dt),s_i(t)}, \qquad \theta_i(t) = h_i + \sum_jJ_{ij}s_j(t),\] where \(\gamma\) is the update rate and \(J_{ij} \neq J_{ji}\) in general. The log-likelihood of the observed sequence over the update times \(\tau_i\) of each gene, \[\mathcal L(\{h_i\}, \{J_{ij}\}) = \sum_i\sum_{\tau_i}\bigl[s_i(\tau_i + dt)\,\theta_i(\tau_i) - \ln2\cosh\theta_i(\tau_i)\bigr],\] is maximised by gradient ascent: \[\delta J_{ij} \propto \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial J_{ij}} = \frac{\dot C_{ij}(0)}{\gamma} + C_{ij}(0) - \bigl\langle\tanh\theta_i(t)\,s_j(t)\bigr\rangle,\] with \(C_{ij}(0) = \langle s_i(t)s_j(t)\rangle\) the equal-time correlation and \(\dot C_{ij}(0)\) its time derivative. The derivative term carries the directional information that equal-time statistics lack.

The resulting pipeline (IGNITE, Corridori et al., 2024): quality control and normalisation, pseudotime ordering, binarisation, inference of \((h, J)\) with hyperparameters selected by how well networks simulated with the learned parameters reproduce the data’s correlation matrix. Applied to mouse embryonic stem cells exiting pluripotency (from a naive to a formative state, sampled at \(0\)–\(48\) h), it gives:

  • a directed, signed, weighted gene regulatory network among key pluripotency and differentiation genes;
  • couplings well outside a null model built from \(\sim 2500\) networks inferred from shuffled data, i.e. statistically significant;
  • a fraction of correctly inferred known interactions comparable to or better than other methods (about \(0.67\), similar to MaxEnt, SCODE and CellOracle);
  • a generative model. Simulating the learned dynamics reproduces the clustering of the data and its trajectory in principal-component space, and switching genes off in silico predicts the effect of knock-outs, which can be compared with perturbation experiments.

17.5 Exercises

(Maximum entropy with fixed mean and variance) Show that the maximum-entropy density on \(\mathbb R\) with fixed mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) is the Gaussian, and that with only the mean fixed on \([0, \infty)\) it is the exponential.

Solution. Stationarity of \(-\int\rho\ln\rho - \nu(\int\rho - 1) - \psi(\int x\rho - \mu) - \lambda(\int x^2\rho - \sigma^2 - \mu^2)\) gives \(-\ln\rho - 1 - \nu - \psi x - \lambda x^2 = 0\), so \(\rho \propto e^{-\psi x - \lambda x^2}\), a Gaussian whose parameters are fixed by the constraints (\(\lambda = 1/2\sigma^2\), \(\psi = -\mu/\sigma^2\)). With only the mean on \([0, \infty)\), \(\rho \propto e^{-\psi x}\): the exponential with \(\psi = 1/\mu\). Maximum entropy reproduces the familiar distributions as the least informative ones given the constraints, and the Lagrange multipliers play the role of couplings.

(Correlation versus interaction) Three genes form a chain \(A - B - C\) with Gaussian interaction matrix \(M = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -0.4 & 0\\ -0.4 & 1 & -0.4\\ 0 & -0.4 & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Compute the correlation matrix. Would thresholding correlations at \(0.15\) recover the true network?

Solution. \(C = M^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1.235 & 0.588 & 0.235\\ 0.588 & 1.471 & 0.588\\ 0.235 & 0.588 & 1.235\end{pmatrix}\), giving correlation coefficients \(\rho_{AB} = \rho_{BC} = 0.44\) and \(\rho_{AC} = 0.19\). Thresholding at \(0.15\) would add a spurious \(A\)–\(C\) link. The partial correlations \(-M_{ij}/\sqrt{M_{ii}M_{jj}}\) are \(0.4\) for \(A\)–\(B\) and \(B\)–\(C\) and exactly \(0\) for \(A\)–\(C\): inverting the covariance removes the indirect correlation mediated by \(B\).

(Pseudo-inverse) With \(N = 1000\) genes and \(T = 50\) samples, how many eigenvalues of the empirical covariance matrix are non-zero? What does the pseudo-inverse do, and what is its main limitation?

Solution. After subtracting the sample mean, the centred data matrix has rank at most \(T - 1 = 49\), so at most \(49\) eigenvalues are non-zero and \(951\) vanish. The pseudo-inverse inverts \(C\) on the \(49\)-dimensional subspace spanned by the data and sets \(M\) to zero on its complement. Interactions are thus inferred only along the directions the data explore, and the smallest non-zero eigenvalues, dominated by sampling noise, are amplified by \(1/\omega_k\). Regularisation (truncating small eigenvalues, ridge or lasso penalties) controls this noise at the cost of bias.

(Kinetic Ising learning) For a single gene \(i\) driven by a single gene \(j\), \(\theta_i = h + Js_j\), show that the maximum-likelihood conditions are \(\langle s_i^{\text{next}}\rangle = \langle\tanh\theta_i\rangle\) and \(\langle s_i^{\text{next}}s_j\rangle = \langle\tanh\theta_i\,s_j\rangle\). If \(s_j = \pm1\) with equal probability and \(s_i^{\text{next}} = s_j\) in \(90\%\) of the updates, what are \(h\) and \(J\)?

Solution. \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial h = \sum[s_i^{\text{next}} - \tanh\theta_i]\) and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial J = \sum[s_i^{\text{next}} - \tanh\theta_i]s_j\); setting both to zero gives the conditions. By symmetry \(h = 0\). Then \(\tanh\theta_i\,s_j = \tanh(J)\) for both values of \(s_j\), and \(\langle s_i^{\text{next}}s_j\rangle = 0.9 - 0.1 = 0.8\). So \(\tanh J = 0.8\) and \(J = \operatorname{artanh}0.8 = 1.10\). The coupling from \(j\) to \(i\) is positive (activation). The reverse coupling \(J_{ji}\) is learned separately from the updates of \(j\), and may be zero.
