3  Dynamics of a single species

This chapter follows Prof. Suweis’s Chapter 2. It builds from the simplest ecological model — a single bacterial population — up to a stochastic description that captures demographic noise. The four subsections mirror the source: (i) exponential and logistic growth from cell-division kinetics; (ii) the consumer–resource model and the quasi-stationary approximation; (iii) the stochastic birth–death master equation and its Fokker–Planck / Langevin limits; (iv) the stationary distribution and stochastic doubling time.

3.1 2.1 Population dynamics: exponential and logistic growth

The paradigmatic microbial system is E. coli in a chemostat. Under the microscope a single bacterium grows up to a critical size and then divides into two — a cascade reproduction process. In optimal conditions the reproduction time is \(\tau \approx 20\) minutes.

Let \(N(t)\) be the population size at time \(t\). If per-capita reproduction happens at rate \(\mu\), the deterministic mean-field law is \[\frac{dN}{dt} = \mu N \implies N(t) = N(0)\,e^{\mu t} = N(0)\,2^{t/T},\] with doubling time \(T = \ln 2 / \mu\). For E. coli in optimal conditions \(\mu \approx 2.1\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}\), so \(T \approx 20\) min.

Remark. Two implicit assumptions: (i) \(N\) is treated as a continuous variable, which is a poor approximation for \(N \sim O(1)\) near \(t = 0\); (ii) resources are assumed unlimited. Removing (ii) is what motivates the logistic model below.

The resource-limited fix is Verhulst’s:

With carrying capacity \(k > 0\) the density-dependent update is \[\frac{dN}{dt} = \mu N\left(1 - \frac{N}{k}\right),\] whose explicit solution is \[N(t) = \frac{N(0)\,e^{\mu t}}{1 + \tfrac{N(0)}{k}(e^{\mu t} - 1)} .\] The two fixed points are \(N^* = 0\) (unstable if \(\mu > 0\)) and \(N^* = k\) (stable).

A batch culture of E. coli fits the logistic law until nutrients deplete. The lag phase corresponds to \(N \ll k\) where \(N(t) \approx N(0)\,e^{\mu t}\); the plateau corresponds to \(N \to k\). Fitting \(\mu\) and \(k\) from an OD-600 curve is one of the first assays a biophysics student runs.

3.1.1 2.1.1 Consumer Resource Model

Rather than encoding resource limitation in an ad-hoc carrying capacity, we can model the resource \(R(t)\) explicitly. Let \(g(R)\) be the resource uptake rate per unit cell and \(\mu(R)\) the growth-resource function. With uptake efficiency \(c\), yield coefficient \(\gamma\) (number of nutrient units to produce a bacterium), and death rate \(d\):

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = (\gamma c\,g(R) - d)\,N, \qquad \frac{dR}{dt} = \mu(R) - c\,g(R)\,N .\]

To get a closed-form population equation, assume the resource equilibrates much faster than the consumers (the quasi-stationary approximation, QSA): set \(dR/dt = 0\) and eliminate \(R\). For a biotic resource with logistic dynamics of its own, \(\mu(R) = R r^*(1 - R/k_c)\), algebra gives an effective logistic law

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = N\,(\tilde k - \tilde a N),\]

with effective growth rate \(\tilde k\) and effective saturation \(\tilde a\) built from the microscopic constants \(\{s, \mu, c, e, m\}\).

In the quasi-stationary limit of the consumer–resource model the population dynamics reduce to \[\dot N = r\,N(1 - N/K) + O(N^2),\] with \(r\) and \(K\) explicit combinations of the microscopic rates. The logistic law of §2.1 is thus a derived description, not a phenomenological guess.

3.2 2.2 Stochastic modelling of population growth

The deterministic ODE ignores the discreteness of individuals and the demographic noise that comes with it. Both matter when \(N\) is small.

3.2.1 2.2.1 Birth–death master equation

Let \(P_n(t)\) be the probability the population is \(n\) at time \(t\), with per-cell birth rate \(b_n\) and death rate \(d_n\). The master equation is \[\frac{dP_n(t)}{dt} = b_{n-1} P_{n-1}(t) + d_{n+1} P_{n+1}(t) - (b_n + d_n) P_n(t) .\] The right-hand side is gain minus loss of probability at state \(n\).

For the linear case \(b_n = bn\), \(d_n = dn\), the moments obey closed equations.

Starting from \(n(0) = n_0\), \[\langle n(t)\rangle = n_0\,e^{(b-d)t}, \qquad \text{Var}[n(t)] = n_0\,\frac{b+d}{b-d}\bigl(e^{2(b-d)t} - e^{(b-d)t}\bigr).\] The Fano factor \(F = \text{Var}[n]/\langle n\rangle\) grows without bound whenever \(b > d\): fluctuations are strongly super-Poissonian.

3.2.2 2.2.2 Fokker–Planck and Langevin equations

Rescaling \(n = \Omega x\) with \(\Omega \gg 1\) and expanding the master equation to second order gives the system-size expansion (Kramers–Moyal):

In the limit of large system size \(\Omega\), \[\partial_t \rho(x, t) = -\partial_x[A(x)\rho] + \frac{1}{2\Omega}\partial_x^2[B(x)\rho],\] with drift \(A(x) = \lim_{\Omega \to \infty}\Omega^{-1}(b_{\Omega x} - d_{\Omega x})\) and diffusion \(B(x) = \lim \Omega^{-1}(b_{\Omega x} + d_{\Omega x})\). The corresponding (Itô) SDE — the Langevin equation — is \[dx = A(x)\,dt + \sqrt{B(x)/\Omega}\,dW_t .\]

For the logistic birth–death process (\(b_n = bn\), \(d_n = dn + qn^2/K\)) one finds \(A(x) = rx(1-x)\) and \(B(x) = (b+d)x\).

3.2.3 2.2.3 Random walk

Close to the extinction boundary \(x \approx 0\), the drift is \(A \approx rx\) and the diffusion \(B \approx (b+d)x\), so \[dx \approx r x\,dt + \sqrt{(b+d)x/\Omega}\,dW_t .\] A Lamperti-type transform \(y = 2\sqrt{x\Omega/(b+d)}\) maps this to a biased random walk: \[dy = \tilde r\,dt + dW_t.\]

Setting \(r = 0\) (\(b = d\)) makes the walker unbiased. Standard random-walk theory shows that a walker on the half-line with a reflecting or absorbing barrier at 0 hits 0 with probability one: the population is extinct almost surely, even though its mean is conserved.

3.2.4 2.2.4 Stationary solution with linear rates

For a birth–death process satisfying detailed balance the stationary distribution is a product of rate ratios: \[P^{ss}_n = P^{ss}_0 \prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{b_{k-1}}{d_k} .\]

For \(b_n = bn\) and \(d_n = dn + qn^2/K\) the stationary distribution has mode near the deterministic fixed point \(n^* = K(1 - d/b)\), with variance \(O(K)\). In the deterministic limit \(K \to \infty\) the distribution concentrates at \(n^*\).

3.2.5 2.2.5 Stochastic doubling

For a linear birth–death process with \(b > d\) the mean doubling time is \[\tau_2 = \ln 2 / (b - d),\] independent of \(n_0\). The fluctuations have coefficient of variation \[\frac{\sigma_{\tau_2}}{\tau_2} \sim \sqrt{\frac{b + d}{n_0\,(b - d)}} ,\] so relative fluctuations matter only for small populations. This is why single-cell lineage-tracking experiments see a broad distribution of division times.

Remark (Physical meaning). The three levels of description — master equation, Fokker–Planck, Langevin — are the same process at different resolutions: the master equation is exact but discrete; the Fokker–Planck retains fluctuations of order \(\Omega^{-1/2}\); the deterministic ODE \(\dot x = A(x)\) keeps only the drift. Going up the ladder trades tractability for exactness.

3.3 Exercises

Solve the logistic ODE explicitly. Verify that \(N(t) \to K\) as \(t \to \infty\) for any \(N_0 > 0\).

Solution. Separate variables: \[\frac{dN}{N(1 - N/K)} = \mu\,dt .\] Partial-fraction expand the left-hand side, integrate, and exponentiate to obtain \[N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + (K/N_0 - 1)e^{-\mu t}} .\] For \(\mu > 0\) the exponential decays, so \(N(t) \to K\).

Show that the linear birth–death process with \(b = d\) has \(\langle n(t)\rangle = n_0\) but \(\text{Var}[n(t)] = 2 b n_0 t\). Interpret the ratio \(\sigma / \langle n\rangle\).

Solution. Set \(b = d\) in the mean/variance formulas and take the limit \(b - d \to 0\) using l’Hôpital. Variance grows linearly in time: the Fano factor grows as \(2bt\), so relative fluctuations grow without bound. A critical branching process eventually goes extinct with probability one even though its mean is conserved.

Derive the QSA effective logistic law from the biotic consumer–resource system. Identify \(r\) and \(K\) explicitly in terms of \(\{r^*, k_c, c, \gamma, d\}\).

Solution. Setting \(\dot R = 0\): \(R^* = k_c(1 - cN/r^*)\). Substituting into \(\dot N/N = \gamma c R^* - d\) gives \(\dot N = N(\tilde k - \tilde a N)\) with \(\tilde k = \gamma c k_c - d\) and \(\tilde a = \gamma c^2 k_c / r^*\). Matching with the standard \(\dot N = rN(1 - N/K)\) yields \(r = \tilde k\) and \(K = \tilde k / \tilde a = r^*(\gamma c k_c - d)/(\gamma c^2 k_c) = r^*(1 - d/(\gamma c k_c))/c\).

3.4 Callouts

The logistic law is a phenomenological summary of many microscopic mechanisms (nutrient limitation, quorum sensing, waste build-up). Fitting a curve to it does not identify a mechanism.

Whenever a deterministic ODE has a fixed point at \(n^* = 0\) and a birth term proportional to \(n\), extinction has finite probability at all finite \(n_0\). Deterministic stability is not the same as stochastic survival.

When simulating a stochastic birth–death process, use the Gillespie algorithm rather than a discretised Langevin scheme for small \(n\): the Gillespie algorithm samples the exact interarrival distribution and preserves integer \(n\).

The Fokker–Planck limit assumes \(\Omega \gg 1\): it fails near extinction (\(n \ll \Omega\)). If you care about extinction probabilities, go back to the master equation.

Fitting parameters \((r, K)\) from data by nonlinear least squares often looks fine but hides large uncertainties: the correlation between \(r\) and \(K\) in the logistic fit is close to \(\pm 1\) unless the early exponential phase is well-sampled.

3.5 Book supplements

The system-size expansion and the birth–death formalism are treated in canonical detail in van Kampen, Stochastic Processes in Physics and Chemistry (Elsevier, 2007), Chapters IV, VII and XI. The QSA and the consumer–resource derivation are covered in Nowak, Evolutionary Dynamics (Harvard, 2006), Chapter 3.

## 2.2 Stochastic modelling of population growth

The deterministic ODE ignores the discreteness of individuals and the demographic noise that comes with it. Both matter when $N$ is small.

### 2.2.1 Birth–death master equation

::: {.definition name="Master equation for a birth–death process"}
Let $P_n(t)$ be the probability the population is $n$ at time $t$, with per-cell birth rate $b_n$ and death rate $d_n$. The **master equation** is
$$\frac{dP_n(t)}{dt} = b_{n-1} P_{n-1}(t) + d_{n+1} P_{n+1}(t) - (b_n + d_n) P_n(t) .$$
The right-hand side is *gain minus loss* of probability at state $n$.
:::

For the *linear* case $b_n = bn$, $d_n = dn$, the moments obey closed equations.

::: {.theorem name="Mean and variance of a linear birth–death process"}
Starting from $n(0) = n_0$,
$$\langle n(t)\rangle = n_0\,e^{(b-d)t}, \qquad \text{Var}[n(t)] = n_0\,\frac{b+d}{b-d}\bigl(e^{2(b-d)t} - e^{(b-d)t}\bigr).$$
The Fano factor $F = \text{Var}[n]/\langle n\rangle$ grows without bound whenever $b > d$: fluctuations are strongly super-Poissonian.
:::

### 2.2.2 Fokker–Planck and Langevin equations

Rescaling $n = \Omega x$ with $\Omega \gg 1$ and expanding the master equation to second order gives the *system-size expansion* (Kramers–Moyal):

::: {.theorem name="Fokker–Planck limit"}
In the limit of large system size $\Omega$,
$$\partial_t \rho(x, t) = -\partial_x[A(x)\rho] + \frac{1}{2\Omega}\partial_x^2[B(x)\rho],$$
with drift $A(x) = \lim_{\Omega \to \infty}\Omega^{-1}(b_{\Omega x} - d_{\Omega x})$ and diffusion $B(x) = \lim \Omega^{-1}(b_{\Omega x} + d_{\Omega x})$. The corresponding (Itô) SDE — the **Langevin equation** — is
$$dx = A(x)\,dt + \sqrt{B(x)/\Omega}\,dW_t .$$
:::

For the logistic birth–death process ($b_n = bn$, $d_n = dn + qn^2/K$) one finds $A(x) = rx(1-x)$ and $B(x) = (b+d)x$.

### 2.2.3 Random walk

Close to the extinction boundary $x \approx 0$, the drift is $A \approx rx$ and the diffusion $B \approx (b+d)x$, so
$$dx \approx r x\,dt + \sqrt{(b+d)x/\Omega}\,dW_t .$$
A Lamperti-type transform $y = 2\sqrt{x\Omega/(b+d)}$ maps this to a *biased random walk*:
$$dy = \tilde r\,dt + dW_t.$$

::: {.example name="Extinction of a critical branching process"}
Setting $r = 0$ ($b = d$) makes the walker unbiased. Standard random-walk theory shows that a walker on the half-line with a reflecting or absorbing barrier at 0 hits 0 with probability one: the population is extinct almost surely, even though its mean is conserved.
:::

### 2.2.4 Stationary solution with linear rates

For a birth–death process satisfying detailed balance the stationary distribution is a product of rate ratios:
$$P^{ss}_n = P^{ss}_0 \prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{b_{k-1}}{d_k} .$$

::: {.proposition name="Stationary of logistic birth–death"}
For $b_n = bn$ and $d_n = dn + qn^2/K$ the stationary distribution has mode near the deterministic fixed point $n^* = K(1 - d/b)$, with variance $O(K)$. In the deterministic limit $K \to \infty$ the distribution concentrates at $n^*$.
:::

### 2.2.5 Stochastic doubling

For a linear birth–death process with $b > d$ the **mean** doubling time is
$$\tau_2 = \ln 2 / (b - d),$$
independent of $n_0$. The *fluctuations* have coefficient of variation
$$\frac{\sigma_{\tau_2}}{\tau_2} \sim \sqrt{\frac{b + d}{n_0\,(b - d)}} ,$$
so relative fluctuations matter only for small populations. This is why single-cell lineage-tracking experiments see a broad distribution of division times.

::: {.remark name="Physical meaning"}
The three levels of description — master equation, Fokker–Planck, Langevin — are the same process at different resolutions: the master equation is exact but discrete; the Fokker–Planck retains fluctuations of order $\Omega^{-1/2}$; the deterministic ODE $\dot x = A(x)$ keeps only the drift. Going up the ladder trades tractability for exactness.
:::
