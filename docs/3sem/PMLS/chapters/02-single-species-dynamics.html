2  Dynamics of a single species

We start from the simplest living system that can be modelled quantitatively: one population of bacteria. The chapter has two halves. The deterministic half derives exponential and logistic growth from a symmetry argument and from an explicit model of resource consumption. The stochastic half replaces the population by a random variable, writes its master equation, and develops the tools used throughout the course: stationary solutions and detailed balance, the Gillespie algorithm, the Fokker–Planck and Langevin limits, and first-passage problems such as the timing of cell division.

2.1 Basic microbiology

A bacterium such as Escherichia coli is a rod roughly \(2\)–\(4\,\mu\)m long and \(0.8\,\mu\)m wide. It is enclosed by an inner membrane, a cell wall and (in Gram-negative species) an outer membrane, and it contains a “soup” of DNA, RNA, proteins and small molecules. Nutrients from the surrounding medium cross the membranes and are converted into biomass by metabolism. When the cell reaches a critical size it divides into two daughters (binary fission). In rich medium at \(37\,^\circ\)C, E. coli divides every \(\tau \approx 20\) minutes.

DNA is a sequence of nucleotides (bases A, C, G, T) organised into genes. To a first approximation each gene can be treated as a binary variable, on when it is transcribed and its protein produced, off otherwise. This picture becomes quantitative in Part III.

2.2 Deterministic growth laws

2.2.1 Growth laws from scale invariance

Let \(N(t)\) be the number of individuals living in an area (or volume) \(A\), and suppose the growth rate depends only on these two quantities, \(\dot N = F(N, A)\). If we consider \(\lambda\) identical, independent copies of the system, the total population must obey the same law: \[F(\lambda N, \lambda A) = \lambda F(N, A).\] \(F\) is homogeneous of degree one. Choosing \(\lambda = 1/A\) gives \(F(N, A) = A\,F(N/A, 1) \equiv A f(\rho)\), so the density \(\rho = N/A\) obeys an autonomous equation \[\dot\rho = f(\rho).\] Expanding \(f\) for small densities, \[f(\rho) = f_0 + f_1\rho + \tfrac12 f_2\rho^2 + \dots,\] each coefficient has a meaning:

  • \(f_0 > 0\) describes immigration into an open system; \(f_0 = 0\) for a closed population, which must stay extinct once extinct.
  • \(f_1 = r\) is the per-capita growth rate at low density. If \(f_1 > 0\) the empty state is unstable and the population grows exponentially.
  • \(f_2 < 0\) describes self-limitation (crowding, competition for resources).

Truncating at second order with \(f_0 = 0\), \(f_1 = r > 0\), \(f_2 = -2r/K\) gives the logistic law. It is the generic behaviour of a closed population near extinction and near saturation, not an ad hoc guess.

Exponential growth: \(\dot N = \mu N\), so \(N(t) = N_0e^{\mu t} = N_0\,2^{t/T}\), with doubling time \(T = \ln 2/\mu\). For E. coli in optimal conditions \(\mu \simeq 2.1\,\mathrm h^{-1}\), so \(T \simeq 20\) min.

Logistic growth (Verhulst, 1838): with carrying capacity \(K\), \[\dot N = \mu N\Bigl(1 - \frac{N}{K}\Bigr), \qquad N(t) = \frac{N_0e^{\mu t}}{1 + \frac{N_0}{K}\bigl(e^{\mu t} - 1\bigr)} .\]

The solution follows by separation of variables, \(\int \frac{dN}{N(1 - N/K)} = \mu t\), and partial fractions. A bacterial batch culture fits it well: an exponential phase while \(N \ll K\), a saturation (stationary) phase as \(N \to K\). A real culture also has an initial lag phase, during which cells adapt to the new medium.

2.2.2 Linear stability

An equilibrium \(N^*\) of \(\dot N = F(N)\) is stable if small perturbations decay. Writing \(N = N^* + \delta\) and expanding, \(\dot\delta = F'(N^*)\delta\), so \(N^*\) is stable if \(F'(N^*) < 0\), with relaxation time \(1/|F'(N^*)|\). For the logistic law \(F'(N) = \mu(1 - 2N/K)\): \(N^* = 0\) has \(F'(0) = \mu > 0\) (unstable) and \(N^* = K\) has \(F'(K) = -\mu\) (stable, relaxation time \(1/\mu\)).

For systems of equations \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf F(\mathbf x)\), the role of \(F'\) is played by the Jacobian \(J_{ij} = \partial F_i/\partial x_j\) at the equilibrium. The equilibrium is stable iff all its eigenvalues have negative real part. This criterion will be the starting point of the random-matrix analysis of Chapter 6.

2.3 Consumer–resource models

The carrying capacity hides the resource. To make it explicit, let \(R(t)\) be the amount of resource, \(g(R)\) the uptake rate per cell, \(\mu(R)\) the resource’s own supply or growth, \(\gamma\) the yield (cells produced per unit of resource), \(c\) an uptake efficiency (set to \(1\) below) and \(d\) the death rate: \[\dot N = \bigl(\gamma c\,g(R) - d\bigr)N, \qquad \dot R = \mu(R) - c\,g(R)\,N .\] Common uptake functions are linear, \(g(R) = qR\) (mass action), and saturating, the Monod law \[g(R) = \frac{q_M R}{K_M + R},\] which states that a cell cannot process resource faster than \(q_M\). \(K_M\) is the concentration at which uptake is half-maximal.

If the resource relaxes much faster than the population, then on the population’s time scale \(R\) is always at its instantaneous equilibrium, found by setting \(\dot R = 0\) at fixed \(N\). Substituting it into \(\dot N\) gives a closed equation for \(N\) alone. This is adiabatic elimination of a fast variable, and it is used throughout physics and biochemistry (Michaelis–Menten kinetics is the same idea).

Biotic resource (MacArthur). If the resource is itself a living population with logistic growth, \(\mu(R) = r^*R(1 - R/k_c)\), and uptake is linear, then \[\dot N = N(\gamma cR - d), \qquad \dot R = R\Bigl[r^*\Bigl(1 - \frac{R}{k_c}\Bigr) - cN\Bigr].\] The QSA with \(R \neq 0\) gives \(R^* = k_c\bigl(1 - cN/r^*\bigr)\), and substituting, \[\dot N = N\bigl(\tilde k - \tilde a N\bigr), \qquad \tilde k = \gamma ck_c - d, \quad \tilde a = \frac{\gamma c^2k_c}{r^*},\] a logistic law with growth rate \(\mu = \tilde k\) and carrying capacity \(K = \tilde k/\tilde a\). The carrying capacity is now derived from the resource’s parameters. The population survives only if \(\gamma ck_c > d\): the resource must be able to sustain more births than deaths.

Abiotic resource. If the resource is supplied at a constant rate, \(\mu(R) = C\) (a chemostat), the QSA gives \(g(R)N = C\) and then \[\dot N = \gamma C - dN, \qquad N^* = \frac{\gamma C}{d},\] whatever the uptake function: the population relaxes linearly to \(N^*\) with time constant \(1/d\), not logistically. The uptake function only fixes the resource level at equilibrium, through \(\gamma g(R^*) = d\): \[R^*_{\text{linear}} = \frac{d}{\gamma q}, \qquad R^*_{\text{Monod}} = \frac{K_Md}{\gamma q_M - d}.\] With Monod uptake a population can persist only if \(\gamma q_M > d\), i.e. if its maximal growth beats death. The QSA also requires \(C/N < q_M\): a small population cannot consume all the supplied resource, which then accumulates.

2.4 Stochastic population dynamics

Populations are made of discrete individuals, and births and deaths are random events. Deterministic equations describe the mean behaviour of large populations. Near extinction, or in small populations, demographic noise dominates.

NoteRates and probabilities

A process occurring at rate \(r\) produces, in a time \(t\), a Poisson number of independent events: \(\Pr[k \text{ events}] = \frac{(rt)^k}{k!}e^{-rt}\). In a short interval \(dt\) the probability of one event is \(r\,dt\) and of two or more is \(O(dt^2)\). Rates are not probabilities: they have dimensions of inverse time and can exceed \(1\).

2.4.1 The birth–death master equation

Let \(P_n(t)\) be the probability of having \(n\) individuals at time \(t\). With birth and death rates \[W^+(n) = T(n \to n + 1), \qquad W^-(n) = T(n \to n - 1),\] the probability balance over \(dt\) gives the master equation \[\frac{dP_n}{dt} = W^+(n - 1)P_{n-1} + W^-(n + 1)P_{n+1} - \bigl[W^+(n) + W^-(n)\bigr]P_n ,\] a gain–loss equation: the first two terms bring probability into state \(n\), the last takes it out. More generally, for a process with transition rates \(W_{n'n}\) from \(n\) to \(n'\), \[\frac{dP_n}{dt} = \sum_{n'}\bigl[W_{nn'}P_{n'} - W_{n'n}P_n\bigr].\]

A logistic population with immigration has \[W^+(n) = bn + m, \qquad W^-(n) = d(n)\,n = \frac{b}{K}n^2 .\] Births are multiplicative (\(\propto n\)) plus an immigration rate \(m\) that removes the absorbing state \(n = 0\). Deaths increase with crowding. The deterministic limit, \(\dot{\bar n} = W^+(\bar n) - W^-(\bar n) = b\bar n(1 - \bar n/K) + m\), recovers the logistic law.

2.4.2 Probability current and stationary solutions

Write the one-step master equation as a discrete continuity equation, \(\dot P_n = J_{n-1} - J_n\), with the probability current between \(n\) and \(n + 1\) \[J_n = W^+(n)P_n - W^-(n + 1)P_{n+1}.\] At stationarity all \(J_n\) are equal. With a reflecting boundary at \(n = 0\) (\(W^-(0) = 0\)), \(J_{-1} = 0\), so every current vanishes: \(J_n^* = 0\). This is detailed balance, \(W^+(n)P_n^* = W^-(n + 1)P^*_{n+1}\), which can be iterated: \[P_n^* = P_0^*\prod_{z=1}^{n}\frac{W^+(z - 1)}{W^-(z)}, \qquad P_0^* = \Bigl[1 + \sum_{n\geq1}\prod_{z=1}^n\frac{W^+(z - 1)}{W^-(z)}\Bigr]^{-1}.\] Every one-step process with reflecting boundaries satisfies detailed balance at stationarity. Processes with several variables generally do not, and then stationary states carry non-zero probability currents (a hallmark of non-equilibrium systems, Chapter 15).

For \(W^+(n) = bn + m\), \(W^-(n) = bn^2/K\), \[P^*_n = P^*_0\,\frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{\Gamma(m/b)}\,\frac{K^n}{(n!)^2} \;\underset{m \ll b}{\simeq}\; P^*_0\,\frac{m}{b}\,\frac{K^n}{n\,n!} \quad (n \geq 1).\]

Proof. \(\prod_{z=1}^n W^+(z-1) = b^n\,\Gamma(n + m/b)/\Gamma(m/b)\) and \(\prod_{z=1}^n W^-(z) = (b/K)^n(n!)^2\). For small \(m/b\), \(\Gamma(n + \varepsilon)/\Gamma(\varepsilon) \simeq \varepsilon(n-1)!\). \(\square\)

The distribution \(K^n/(n\,n!)\) is peaked near \(n \simeq K\) with width \(\simeq\sqrt K\). Unlike the deterministic model, the population can exceed the carrying capacity: for \(K = 50\), \(b = 1\), \(m = 0.1\), the mean is \(49.1\), the standard deviation \(7.1\), and \(P(n > K) \simeq 0.41\).

2.4.3 Moments and the failure of mean-field closure

Multiplying the master equation by \(n\) and summing, \[\frac{d\langle n\rangle}{dt} = \langle W^+(n) - W^-(n)\rangle = b\langle n\rangle + m - \frac bK\langle n^2\rangle .\] Because the death rate is nonlinear, the mean depends on the second moment, the second moment on the third, and so on. The deterministic equation is recovered only by the mean-field approximation \(\langle n^2\rangle \simeq \langle n\rangle^2\), which neglects fluctuations, \(\langle n^2\rangle - \langle n\rangle^2 = O(K)\). Their relative size \(O(K^{-1/2})\) vanishes for large systems. This is the first instance of the system-size expansion developed below and in Chapter 5.

2.4.4 The Gillespie algorithm

Master equations are rarely solvable, but they can be sampled exactly. Consider reactions \(j = 1, \dots, M\) with propensities \(a_j(\mathbf x)\) (rates in state \(\mathbf x\)) and state changes \(\mathbf v_j\), and let \(a_0 = \sum_ja_j\). The probability that nothing happens in \([t, t + \tau]\) satisfies \(P_0(\tau + d\tau) = P_0(\tau)(1 - a_0d\tau)\), so \(P_0(\tau) = e^{-a_0\tau}\). The probability that the next event is reaction \(j\), occurring in \([t + \tau, t + \tau + d\tau]\), is therefore \[P(\tau, j)\,d\tau = a_j\,e^{-a_0\tau}\,d\tau = \underbrace{a_0e^{-a_0\tau}d\tau}_{\text{when}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{a_j}{a_0}}_{\text{which}} .\] The waiting time and the identity of the reaction are independent.

  1. Initialise \(\mathbf x(0)\) and \(t = 0\).
  2. Compute all propensities \(a_j(\mathbf x)\) and \(a_0 = \sum_ja_j\).
  3. Draw the waiting time \(\tau \sim \mathrm{Exp}(a_0)\), e.g. \(\tau = -\ln u_1/a_0\) with \(u_1\) uniform on \((0, 1)\).
  4. Draw \(u_2\) uniform and choose the smallest \(j\) with \(\sum_{k\leq j}a_k > u_2a_0\).
  5. Update \(t \to t + \tau\), \(\mathbf x \to \mathbf x + \mathbf v_j\), and return to step 2.

Each trajectory is an exact sample of the process described by the master equation. Averaging many trajectories reconstructs \(P_n(t)\). For the logistic process with \(K = 50\), starting from \(n = 5\), the average of \(300\) trajectories at \(t = 2, 4, 8\) is \(22.0\), \(40.8\), \(48.4\), against the deterministic \(22.5\), \(42.9\), \(49.9\). The stochastic mean lags slightly behind and saturates slightly below \(K\), as predicted by the moment equation (the \(\langle n^2\rangle\) term exceeds \(\langle n\rangle^2\)).

In a population of \(N\) individuals, \(I\) infected and \(S = N - I\) susceptible, infected individuals recover at rate \(\mu\) (\(I \to S\)) and infect susceptibles on contact (\(I + S \xrightarrow{\lambda} 2I\)). The rates are \[W^-(I) = \mu I, \qquad W^+(I) = \lambda\frac{I(N - I)}{N}.\] \(I = 0\) is absorbing, so \(P(I, t) \to \delta_{I,0}\) as \(t \to \infty\). Yet for \(\lambda > \mu\) the deterministic dynamics has an endemic equilibrium \(I^* = N(1 - \mu/\lambda)\). The stochastic system sits there for a time that grows exponentially with \(N\) before fluctuations drive it to extinction: a metastable (quasi-stationary) state, the relevant one in practice.

2.5 From master equation to Fokker–Planck and Langevin

2.5.1 The continuum limit

When populations are large, let \(\Omega\) be a system size (the carrying capacity \(K\), the total population \(N\)) and \(x = n/\Omega\) a density, which becomes continuous as \(\Omega \to \infty\). For rates of the form \(W^\pm(n) = \Omega\,w^\pm(x)\) and \(P_n(t) = P(x, t)/\Omega\), the master equation reads \[\partial_tP(x, t) = \Omega\bigl[(\mathbb E^- - 1)\,w^+(x)P(x, t) + (\mathbb E^+ - 1)\,w^-(x)P(x, t)\bigr],\] with the shift operators \(\mathbb E^\pm f(x) = f(x \pm 1/\Omega)\). Expanding \(\mathbb E^\pm - 1 = \pm\frac1\Omega\partial_x + \frac1{2\Omega^2}\partial_x^2 + \dots\) (Kramers–Moyal expansion) and truncating at second order gives the Fokker–Planck equation \[\partial_tP = -\partial_x\bigl[A(x)P\bigr] + \frac12\partial_x^2\bigl[B(x)P\bigr], \qquad A = w^+ - w^-, \quad B = \frac{w^+ + w^-}{\Omega}.\] The drift \(A\) is the deterministic law. The diffusion \(B\) is small, \(O(1/\Omega)\), and measures demographic noise. Births and deaths both add to it: noise comes from the total event rate, not the net one.

For the stochastic logistic (\(\Omega = K\), \(m = 0\)), \(w^+ = bx\) and \(w^- = bx^2\): \[A(x) = bx(1 - x), \qquad B(x) = \frac bK\,x(1 + x).\] With immigration, \(A = b\bigl[x(1 - x) + \frac m{bK}\bigr]\) and \(B = \frac b{K^2}\bigl[Kx(1 + x) + \frac mb\bigr]\), which no longer vanishes at \(x = 0\): immigration removes the absorbing state.

2.5.2 The Langevin equation

The same process can be described by a stochastic differential equation for a single trajectory, \[\dot x = A(x) + \sqrt{B(x)}\,\xi(t), \qquad \langle\xi(t)\rangle = 0, \quad \langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle = \delta(t - t'),\] whose ensemble of solutions has density \(P(x, t)\) obeying the Fokker–Planck equation (in the Itô interpretation). For the logistic case \[\dot x = bx(1 - x) + \sqrt{\frac{bx(1 + x)}{K}}\;\xi(t).\] The noise amplitude depends on the state: it is multiplicative noise. Numerically, the Euler–Maruyama scheme \[x_{t + \Delta t} = x_t + A(x_t)\Delta t + \sqrt{B(x_t)\,\Delta t}\;\eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),\] evaluates the noise amplitude at the beginning of the step (Itô). Evaluating it at the midpoint gives the Stratonovich interpretation, which corresponds to a different Fokker–Planck equation with an extra “noise-induced drift” \(\frac14B'(x)\). This is the Itô–Stratonovich dilemma: for multiplicative white noise the equation alone is ambiguous, and the interpretation is part of the model. Equations derived from master equations, like ours, are Itô.

2.5.3 Random walk and diffusion

The symmetric random walk, \(W^\pm(n) = \lambda\), is the simplest case. With \(x = n\,\Delta x\), \[\partial_tP(x, t) = \lambda\bigl[P(x - \Delta x) + P(x + \Delta x) - 2P(x)\bigr] \simeq \lambda\,\Delta x^2\,\partial_x^2P,\] the diffusion equation \(\partial_tP = D\,\partial_x^2P\), provided \(\lambda \to \infty\) and \(\Delta x \to 0\) with \(D = \lambda\,\Delta x^2\) fixed. Its Langevin form is \(\dot x = \sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t)\), with Gaussian propagator \(P(x, t) = (4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/4Dt}\).

2.5.4 Stationary solutions of the Fokker–Planck equation

Writing Fokker–Planck as a continuity equation, \(\partial_tP = -\partial_xJ\), with current \(J = AP - \frac12\partial_x(BP)\), a stationary state has constant \(J\). With a reflecting boundary \(J = 0\) everywhere, so \(\partial_x(BP) = 2A\,P\), which integrates to \[P^*(x) = \frac{\mathcal N}{B(x)}\exp\Bigl(2\int^x\frac{A(y)}{B(y)}\,dy\Bigr).\]

For linear rates \(W^+(n) = bn + m\), \(W^-(n) = dn\) with \(d > b\) (decline compensated by immigration), work directly with \(n\): \(A(n) = m - (d - b)n\), \(B(n) \simeq (b + d)n\). Then \[P^*(n) \propto n^{\frac{2m}{b + d} - 1}\,e^{-\frac{2(d - b)}{b + d}\,n},\] a Gamma distribution. The exact discrete solution of the master equation is the negative binomial \[P^*_n = \frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{\Gamma(m/b)\,n!}\Bigl(\frac bd\Bigr)^n\Bigl(1 - \frac bd\Bigr)^{m/b},\] whose continuum limit is a Gamma distribution with shape \(m/b\) and rate \(1 - b/d\). The two agree when \(b \simeq d\), the regime where diffusion is accurate.

Proof. With \(A/B = \frac{m}{(b+d)n} - \frac{d - b}{b + d}\), \(2\int A/B = \frac{2m}{b+d}\ln n - \frac{2(d - b)}{b + d}n\), and dividing by \(B \propto n\) gives the result. The discrete one follows from the product formula with \(W^+(z - 1)/W^-(z) = (b(z-1) + m)/(dz)\). \(\square\)

The negative binomial is the cornerstone of neutral theory (Chapter 3). There, each species follows exactly this linear birth–death–immigration process, and the near-critical case \(b \to d\) produces the log-series of species abundances.

2.6 Stochastic doubling and the timing of division

So far each cell divided at a constant rate. Real E. coli cells grow exponentially in size, \(x(t) = x_0e^{\alpha t}\), and divide at a size \(x_f\) that varies from generation to generation (Osella et al., 2014). Division can be modelled as a random event with a hazard rate \(h_d(x, t)\): the probability of dividing in \([t, t + dt]\), given no division so far, is \(h_d\,dt\).

Let \(P_0(t \mid x_0)\) be the probability that a cell born with size \(x_0\) has not divided by time \(t\) (the survival function). Not dividing up to \(t + dt\) means not dividing up to \(t\) and then not in \([t, t + dt]\): \[P_0(t + dt) = P_0(t)\bigl[1 - h_d(x(t), t)\,dt\bigr] \;\Longrightarrow\; P_0(t \mid x_0) = \exp\Bigl(-\int_0^t h_d(x(s), s)\,ds\Bigr).\] The distribution of division times is then \[P(t \mid x_0) = h_d\bigl(x(t), t\bigr)P_0(t \mid x_0) = -\frac{d}{dt}P_0(t \mid x_0).\] Because size grows monotonically, the same can be written in terms of size, with \(h^*_d(x)\,dx = h_d\,dt\) and \(dx/dt = \alpha x\): \[P(x_f \mid x_0) = h^*_d(x_f)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{x_0}^{x_f}h_d^*(s)\,ds\Bigr).\] The functional form of \(h_d\) distinguishes the classical models of cell-size control:

  • a timer (\(h_d\) depends on time since birth only),
  • a sizer (\(h^*_d\) depends on current size only),
  • an adder (\(h^*_d\) depends on the size added since birth, \(x - x_0\)).

The data favour a “concerted” control, close to the adder, which makes the added size \(x_f - x_0\) independent of \(x_0\) and keeps the size distribution stable over generations. A constant hazard gives back exponentially distributed division times: the Poisson birth process of the master equation.

2.7 Exercises

(HW 1.1) Linear stability. (a) Perform the linear stability analysis of the logistic equation. (b) Do the same for the consumer–resource model with abiotic resource, \(\dot N = (\gamma qR - d)N\), \(\dot R = C - qRN\).

Solution.

  1. \(F(N) = \mu N(1 - N/K)\), \(F'(N) = \mu(1 - 2N/K)\). \(N^* = 0\): \(F' = \mu > 0\), unstable. \(N^* = K\): \(F' = -\mu < 0\), stable, with relaxation time \(1/\mu\).

  2. The interior equilibrium is \(R^* = d/(\gamma q)\), \(N^* = \gamma C/d\). The Jacobian there is \[J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \gamma qN^* \\ -qR^* & -qN^* \end{pmatrix},\] with \(\operatorname{tr}J = -qN^* < 0\) and \(\det J = \gamma q^2R^*N^* = qC > 0\). Both eigenvalues have negative real part, so the equilibrium is stable. It is a stable node if \((\operatorname{tr}J)^2 > 4\det J\), i.e. \(q^2N^{*2} > 4qC\), which reads \(q\gamma^2C/d^2 > 4\). Otherwise it is a stable focus, approached through damped oscillations. With the numbers of HW 1.2, \(q\gamma^2C/d^2 = 0.2\times0.64\times5/0.16 = 4\): the system sits exactly at the boundary.

(HW 1.2) The QSA in the abiotic case. Apply the QSA to the abiotic consumer–resource model with linear uptake. Check the analytical result with simulations, e.g. with \(\gamma = 0.8\), \(q = 0.2\), \(C = 5\), \(d = 0.4\). How does the solution change with Monod uptake (\(q_M = 3\), \(K_M = 8\))?

Solution. QSA: \(\dot R = 0\) gives \(R = C/(qN)\); then \(\dot N = (\gamma qC/(qN) - d)N = \gamma C - dN\), so \(N(t) = N^* + (N_0 - N^*)e^{-dt}\) with \(N^* = \gamma C/d = 10\), and \(R^* = C/(qN^*) = d/(\gamma q) = 2.5\). Simulating the full system from, e.g., \(N_0 = 1\), \(R_0 = 0\), the population relaxes to \(10\) and the resource to \(2.5\). The match improves the faster the resource relaxes (large \(qN\)). With Monod uptake, QSA gives \(g(R)N = C\) and again \(\dot N = \gamma C - dN\): the same \(N^* = 10\). Only the resource level changes, \(R^* = K_Md/(\gamma q_M - d) = 8 \times 0.4/2 = 1.6\). With Monod the QSA fails when \(N < C/q_M = 5/3\): the cells cannot take up all the supply and \(R\) accumulates, so early transients from small \(N_0\) differ from the QSA prediction.

(HW 1.3, optional) Stochastic versus deterministic logistic growth. Simulate the birth–death process with \(W^+ = bn + m\), \(W^- = bn^2/K\) (\(K = 50\), \(b = 1\), \(m = 0.1\)) with the Gillespie algorithm and compare the mean trajectory with the deterministic solution. Compare the long-time histogram with the exact stationary distribution.

Solution. Averaging \(300\) trajectories from \(n_0 = 5\): \(\langle n\rangle = 22.0\), \(40.8\), \(48.4\), \(49.1\) at \(t = 2, 4, 8, 20\), against the deterministic \(22.5\), \(42.9\), \(49.9\), \(50.0\). The stochastic mean is slightly lower because \(\frac{d\langle n\rangle}{dt}\) involves \(\langle n^2\rangle > \langle n\rangle^2\), which enhances death. At long times the histogram matches \(P_n^* \propto \frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{(n!)^2}K^n\): mean \(49.1\), standard deviation \(7.1 \approx \sqrt K\), and \(41\%\) of the time \(n > K\). Individual trajectories fluctuate by \(O(\sqrt K)\) around \(K\), while the deterministic solution sits exactly at \(K\).

(Detailed balance) Show that for any one-step process on \(\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\) with \(W^-(0) = 0\), the stationary currents \(J_n = W^+(n)P_n - W^-(n+1)P_{n+1}\) vanish. Why does this fail for the symmetric random walk on a ring of \(L\) sites with a bias (\(W^+ \neq W^-\))?

Solution. At stationarity \(\dot P_n = J_{n-1} - J_n = 0\), so \(J_n\) is independent of \(n\). At the boundary \(J_{-1} = 0\) (nothing flows below \(0\)), hence all \(J_n = 0\). On a ring there is no boundary: the stationary state is uniform, \(P_n = 1/L\), and carries a constant current \(J = (W^+ - W^-)/L \neq 0\). It is a non-equilibrium steady state, which does not satisfy detailed balance.

(Linear birth–death–immigration) For \(W^+(n) = bn + m\), \(W^-(n) = dn\), \(d > b\): (a) find the stationary distribution from the product formula; (b) compute its mean and compare with the deterministic equilibrium; (c) what happens as \(b \to d\)?

Solution.

  1. \(P_n^* = P_0^*\prod_{z=1}^n\frac{b(z-1) + m}{dz} = P_0^*\frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{\Gamma(m/b)\,n!}\bigl(\frac bd\bigr)^n\), a negative binomial with \(P_0^* = (1 - b/d)^{m/b}\). (b) Its mean is \(\frac{m/b\cdot b/d}{1 - b/d} = \frac{m}{d - b}\), equal to the deterministic equilibrium of \(\dot n = m - (d - b)n\): for linear rates the mean obeys the deterministic equation exactly. (c) As \(b \to d\) the mean diverges and the distribution develops a long tail \(P_n \propto n^{m/b - 1}(b/d)^n\). For \(m/b \to 0\) this is \(\propto x^n/n\), Fisher’s log-series (Chapter 3).

(Fokker–Planck coefficients) For the logistic process with immigration, derive \(A(x)\) and \(B(x)\) by the Kramers–Moyal expansion and write the associated Langevin equation. Show that \(x = 0\) is no longer absorbing.

Solution. With \(x = n/K\): \(w^+(x) = W^+/K = bx + m/K\) and \(w^-(x) = W^-/K = bx^2\). Then \(A = w^+ - w^- = b\bigl(x(1 - x) + \frac m{bK}\bigr)\) and \(B = (w^+ + w^-)/K = \frac b{K^2}\bigl(Kx(1 + x) + \frac mb\bigr)\). The Langevin equation is \(\dot x = A(x) + \sqrt{B(x)}\,\xi\). At \(x = 0\): \(A(0) = m/K > 0\) and \(B(0) = m/K^2 > 0\), so the drift pushes the population away from zero. Without immigration \(A(0) = B(0) = 0\) and extinction is permanent.

(Division with constant hazard) (a) If \(h_d = k\) is constant, find the distribution of division times and its mean. (b) For a sizer with \(h^*_d(x) = \kappa(x - x_c)^2\) for \(x > x_c\) (\(0\) otherwise), and exponential growth from \(x_0 < x_c\), find \(P(x_f \mid x_0)\) and explain why it does not depend on \(x_0\).

Solution.

  1. \(P_0(t) = e^{-kt}\) and \(P(t) = ke^{-kt}\), exponential with mean \(1/k\): memoryless division, as in the birth process. (b) \(P_0(x_f) = \exp\bigl(-\int_{x_c}^{x_f}\kappa(s - x_c)^2ds\bigr) = e^{-\kappa(x_f - x_c)^3/3}\), so \(P(x_f) = \kappa(x_f - x_c)^2e^{-\kappa(x_f - x_c)^3/3}\) for \(x_f > x_c\), a Weibull distribution. Because the hazard is zero below \(x_c\), the birth size only shifts when \(x_c\) is reached, not the distribution of division sizes. This is the defining property of a sizer: the final size carries no memory of the initial one. An adder instead gives \(x_f - x_0\) independent of \(x_0\).

We start from the simplest living system that can be modelled quantitatively: one population of bacteria. The chapter has two halves. The deterministic half derives exponential and logistic growth from a symmetry argument and from an explicit model of resource consumption. The stochastic half replaces the population by a random variable, writes its master equation, and develops the tools used throughout the course: stationary solutions and detailed balance, the Gillespie algorithm, the Fokker–Planck and Langevin limits, and first-passage problems such as the timing of cell division.

## Basic microbiology

A bacterium such as *Escherichia coli* is a rod roughly $2$–$4\,\mu$m long and $0.8\,\mu$m wide. It is enclosed by an inner membrane, a cell wall and (in Gram-negative species) an outer membrane, and it contains a "soup" of DNA, RNA, proteins and small molecules. Nutrients from the surrounding medium cross the membranes and are converted into biomass by **metabolism**. When the cell reaches a critical size it divides into two daughters (binary fission). In rich medium at $37\,^\circ$C, *E. coli* divides every $\tau \approx 20$ minutes.

DNA is a sequence of nucleotides (bases A, C, G, T) organised into **genes**. To a first approximation each gene can be treated as a binary variable, *on* when it is transcribed and its protein produced, *off* otherwise. This picture becomes quantitative in Part III.

## Deterministic growth laws

### Growth laws from scale invariance

Let $N(t)$ be the number of individuals living in an area (or volume) $A$, and suppose the growth rate depends only on these two quantities, $\dot N = F(N, A)$. If we consider $\lambda$ identical, independent copies of the system, the total population must obey the same law:
$$F(\lambda N, \lambda A) = \lambda F(N, A).$$
$F$ is homogeneous of degree one. Choosing $\lambda = 1/A$ gives $F(N, A) = A\,F(N/A, 1) \equiv A f(\rho)$, so the **density** $\rho = N/A$ obeys an autonomous equation
$$\dot\rho = f(\rho).$$
Expanding $f$ for small densities,
$$f(\rho) = f_0 + f_1\rho + \tfrac12 f_2\rho^2 + \dots,$$
each coefficient has a meaning:

- $f_0 > 0$ describes **immigration** into an open system; $f_0 = 0$ for a closed population, which must stay extinct once extinct.
- $f_1 = r$ is the per-capita growth rate at low density. If $f_1 > 0$ the empty state is unstable and the population grows exponentially.
- $f_2 < 0$ describes **self-limitation** (crowding, competition for resources).

Truncating at second order with $f_0 = 0$, $f_1 = r > 0$, $f_2 = -2r/K$ gives the logistic law. It is the *generic* behaviour of a closed population near extinction and near saturation, not an ad hoc guess.

::: {.definition name="Exponential and logistic growth"}
**Exponential growth:** $\dot N = \mu N$, so $N(t) = N_0e^{\mu t} = N_0\,2^{t/T}$, with **doubling time** $T = \ln 2/\mu$. For *E. coli* in optimal conditions $\mu \simeq 2.1\,\mathrm h^{-1}$, so $T \simeq 20$ min.

**Logistic growth** (Verhulst, 1838): with **carrying capacity** $K$,
$$\dot N = \mu N\Bigl(1 - \frac{N}{K}\Bigr), \qquad N(t) = \frac{N_0e^{\mu t}}{1 + \frac{N_0}{K}\bigl(e^{\mu t} - 1\bigr)} .$$
:::

The solution follows by separation of variables, $\int \frac{dN}{N(1 - N/K)} = \mu t$, and partial fractions. A bacterial batch culture fits it well: an exponential phase while $N \ll K$, a saturation (stationary) phase as $N \to K$. A real culture also has an initial *lag* phase, during which cells adapt to the new medium.

### Linear stability

An equilibrium $N^*$ of $\dot N = F(N)$ is stable if small perturbations decay. Writing $N = N^* + \delta$ and expanding, $\dot\delta = F'(N^*)\delta$, so $N^*$ is stable if $F'(N^*) < 0$, with relaxation time $1/|F'(N^*)|$. For the logistic law $F'(N) = \mu(1 - 2N/K)$: $N^* = 0$ has $F'(0) = \mu > 0$ (unstable) and $N^* = K$ has $F'(K) = -\mu$ (stable, relaxation time $1/\mu$).

For systems of equations $\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf F(\mathbf x)$, the role of $F'$ is played by the **Jacobian** $J_{ij} = \partial F_i/\partial x_j$ at the equilibrium. The equilibrium is stable iff all its eigenvalues have negative real part. This criterion will be the starting point of the random-matrix analysis of Chapter 6.

## Consumer–resource models

The carrying capacity hides the resource. To make it explicit, let $R(t)$ be the amount of resource, $g(R)$ the uptake rate per cell, $\mu(R)$ the resource's own supply or growth, $\gamma$ the **yield** (cells produced per unit of resource), $c$ an uptake efficiency (set to $1$ below) and $d$ the death rate:
$$\dot N = \bigl(\gamma c\,g(R) - d\bigr)N, \qquad \dot R = \mu(R) - c\,g(R)\,N .$$
Common uptake functions are linear, $g(R) = qR$ (mass action), and saturating, the **Monod** law
$$g(R) = \frac{q_M R}{K_M + R},$$
which states that a cell cannot process resource faster than $q_M$. $K_M$ is the concentration at which uptake is half-maximal.

::: {.definition name="Quasi-stationary approximation (QSA)"}
If the resource relaxes much faster than the population, then on the population's time scale $R$ is always at its instantaneous equilibrium, found by setting $\dot R = 0$ at fixed $N$. Substituting it into $\dot N$ gives a closed equation for $N$ alone. This is adiabatic elimination of a fast variable, and it is used throughout physics and biochemistry (Michaelis–Menten kinetics is the same idea).
:::

**Biotic resource (MacArthur).** If the resource is itself a living population with logistic growth, $\mu(R) = r^*R(1 - R/k_c)$, and uptake is linear, then
$$\dot N = N(\gamma cR - d), \qquad \dot R = R\Bigl[r^*\Bigl(1 - \frac{R}{k_c}\Bigr) - cN\Bigr].$$
The QSA with $R \neq 0$ gives $R^* = k_c\bigl(1 - cN/r^*\bigr)$, and substituting,
$$\dot N = N\bigl(\tilde k - \tilde a N\bigr), \qquad \tilde k = \gamma ck_c - d, \quad \tilde a = \frac{\gamma c^2k_c}{r^*},$$
a logistic law with growth rate $\mu = \tilde k$ and carrying capacity $K = \tilde k/\tilde a$. The carrying capacity is now *derived* from the resource's parameters. The population survives only if $\gamma ck_c > d$: the resource must be able to sustain more births than deaths.

**Abiotic resource.** If the resource is supplied at a constant rate, $\mu(R) = C$ (a chemostat), the QSA gives $g(R)N = C$ and then
$$\dot N = \gamma C - dN, \qquad N^* = \frac{\gamma C}{d},$$
whatever the uptake function: the population relaxes *linearly* to $N^*$ with time constant $1/d$, not logistically. The uptake function only fixes the resource level at equilibrium, through $\gamma g(R^*) = d$:
$$R^*_{\text{linear}} = \frac{d}{\gamma q}, \qquad R^*_{\text{Monod}} = \frac{K_Md}{\gamma q_M - d}.$$
With Monod uptake a population can persist only if $\gamma q_M > d$, i.e. if its maximal growth beats death. The QSA also requires $C/N < q_M$: a small population cannot consume all the supplied resource, which then accumulates.

## Stochastic population dynamics

Populations are made of discrete individuals, and births and deaths are random events. Deterministic equations describe the *mean* behaviour of large populations. Near extinction, or in small populations, **demographic noise** dominates.

::: {.callout-note title="Rates and probabilities"}
A process occurring at rate $r$ produces, in a time $t$, a Poisson number of independent events: $\Pr[k \text{ events}] = \frac{(rt)^k}{k!}e^{-rt}$. In a short interval $dt$ the probability of one event is $r\,dt$ and of two or more is $O(dt^2)$. Rates are not probabilities: they have dimensions of inverse time and can exceed $1$.
:::

### The birth–death master equation

Let $P_n(t)$ be the probability of having $n$ individuals at time $t$. With birth and death rates
$$W^+(n) = T(n \to n + 1), \qquad W^-(n) = T(n \to n - 1),$$
the probability balance over $dt$ gives the **master equation**
$$\frac{dP_n}{dt} = W^+(n - 1)P_{n-1} + W^-(n + 1)P_{n+1} - \bigl[W^+(n) + W^-(n)\bigr]P_n ,$$
a gain–loss equation: the first two terms bring probability into state $n$, the last takes it out. More generally, for a process with transition rates $W_{n'n}$ from $n$ to $n'$,
$$\frac{dP_n}{dt} = \sum_{n'}\bigl[W_{nn'}P_{n'} - W_{n'n}P_n\bigr].$$

A logistic population with immigration has
$$W^+(n) = bn + m, \qquad W^-(n) = d(n)\,n = \frac{b}{K}n^2 .$$
Births are multiplicative ($\propto n$) plus an immigration rate $m$ that removes the absorbing state $n = 0$. Deaths increase with crowding. The deterministic limit, $\dot{\bar n} = W^+(\bar n) - W^-(\bar n) = b\bar n(1 - \bar n/K) + m$, recovers the logistic law.

### Probability current and stationary solutions

Write the one-step master equation as a discrete continuity equation, $\dot P_n = J_{n-1} - J_n$, with the **probability current** between $n$ and $n + 1$
$$J_n = W^+(n)P_n - W^-(n + 1)P_{n+1}.$$
At stationarity all $J_n$ are equal. With a reflecting boundary at $n = 0$ ($W^-(0) = 0$), $J_{-1} = 0$, so every current vanishes: $J_n^* = 0$. This is **detailed balance**, $W^+(n)P_n^* = W^-(n + 1)P^*_{n+1}$, which can be iterated:
$$P_n^* = P_0^*\prod_{z=1}^{n}\frac{W^+(z - 1)}{W^-(z)}, \qquad P_0^* = \Bigl[1 + \sum_{n\geq1}\prod_{z=1}^n\frac{W^+(z - 1)}{W^-(z)}\Bigr]^{-1}.$$
Every one-step process with reflecting boundaries satisfies detailed balance at stationarity. Processes with several variables generally do not, and then stationary states carry non-zero probability currents (a hallmark of non-equilibrium systems, Chapter 15).

::: {.proposition name="Stationary stochastic logistic"}
For $W^+(n) = bn + m$, $W^-(n) = bn^2/K$,
$$P^*_n = P^*_0\,\frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{\Gamma(m/b)}\,\frac{K^n}{(n!)^2} \;\underset{m \ll b}{\simeq}\; P^*_0\,\frac{m}{b}\,\frac{K^n}{n\,n!} \quad (n \geq 1).$$
:::

*Proof.* $\prod_{z=1}^n W^+(z-1) = b^n\,\Gamma(n + m/b)/\Gamma(m/b)$ and $\prod_{z=1}^n W^-(z) = (b/K)^n(n!)^2$. For small $m/b$, $\Gamma(n + \varepsilon)/\Gamma(\varepsilon) \simeq \varepsilon(n-1)!$. $\square$

The distribution $K^n/(n\,n!)$ is peaked near $n \simeq K$ with width $\simeq\sqrt K$. Unlike the deterministic model, the population can exceed the carrying capacity: for $K = 50$, $b = 1$, $m = 0.1$, the mean is $49.1$, the standard deviation $7.1$, and $P(n > K) \simeq 0.41$.

### Moments and the failure of mean-field closure

Multiplying the master equation by $n$ and summing,
$$\frac{d\langle n\rangle}{dt} = \langle W^+(n) - W^-(n)\rangle = b\langle n\rangle + m - \frac bK\langle n^2\rangle .$$
Because the death rate is nonlinear, the mean depends on the second moment, the second moment on the third, and so on. The deterministic equation is recovered only by the **mean-field** approximation $\langle n^2\rangle \simeq \langle n\rangle^2$, which neglects fluctuations, $\langle n^2\rangle - \langle n\rangle^2 = O(K)$. Their relative size $O(K^{-1/2})$ vanishes for large systems. This is the first instance of the **system-size expansion** developed below and in Chapter 5.

### The Gillespie algorithm

Master equations are rarely solvable, but they can be sampled exactly. Consider reactions $j = 1, \dots, M$ with propensities $a_j(\mathbf x)$ (rates in state $\mathbf x$) and state changes $\mathbf v_j$, and let $a_0 = \sum_ja_j$. The probability that nothing happens in $[t, t + \tau]$ satisfies $P_0(\tau + d\tau) = P_0(\tau)(1 - a_0d\tau)$, so $P_0(\tau) = e^{-a_0\tau}$. The probability that the next event is reaction $j$, occurring in $[t + \tau, t + \tau + d\tau]$, is therefore
$$P(\tau, j)\,d\tau = a_j\,e^{-a_0\tau}\,d\tau = \underbrace{a_0e^{-a_0\tau}d\tau}_{\text{when}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{a_j}{a_0}}_{\text{which}} .$$
The waiting time and the identity of the reaction are independent.

::: {.definition name="Gillespie's stochastic simulation algorithm"}
1. Initialise $\mathbf x(0)$ and $t = 0$.
2. Compute all propensities $a_j(\mathbf x)$ and $a_0 = \sum_ja_j$.
3. Draw the waiting time $\tau \sim \mathrm{Exp}(a_0)$, e.g. $\tau = -\ln u_1/a_0$ with $u_1$ uniform on $(0, 1)$.
4. Draw $u_2$ uniform and choose the smallest $j$ with $\sum_{k\leq j}a_k > u_2a_0$.
5. Update $t \to t + \tau$, $\mathbf x \to \mathbf x + \mathbf v_j$, and return to step 2.
:::

Each trajectory is an exact sample of the process described by the master equation. Averaging many trajectories reconstructs $P_n(t)$. For the logistic process with $K = 50$, starting from $n = 5$, the average of $300$ trajectories at $t = 2, 4, 8$ is $22.0$, $40.8$, $48.4$, against the deterministic $22.5$, $42.9$, $49.9$. The stochastic mean lags slightly behind and saturates slightly below $K$, as predicted by the moment equation (the $\langle n^2\rangle$ term exceeds $\langle n\rangle^2$).

::: {.example name="SIS epidemic"}
In a population of $N$ individuals, $I$ infected and $S = N - I$ susceptible, infected individuals recover at rate $\mu$ ($I \to S$) and infect susceptibles on contact ($I + S \xrightarrow{\lambda} 2I$). The rates are
$$W^-(I) = \mu I, \qquad W^+(I) = \lambda\frac{I(N - I)}{N}.$$
$I = 0$ is absorbing, so $P(I, t) \to \delta_{I,0}$ as $t \to \infty$. Yet for $\lambda > \mu$ the deterministic dynamics has an endemic equilibrium $I^* = N(1 - \mu/\lambda)$. The stochastic system sits there for a time that grows exponentially with $N$ before fluctuations drive it to extinction: a **metastable** (quasi-stationary) state, the relevant one in practice.
:::

## From master equation to Fokker–Planck and Langevin

### The continuum limit

When populations are large, let $\Omega$ be a system size (the carrying capacity $K$, the total population $N$) and $x = n/\Omega$ a density, which becomes continuous as $\Omega \to \infty$. For rates of the form $W^\pm(n) = \Omega\,w^\pm(x)$ and $P_n(t) = P(x, t)/\Omega$, the master equation reads
$$\partial_tP(x, t) = \Omega\bigl[(\mathbb E^- - 1)\,w^+(x)P(x, t) + (\mathbb E^+ - 1)\,w^-(x)P(x, t)\bigr],$$
with the shift operators $\mathbb E^\pm f(x) = f(x \pm 1/\Omega)$. Expanding $\mathbb E^\pm - 1 = \pm\frac1\Omega\partial_x + \frac1{2\Omega^2}\partial_x^2 + \dots$ (Kramers–Moyal expansion) and truncating at second order gives the **Fokker–Planck equation**
$$\partial_tP = -\partial_x\bigl[A(x)P\bigr] + \frac12\partial_x^2\bigl[B(x)P\bigr], \qquad A = w^+ - w^-, \quad B = \frac{w^+ + w^-}{\Omega}.$$
The **drift** $A$ is the deterministic law. The **diffusion** $B$ is small, $O(1/\Omega)$, and measures demographic noise. Births and deaths both *add* to it: noise comes from the total event rate, not the net one.

For the stochastic logistic ($\Omega = K$, $m = 0$), $w^+ = bx$ and $w^- = bx^2$:
$$A(x) = bx(1 - x), \qquad B(x) = \frac bK\,x(1 + x).$$
With immigration, $A = b\bigl[x(1 - x) + \frac m{bK}\bigr]$ and $B = \frac b{K^2}\bigl[Kx(1 + x) + \frac mb\bigr]$, which no longer vanishes at $x = 0$: immigration removes the absorbing state.

### The Langevin equation

The same process can be described by a stochastic differential equation for a single trajectory,
$$\dot x = A(x) + \sqrt{B(x)}\,\xi(t), \qquad \langle\xi(t)\rangle = 0, \quad \langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle = \delta(t - t'),$$
whose ensemble of solutions has density $P(x, t)$ obeying the Fokker–Planck equation (in the Itô interpretation). For the logistic case
$$\dot x = bx(1 - x) + \sqrt{\frac{bx(1 + x)}{K}}\;\xi(t).$$
The noise amplitude depends on the state: it is **multiplicative noise**. Numerically, the Euler–Maruyama scheme
$$x_{t + \Delta t} = x_t + A(x_t)\Delta t + \sqrt{B(x_t)\,\Delta t}\;\eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),$$
evaluates the noise amplitude at the beginning of the step (Itô). Evaluating it at the midpoint gives the Stratonovich interpretation, which corresponds to a *different* Fokker–Planck equation with an extra "noise-induced drift" $\frac14B'(x)$. This is the **Itô–Stratonovich dilemma**: for multiplicative white noise the equation alone is ambiguous, and the interpretation is part of the model. Equations derived from master equations, like ours, are Itô.

### Random walk and diffusion

The symmetric random walk, $W^\pm(n) = \lambda$, is the simplest case. With $x = n\,\Delta x$,
$$\partial_tP(x, t) = \lambda\bigl[P(x - \Delta x) + P(x + \Delta x) - 2P(x)\bigr] \simeq \lambda\,\Delta x^2\,\partial_x^2P,$$
the **diffusion equation** $\partial_tP = D\,\partial_x^2P$, provided $\lambda \to \infty$ and $\Delta x \to 0$ with $D = \lambda\,\Delta x^2$ fixed. Its Langevin form is $\dot x = \sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t)$, with Gaussian propagator $P(x, t) = (4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/4Dt}$.

### Stationary solutions of the Fokker–Planck equation

Writing Fokker–Planck as a continuity equation, $\partial_tP = -\partial_xJ$, with current $J = AP - \frac12\partial_x(BP)$, a stationary state has constant $J$. With a reflecting boundary $J = 0$ everywhere, so $\partial_x(BP) = 2A\,P$, which integrates to
$$P^*(x) = \frac{\mathcal N}{B(x)}\exp\Bigl(2\int^x\frac{A(y)}{B(y)}\,dy\Bigr).$$

::: {.proposition name="Linear birth and death: a Gamma distribution"}
For linear rates $W^+(n) = bn + m$, $W^-(n) = dn$ with $d > b$ (decline compensated by immigration), work directly with $n$: $A(n) = m - (d - b)n$, $B(n) \simeq (b + d)n$. Then
$$P^*(n) \propto n^{\frac{2m}{b + d} - 1}\,e^{-\frac{2(d - b)}{b + d}\,n},$$
a **Gamma distribution**. The exact discrete solution of the master equation is the **negative binomial**
$$P^*_n = \frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{\Gamma(m/b)\,n!}\Bigl(\frac bd\Bigr)^n\Bigl(1 - \frac bd\Bigr)^{m/b},$$
whose continuum limit is a Gamma distribution with shape $m/b$ and rate $1 - b/d$. The two agree when $b \simeq d$, the regime where diffusion is accurate.
:::

*Proof.* With $A/B = \frac{m}{(b+d)n} - \frac{d - b}{b + d}$, $2\int A/B = \frac{2m}{b+d}\ln n - \frac{2(d - b)}{b + d}n$, and dividing by $B \propto n$ gives the result. The discrete one follows from the product formula with $W^+(z - 1)/W^-(z) = (b(z-1) + m)/(dz)$. $\square$

The negative binomial is the cornerstone of neutral theory (Chapter 3). There, each species follows exactly this linear birth–death–immigration process, and the near-critical case $b \to d$ produces the log-series of species abundances.

## Stochastic doubling and the timing of division

So far each cell divided at a constant rate. Real *E. coli* cells grow exponentially in size, $x(t) = x_0e^{\alpha t}$, and divide at a size $x_f$ that varies from generation to generation (Osella et al., 2014). Division can be modelled as a random event with a **hazard rate** $h_d(x, t)$: the probability of dividing in $[t, t + dt]$, given no division so far, is $h_d\,dt$.

Let $P_0(t \mid x_0)$ be the probability that a cell born with size $x_0$ has not divided by time $t$ (the *survival* function). Not dividing up to $t + dt$ means not dividing up to $t$ and then not in $[t, t + dt]$:
$$P_0(t + dt) = P_0(t)\bigl[1 - h_d(x(t), t)\,dt\bigr] \;\Longrightarrow\; P_0(t \mid x_0) = \exp\Bigl(-\int_0^t h_d(x(s), s)\,ds\Bigr).$$
The distribution of division times is then
$$P(t \mid x_0) = h_d\bigl(x(t), t\bigr)P_0(t \mid x_0) = -\frac{d}{dt}P_0(t \mid x_0).$$
Because size grows monotonically, the same can be written in terms of size, with $h^*_d(x)\,dx = h_d\,dt$ and $dx/dt = \alpha x$:
$$P(x_f \mid x_0) = h^*_d(x_f)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{x_0}^{x_f}h_d^*(s)\,ds\Bigr).$$
The functional form of $h_d$ distinguishes the classical models of cell-size control:

- a **timer** ($h_d$ depends on time since birth only),
- a **sizer** ($h^*_d$ depends on current size only),
- an **adder** ($h^*_d$ depends on the size *added* since birth, $x - x_0$).

The data favour a "concerted" control, close to the adder, which makes the added size $x_f - x_0$ independent of $x_0$ and keeps the size distribution stable over generations. A constant hazard gives back exponentially distributed division times: the Poisson birth process of the master equation.

**(HW 1.1) Linear stability.** (a) Perform the linear stability analysis of the logistic equation. (b) Do the same for the consumer–resource model with abiotic resource, $\dot N = (\gamma qR - d)N$, $\dot R = C - qRN$.

::: {.solution}
(a) $F(N) = \mu N(1 - N/K)$, $F'(N) = \mu(1 - 2N/K)$. $N^* = 0$: $F' = \mu > 0$, unstable. $N^* = K$: $F' = -\mu < 0$, stable, with relaxation time $1/\mu$.

(b) The interior equilibrium is $R^* = d/(\gamma q)$, $N^* = \gamma C/d$. The Jacobian there is
$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \gamma qN^* \\ -qR^* & -qN^* \end{pmatrix},$$
with $\operatorname{tr}J = -qN^* < 0$ and $\det J = \gamma q^2R^*N^* = qC > 0$. Both eigenvalues have negative real part, so the equilibrium is stable. It is a stable node if $(\operatorname{tr}J)^2 > 4\det J$, i.e. $q^2N^{*2} > 4qC$, which reads $q\gamma^2C/d^2 > 4$. Otherwise it is a stable focus, approached through damped oscillations. With the numbers of HW 1.2, $q\gamma^2C/d^2 = 0.2\times0.64\times5/0.16 = 4$: the system sits exactly at the boundary.

**(HW 1.2) The QSA in the abiotic case.** Apply the QSA to the abiotic consumer–resource model with linear uptake. Check the analytical result with simulations, e.g. with $\gamma = 0.8$, $q = 0.2$, $C = 5$, $d = 0.4$. How does the solution change with Monod uptake ($q_M = 3$, $K_M = 8$)?

::: {.exercise}
**(Detailed balance)** Show that for any one-step process on $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ with $W^-(0) = 0$, the stationary currents $J_n = W^+(n)P_n - W^-(n+1)P_{n+1}$ vanish. Why does this fail for the symmetric random walk on a ring of $L$ sites with a bias ($W^+ \neq W^-$)?
:::

::: {.solution}
At stationarity $\dot P_n = J_{n-1} - J_n = 0$, so $J_n$ is independent of $n$. At the boundary $J_{-1} = 0$ (nothing flows below $0$), hence all $J_n = 0$. On a ring there is no boundary: the stationary state is uniform, $P_n = 1/L$, and carries a constant current $J = (W^+ - W^-)/L \neq 0$. It is a non-equilibrium steady state, which does not satisfy detailed balance.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Linear birth–death–immigration)** For $W^+(n) = bn + m$, $W^-(n) = dn$, $d > b$: (a) find the stationary distribution from the product formula; (b) compute its mean and compare with the deterministic equilibrium; (c) what happens as $b \to d$?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $P_n^* = P_0^*\prod_{z=1}^n\frac{b(z-1) + m}{dz} = P_0^*\frac{\Gamma(n + m/b)}{\Gamma(m/b)\,n!}\bigl(\frac bd\bigr)^n$, a negative binomial with $P_0^* = (1 - b/d)^{m/b}$. (b) Its mean is $\frac{m/b\cdot b/d}{1 - b/d} = \frac{m}{d - b}$, equal to the deterministic equilibrium of $\dot n = m - (d - b)n$: for linear rates the mean obeys the deterministic equation exactly. (c) As $b \to d$ the mean diverges and the distribution develops a long tail $P_n \propto n^{m/b - 1}(b/d)^n$. For $m/b \to 0$ this is $\propto x^n/n$, Fisher's log-series (Chapter 3).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Fokker–Planck coefficients)** For the logistic process with immigration, derive $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ by the Kramers–Moyal expansion and write the associated Langevin equation. Show that $x = 0$ is no longer absorbing.
:::

::: {.solution}
With $x = n/K$: $w^+(x) = W^+/K = bx + m/K$ and $w^-(x) = W^-/K = bx^2$. Then $A = w^+ - w^- = b\bigl(x(1 - x) + \frac m{bK}\bigr)$ and $B = (w^+ + w^-)/K = \frac b{K^2}\bigl(Kx(1 + x) + \frac mb\bigr)$. The Langevin equation is $\dot x = A(x) + \sqrt{B(x)}\,\xi$. At $x = 0$: $A(0) = m/K > 0$ and $B(0) = m/K^2 > 0$, so the drift pushes the population away from zero. Without immigration $A(0) = B(0) = 0$ and extinction is permanent.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Division with constant hazard)** (a) If $h_d = k$ is constant, find the distribution of division times and its mean. (b) For a sizer with $h^*_d(x) = \kappa(x - x_c)^2$ for $x > x_c$ ($0$ otherwise), and exponential growth from $x_0 < x_c$, find $P(x_f \mid x_0)$ and explain why it does not depend on $x_0$.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $P_0(t) = e^{-kt}$ and $P(t) = ke^{-kt}$, exponential with mean $1/k$: memoryless division, as in the birth process. (b) $P_0(x_f) = \exp\bigl(-\int_{x_c}^{x_f}\kappa(s - x_c)^2ds\bigr) = e^{-\kappa(x_f - x_c)^3/3}$, so $P(x_f) = \kappa(x_f - x_c)^2e^{-\kappa(x_f - x_c)^3/3}$ for $x_f > x_c$, a Weibull distribution. Because the hazard is zero below $x_c$, the birth size only shifts *when* $x_c$ is reached, not the distribution of division *sizes*. This is the defining property of a sizer: the final size carries no memory of the initial one. An adder instead gives $x_f - x_0$ independent of $x_0$.
:::
