8  Firing-rate networks

We now switch scale: from populations of species to populations of neurons. The ecological toolkit — mean-field ODEs, master equations, mean-field random matrices — carries over almost unchanged, and the first stop is the firing-rate model, the simplest description of neural population activity.

8.1 Rate versus spike models

Individual neurons emit action potentials — stereotyped voltage pulses of \(\sim 1\) ms duration. When many neurons fire the informative signal is not the timing of each spike but the rate \(\phi(t)\) at which spikes are emitted in a short window. Firing-rate models replace the discrete spike train by this continuous rate.

The firing rate of a neuron is \[\phi(t) = \frac{\text{number of spikes in a window } [t - \Delta/2,\, t + \Delta/2]}{\Delta},\] in the limit \(\Delta \to 0\) after ensemble-averaging over trials. For a stationary point process \(\phi(t) = \lambda\) is the intensity of the spike train.

Remark. For most biophysical purposes the rate description is valid whenever the network activity varies on timescales \(\gg 1/\lambda\). Below that scale (single-spike coincidence coding) rate models fail and one must go back to spike-timing models such as leaky integrate-and-fire.

8.2 Excitatory–inhibitory populations

Even before we introduce networks, the effective single-population equation is instructive. Let \(\phi(t)\) be the mean rate of a homogeneous population; the deterministic rate equation is \[\tau\,\dot \phi = -\phi + F(I),\] where \(I\) is the input current and \(F\) is a monotone gain function (the transfer function), typically sigmoidal.

The next level of realism separates the population into excitatory (\(E\)) and inhibitory (\(I\)) sub-populations that interact via signed synapses:

The Wilson–Cowan equations for the mean firing rates of coupled excitatory and inhibitory populations are \[\begin{aligned} \tau_E\,\dot\phi_E &= -\phi_E + F_E\bigl(W_{EE}\phi_E - W_{EI}\phi_I + I_E^{ext}\bigr),\\ \tau_I\,\dot\phi_I &= -\phi_I + F_I\bigl(W_{IE}\phi_E - W_{II}\phi_I + I_I^{ext}\bigr). \end{aligned}\] The four constants \(W_{XY} > 0\) encode the average synaptic weights between populations; \(F_{E,I}\) are sigmoid transfer functions saturating at some maximum rate.

The Wilson–Cowan system exhibits three qualitatively different regimes: (i) a single stable low-activity fixed point (rest state); (ii) two stable fixed points separated by a saddle (bistability, working memory); (iii) a Hopf-bifurcated limit cycle (oscillation). The transitions occur along a cusp in the \((I_E, I_I)\) external-input plane.

Wilson–Cowan networks with excitatory time constant \(\tau_E \sim 5\) ms and inhibitory \(\tau_I \sim 10\) ms show a Hopf bifurcation to oscillations at \(\sim 40\) Hz — the gamma rhythm implicated in perceptual binding and attention. The oscillation frequency depends on the lag between E and I responses, not on any individual neuron’s intrinsic frequency.

8.3 Recurrent networks

A more microscopic description replaces the two lumped populations by \(N\) individual rate units: \[\tau\,\dot\phi_i = -\phi_i + F\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N} W_{ij}\phi_j + I_i^{ext}\Bigr), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, N.\] When \(W\) is a full matrix this is a recurrent rate network — the object of the rest of the chapter.

8.4 Linear recurrent networks

Suppose \(F\) is linear, \(F(x) = x\). Then the dynamics is \[\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol\phi} = (W - I)\boldsymbol\phi + \mathbf I^{ext} .\] The behaviour is governed by the spectrum of \(W\):

The linear rate network is stable if and only if the leading eigenvalue of \(W\) has real part less than \(1\). If \(W\) is drawn from the May ensemble of Chapter 4 with parameters \((N, c, \sigma)\), the leading eigenvalue is \(\sigma\sqrt{cN}\); the network is stable iff \(\sigma\sqrt{cN} < 1\), exactly the ecological threshold.

The equivalence between May’s stability criterion and the transition to chaos in random recurrent networks is one of the most striking cross-domain results in the notes: Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers (1988) proved that a nonlinear random rate network transitions from a fixed point to deterministic chaos exactly at \(\sigma\sqrt{N} = 1\).

For a nonlinear rate network with \(W_{ij}\) i.i.d. Gaussian of variance \(\sigma^2/N\) and gain function \(\phi = \tanh(x)\), the fixed point at zero loses stability at \(\sigma = 1\). For \(\sigma > 1\) the dynamics is deterministically chaotic, with a positive Lyapunov exponent that grows continuously from zero.

8.5 Stochastic rate networks

Adding noise to the rate equation gives a Langevin picture: \[d\phi_i = \tau^{-1}\bigl(-\phi_i + F(\sum_j W_{ij}\phi_j)\bigr)\,dt + \sigma_\eta\,dW_i(t).\] For a linear \(F\) the process is Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, and its power spectrum is \[S(\omega) = \frac{2\sigma_\eta^2\,\tau^2}{|(W - I)\tau + i\omega\tau I|^2 + \omega^2\tau^2},\] generalising the stochastic-Volterra result of Chapter 4.

Near the stability boundary \(\sigma\sqrt{cN} = 1\), the linear stochastic rate network shows avalanches of bursty activity whose size distribution follows a power law \(P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}\), characteristic of a critical branching process. This has been proposed as a mechanism for the empirically observed avalanche statistics in cortical recordings.

8.6 Exercises

Derive the mean-field fixed point of a Wilson–Cowan network with symmetric parameters \(\tau_E = \tau_I\), \(W_{EE} = W_{II} = 0\), \(W_{EI} = W_{IE} = w > 0\), and no external input. When does a Hopf bifurcation occur as \(w\) is increased?

Solution. The fixed points satisfy \(\phi_E = F(-w\phi_I)\) and \(\phi_I = F(w\phi_E)\). If \(F\) is odd, \(\phi_E = -\phi_I\) is a solution which, for \(F(x) = \tanh(x)\), gives \(\phi_E = \tanh(w\phi_E)\): only \(\phi_E = 0\) if \(w < 1\); two symmetric nontrivial roots for \(w > 1\). Linearising around \((0,0)\): eigenvalues of the Jacobian are \(\pm i w F'(0)\), so at \(w F'(0) = 1\) we have a pure imaginary pair — the Hopf point. For \(w > w_c\) a limit cycle grows out of it.

Show that the transition to chaos in the Sompolinsky–Crisanti–Sommers network can be understood as a growth of the largest Lyapunov exponent from zero. Use the dynamic mean-field theory to write a self-consistent equation for the autocorrelation \(C(t) = \langle \phi_i(t')\phi_i(t' + t)\rangle\).

Solution. The dynamic mean-field ansatz replaces the recurrent input to any neuron by a Gaussian field with the same auto-covariance. Writing \(\phi_i(t) = F(x_i(t))\) with \(\langle x_i(t) x_i(t')\rangle = \sigma^2 C_\phi(t - t')\), self-consistency gives \[C_\phi(\tau) = \int \tanh(\sqrt{\sigma^2 C_\phi(0)}\,z_1)\tanh(\sqrt{\sigma^2 C_\phi(0)}\,z_2)\,d\mathcal N,\] which is a fixed-point equation for the correlation function. Its trivial solution \(C_\phi \equiv 0\) becomes unstable at \(\sigma = 1\); a nontrivial oscillating solution grows continuously above threshold, matching the Lyapunov diagnosis.

8.7 Callouts

Firing-rate models describe populations, not single neurons. Predictions such as gamma oscillations or avalanches are about ensembles; single-neuron voltage traces look nothing like their solutions.

The single most useful check on any recurrent-network simulation is the eigenvalue spectrum of \(W\): if the leading eigenvalue’s real part exceeds one, the linear part is already unstable and the dynamics you are seeing is dominated by the nonlinearity, not by the network you intended to model.

The result “power-law avalanches with exponent \(-3/2\)” appears in many neuroscience datasets. It is not by itself evidence of criticality: a wide variety of non-critical processes produce the same exponent.

## Recurrent networks

A more microscopic description replaces the two lumped populations by $N$ individual rate units:
$$\tau\,\dot\phi_i = -\phi_i + F\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N} W_{ij}\phi_j + I_i^{ext}\Bigr), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, N.$$
When $W$ is a full matrix this is a *recurrent* rate network — the object of the rest of the chapter.

## Linear recurrent networks

Suppose $F$ is linear, $F(x) = x$. Then the dynamics is
$$\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol\phi} = (W - I)\boldsymbol\phi + \mathbf I^{ext} .$$
The behaviour is governed by the spectrum of $W$:

::: {.proposition name="Stability of linear recurrent nets"}
The linear rate network is stable if and only if the leading eigenvalue of $W$ has real part less than $1$. If $W$ is drawn from the May ensemble of Chapter 4 with parameters $(N, c, \sigma)$, the leading eigenvalue is $\sigma\sqrt{cN}$; the network is stable iff $\sigma\sqrt{cN} < 1$, exactly the ecological threshold.

::: {.theorem name="Sompolinsky–Crisanti–Sommers transition"}

:::
