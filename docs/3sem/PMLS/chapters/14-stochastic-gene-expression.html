14  The cell and stochastic gene expression

The third module moves inside the cell. Genes are the units, proteins and mRNA are the “populations”, and transcription factors play the role of interactions. Molecule numbers are small (a few to a few thousand copies per cell), so noise is not a correction but a defining feature: genetically identical cells in identical environments express genes at very different levels. This chapter introduces the molecular machinery of gene expression and solves the two classic stochastic models: the one-stage (birth–death) model, which gives Poisson statistics, and the two-stage (mRNA–protein) model, which gives protein bursts and a negative binomial distribution, directly comparable with single-cell data.

14.1 The cell and the central dogma

A cell is a crowded, compartmentalised reactor. Its genome (about \(4.6\) Mb in E. coli, \(3\) Gb in humans) encodes genes that are transcribed by RNA polymerase into messenger RNA (mRNA), which is translated by ribosomes into proteins. Each codon of three nucleotides (\(4^3 = 64\) possibilities) codes for one of \(20\) amino acids, plus stop signals. A protein is a chain of \(50\)–\(1000\) amino acids that folds into a specific three-dimensional structure (Chapter 18). The folding is driven by the free energy \(F = E - TS\): hydrophobic residues hide in the core, and the folded state has low energy but also low entropy.

Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that bind specific DNA sequences (operators) near a gene’s promoter and activate or repress its transcription. Gene expression is therefore regulated by the products of other genes, forming gene regulatory networks (Chapters 16–17).

Typical numbers for E. coli show why noise matters:

quantity value
cell volume \(\sim 1\,\mu\text{m}^3\) (so \(1\) molecule \(\simeq 1.7\) nM)
mRNA lifetime \(1/d_0\) a few minutes
protein lifetime \(1/d_1\) set by dilution, about one cell cycle (\(\sim 20\)–\(60\) min)
proteins per mRNA tens
copies of a typical TF \(10\)–\(100\)

14.2 The one-stage model

Ignore mRNA and let a protein be produced at rate \(v\) and degraded (or diluted) at rate \(d\) per molecule: \[\varnothing \xrightarrow{v} P, \qquad P \xrightarrow{d} \varnothing .\] The master equation for \(P_n(t)\), the probability of \(n\) proteins, is the birth–death equation of Chapter 2 with constant birth rate: \[\frac{dP_n}{dt} = v(P_{n-1} - P_n) + d\bigl[(n + 1)P_{n+1} - nP_n\bigr].\] With the generating function \(G(z, t) = \sum_nP_nz^n\), using \(\sum_nnP_nz^n = z\partial_zG\), \[\partial_tG = (z - 1)\bigl(vG - d\,\partial_zG\bigr).\] At stationarity \(\partial_zG = \frac vdG\), so \(G = e^{(v/d)(z - 1)}\): a Poisson distribution with mean \(\langle n\rangle = v/d\) and Fano factor \(\sigma^2/\langle n\rangle = 1\). The time-dependent solution from \(n = 0\) is Poisson with mean \(\frac vd(1 - e^{-dt})\), and relaxation happens on the time scale \(1/d\).

A single molecule survives a time \(t\) with probability \(P_>(t) = e^{-dt}\), so its half-life is \(t_{1/2} = \ln2/d\): minutes for mRNA, while stable proteins are mostly removed by dilution at cell division.

14.3 The two-stage model

Experiments find Fano factors much larger than one. The reason is that proteins are made from a small number of mRNAs, each producing many proteins. The two-stage model includes both species: \[\text{DNA} \xrightarrow{v_0} \text{mRNA}, \qquad \text{mRNA} \xrightarrow{d_0} \varnothing, \qquad \text{mRNA} \xrightarrow{v_1} \text{mRNA} + P, \qquad P \xrightarrow{d_1} \varnothing .\] With \(m\) mRNAs and \(n\) proteins, \[\frac{dP_{m,n}}{dt} = v_0(P_{m-1,n} - P_{m,n}) + v_1m(P_{m,n-1} - P_{m,n}) + d_0\bigl[(m + 1)P_{m+1,n} - mP_{m,n}\bigr] + d_1\bigl[(n + 1)P_{m,n+1} - nP_{m,n}\bigr].\] The generating function \(F(z', z, t) = \sum_{m,n}P_{m,n}z'^mz^n\) obeys, with \(u = z' - 1\) and \(v = z - 1\), \[\partial_tF = v_0u\,F + v_1(1 + u)v\,\partial_uF - d_0u\,\partial_uF - d_1v\,\partial_vF .\]

14.3.1 Moments

From the equations for the means and second moments (or by differentiating \(F\) at \(u = v = 0\)), \[\langle m\rangle = \frac{v_0}{d_0}, \qquad \langle n\rangle = \frac{v_0v_1}{d_0d_1}, \qquad \frac{\sigma_n^2}{\langle n\rangle} = 1 + \frac{v_1}{d_0 + d_1}.\] Define the burst frequency \(a = v_0/d_1\) (mRNAs made per protein lifetime) and the burst size \(b = v_1/d_0\) (proteins made per mRNA). Then \(\langle n\rangle = ab\), and when \(\gamma = d_0/d_1 \gg 1\) (mRNA much shorter-lived than protein), the Fano factor is \(1 + b\). Protein noise is set by the burst size, not by the mean.

14.3.2 Bursts and the negative binomial

When \(\gamma \gg 1\), each mRNA lives briefly and produces a geometrically distributed burst of proteins with mean \(b\) (each step, translation with probability \(\frac{v_1}{v_1 + d_0}\) or decay). Bursts arrive as a Poisson process at rate \(v_0\), and proteins decay slowly. Solving the PDE by characteristics in this limit (Shahrezaei and Swain, 2008), the mRNA variable relaxes adiabatically to \(u \simeq \frac{bv}{1 - bv}\), and the stationary protein generating function becomes \[F(z) = \Bigl(\frac{1}{1 + b - bz}\Bigr)^a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_n = \frac{\Gamma(a + n)}{\Gamma(n + 1)\Gamma(a)}\Bigl(\frac{b}{1 + b}\Bigr)^n\Bigl(\frac{1}{1 + b}\Bigr)^a :\] a negative binomial with mean \(ab\) and variance \(ab(1 + b)\). The same distribution appeared for species abundances (Chapter 3): there, too, it reflected multiplicative “bursty” growth with immigration. The continuum limit is a Gamma distribution with shape \(a\) and scale \(b\).

Two genes with the same mean of \(50\) proteins:

  • \(a = 20\), \(b = 2.5\): frequent small bursts. The distribution is nearly Gaussian, with Fano factor \(3.5\) (Gillespie simulation: \(3.4\)).
  • \(a = 0.5\), \(b = 100\): rare large bursts. The distribution is strongly skewed, with a peak at zero and a long tail, and the Fano factor is \(101\) (simulation: \(102\)).

Time traces of the second gene show long silent periods interrupted by sudden jumps, exactly what is seen in single-molecule experiments (Cai, Friedman and Xie, 2006).

14.3.3 Intrinsic and extrinsic noise

Elowitz et al. (2002) put two copies of the same promoter, driving different fluorescent proteins, into the same E. coli cell. Fluctuations that affect both copies equally (cell size, ribosome and polymerase numbers, cell-cycle phase) are extrinsic. Fluctuations that make the two copies differ (the randomness of individual reaction events) are intrinsic. With normalised expressions \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \[\eta_{\text{int}}^2 = \frac{\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\rangle}{2\langle x_1\rangle\langle x_2\rangle}, \qquad \eta_{\text{ext}}^2 = \frac{\langle x_1x_2\rangle - \langle x_1\rangle\langle x_2\rangle}{\langle x_1\rangle\langle x_2\rangle}.\] Both contributions are comparable in bacteria. The models of this chapter describe intrinsic noise; extrinsic noise enters as slow fluctuations of the rates.

14.4 Why noise matters

Noise is not only a nuisance. Bet hedging: in fluctuating environments, populations in which some cells randomly express a stress-response program survive sudden stresses, as in bacterial persistence to antibiotics. Cell-fate decisions: noise can push cells across thresholds of bistable circuits (Chapter 16), as in the decision of B. subtilis to become competent or of phage λ to choose lysis or lysogeny. Conversely, cells suppress noise where precision matters, with negative feedback (Chapter 16) and with multiple gene copies.

14.5 Exercises

(One-stage model) Show that \(G(z, t) = \exp\bigl[\frac vd(1 - e^{-dt})(z - 1)\bigr]\) solves the one-stage generating-function equation with \(P_n(0) = \delta_{n0}\). What are the mean, variance and half-life?

Solution. With \(\mu(t) = \frac vd(1 - e^{-dt})\), \(G = e^{\mu(z - 1)}\), so \(\partial_tG = \dot\mu(z - 1)G = ve^{-dt}(z - 1)G\) and \(\partial_zG = \mu G\). The right-hand side is \((z - 1)(v - d\mu)G = (z - 1)ve^{-dt}G\), equal to the left. At \(t = 0\), \(G = 1\). The distribution is Poisson at all times, with mean \(=\) variance \(= \mu(t) \to v/d\). One molecule survives with \(e^{-dt}\), so \(t_{1/2} = \ln2/d\).

(Two-stage moments) Derive \(\langle m\rangle\), \(\langle n\rangle\) and the protein Fano factor from the moment equations of the two-stage model.

Solution. Means: \(\dot{\langle m\rangle} = v_0 - d_0\langle m\rangle\) and \(\dot{\langle n\rangle} = v_1\langle m\rangle - d_1\langle n\rangle\), so \(\langle m\rangle = v_0/d_0\) and \(\langle n\rangle = v_0v_1/(d_0d_1)\). Second moments at stationarity (writing covariances): \(\sigma_m^2 = \langle m\rangle\) (Poisson); \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Cov}(m, n) = v_1\sigma_m^2 - (d_0 + d_1)\mathrm{Cov} = 0\), so \(\mathrm{Cov} = \frac{v_1\langle m\rangle}{d_0 + d_1}\); \(\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_n^2 = v_1\langle m\rangle + d_1\langle n\rangle + 2v_1\mathrm{Cov} - 2d_1\sigma_n^2 = 0\). Using \(v_1\langle m\rangle = d_1\langle n\rangle\): \(\sigma_n^2 = \langle n\rangle + \frac{v_1}{d_1}\mathrm{Cov} = \langle n\rangle\bigl(1 + \frac{v_1}{d_0 + d_1}\bigr)\). Fano factor \(1 + \frac{v_1}{d_0 + d_1} \to 1 + b\) for \(d_0 \gg d_1\).

(Bursts by simulation) Simulate the two-stage model with the Gillespie algorithm for \(d_0 = 1\), \(d_1 = 0.01\) (in min\(^{-1}\)), and \((a, b) = (20, 2.5)\) and \((0.5, 100)\). Compare the time-averaged histograms with the negative binomial.

Solution. Rates: \(v_0 = ad_1\), \(v_1 = bd_0\). Time-weighted statistics over \(3\times10^5\) min give mean \(50.6\) and Fano factor \(3.40\) for \((20, 2.5)\), and mean \(49.7\) and Fano factor \(101.7\) for \((0.5, 100)\), matching \(ab = 50\) and \(1 + b = 3.5\), \(101\). Histograms agree with \(\mathrm{NB}(a, b/(1 + b))\): the first is unimodal around \(50\), the second has its mode at \(0\) and a long tail up to several hundred. With \(a < 1\) the gene is “off” most of the time.

(Negative binomial from bursts) Assume bursts arrive at rate \(v_0\), each adding a geometric number of proteins with mean \(b\), and proteins decay at rate \(d_1\). Show that the stationary generating function is \((1 + b - bz)^{-a}\) with \(a = v_0/d_1\).

Solution. A geometric burst has generating function \(g(z) = \frac{1}{1 + b - bz}\). The master equation in generating-function form is \(\partial_tF = v_0[g(z) - 1]F - d_1(z - 1)\partial_zF\). At stationarity \(\partial_z\ln F = \frac{a[g(z) - 1]}{z - 1} = \frac{ab}{1 + b - bz}\), which integrates to \(\ln F = -a\ln(1 + b - bz)\), using \(F(1) = 1\). Hence \(F = (1 + b - bz)^{-a}\), the negative binomial. Its mean is \(F'(1) = ab\) and its variance \(ab(1 + b)\).

(Intrinsic noise) For the one-stage model, compute \(\eta^2 = \sigma^2/\langle n\rangle^2\). How many proteins are needed for \(10\%\) noise? What if the protein is made in bursts of size \(b = 20\)?

Solution. Poisson: \(\eta^2 = 1/\langle n\rangle\), so \(10\%\) noise (\(\eta^2 = 0.01\)) needs \(\langle n\rangle = 100\). With bursts, \(\eta^2 = (1 + b)/\langle n\rangle\), so \(\langle n\rangle = 2100\) proteins are needed for the same precision: bursting is costly for precision, which is one reason why highly expressed, noise-sensitive genes tend to use high transcription and low translation rates (small \(b\)).
