18  Proteins as physical objects: polymers, folding and the tube model

Proteins are the working molecules of the cell: enzymes, motors, channels, transcription factors. Each is a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific three-dimensional shape that determines its function. Can polymer physics explain why protein structures are what they are? This chapter recalls the scaling theory of homopolymers (ideal chains, self-avoiding chains, compact globules), lists the experimental facts that make proteins special, and presents the tube model of Banavar and Maritan. Treating the chain as a tube of finite thickness, rather than a string of beads, generates the menu of secondary structures (helices and sheets) from geometry and symmetry alone. The chapter ends with an outlook on topics treated in the course projects.

18.1 Homopolymers: scaling laws

A polymer is a chain of \(L\) similar monomers at positions \(\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_L\). Its size is measured by the end-to-end distance \(R_L = |\mathbf r_L - \mathbf r_1|\) or the radius of gyration \(R_G^2 = \frac1L\sum_i(\mathbf r_i - \mathbf R_c)^2\). Both scale with length as \[R \sim L^\nu ,\] with a universal exponent \(\nu\) that depends only on the dimension and on the phase of the polymer, not on chemical details, beyond the persistence length.

18.1.1 Ideal chains

If the chain can cross itself (no excluded volume), it is a random walk of \(L/\ell_p\) independent segments of length \(\sim\ell_p\) (the persistence length). By the central limit theorem the end-to-end vector is Gaussian, \[P_{\text{ideal}}(\mathbf R \mid L) = \Bigl(\frac{3}{2\pi L\ell_p}\Bigr)^{3/2}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{3R^2}{2L\ell_p}\Bigr), \qquad \langle R^2\rangle = L\ell_p, \qquad \nu = \frac12 .\] The Gaussian form has an entropic interpretation: the free energy \(F(R) = -k_BT\ln P = \frac{3k_BTR^2}{2L\ell_p}\) is that of an entropic spring. Stretching a chain reduces the number of its conformations, which is why rubber is elastic.

18.1.2 Self-avoiding chains: Flory’s argument

Real chains cannot overlap. Excluded volume swells them, with \(\nu > 1/2\). Flory’s estimate balances the elastic entropy against the repulsion between monomers in a volume \(R^d\): \[\frac{F(R)}{k_BT} \simeq \frac{R^2}{L\ell_p} + v\frac{L^2}{R^d} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R \sim L^{\nu_F}, \qquad \nu_F = \frac{3}{d + 2}.\] This gives \(\nu = 3/5\) in \(d = 3\) (the exact value is \(0.588\)), \(3/4\) in \(d = 2\) (exact), and \(1/2\) at the upper critical dimension \(d = 4\). The end-to-end distribution of a self-avoiding chain is non-Gaussian, with the scaling form \(P_{\text{real}}(\mathbf R \mid L) \propto x^{\theta}e^{-x^{\delta}}\), \(x = R/L^\nu\), \(\delta = 1/(1 - \nu)\).

18.1.3 Good and poor solvents

Monomers also attract each other with an energy that competes with solvent affinity. In a good solvent (high temperature) the chain is swollen (\(\nu = 0.588\)). In a poor solvent (low temperature) attraction wins and the chain collapses into a compact globule that fills space, so \(R \sim L^{1/3}\). At the \(\Theta\)-point between them, the second virial coefficient vanishes and the chain is nearly ideal (\(\nu = 1/2\)). Flory’s theorem states that in a dense, compact polymer a sub-chain of length \(\ell\) behaves as ideal, \(R_G(\ell) \sim \ell^{1/2}\), because excluded volume is screened by the other chains.

The compact phase of standard models (beads on a string with pair potentials, or the Edwards continuum model \(H = \int ds\,[\dot{\mathbf r}^2 + \kappa\ddot{\mathbf r}^2] + v\iint ds\,ds'\,\delta(\mathbf r(s) - \mathbf r(s'))\)) is either a crystal of Hamiltonian walks or a disordered liquid-like globule. Its number of conformations grows exponentially, \(\mathcal N(L) \sim L^{\gamma - 1}\mu^L\), and none of them is special. This is very far from proteins.

18.2 What makes proteins special

A protein is a heteropolymer of \(20\) amino acids, with a backbone (\(\text{N}\)–\(\text{C}_\alpha\)–\(\text{C}'\)) and side chains of different sizes and chemistry (hydrophobic, polar, charged). Experiments show:

  1. Proteins fold rapidly (microseconds to seconds) and reproducibly into a native state.
  2. The native geometry determines function.
  3. The number of distinct folds is small, a few thousand, much less than the number of sequences.
  4. Many different sequences fold into the same structure: folds are robust to mutations.
  5. Structures are built from a few modular secondary motifs: \(\alpha\)-helices and almost-planar \(\beta\)-sheets, as predicted by Pauling and Corey (1951) from hydrogen-bond geometry.
  6. Proteins with the same fold have similar folding rates and transition states.
  7. Native structures are flexible and interact with ligands and with each other in versatile ways. Misfolded proteins can aggregate into amyloid fibrils (sheet-rich structures common to Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases) that any sequence can form.

Levinthal’s paradox (1969): if each residue of a \(100\)-residue protein had \(3\) conformations, a random search among \(3^{100} \simeq 5\times10^{47}\) states at \(10^{13}\) per second would take \(\sim 10^{27}\) years. Folding must follow a biased funnel-shaped energy landscape, not a random search. These facts suggest that the menu of folds is determined by general physical principles shared by all sequences, with the sequence choosing among a few pre-sculpted possibilities.

18.3 The tube model

18.3.1 Thickness as a missing ingredient

A string of beads with pair potentials has a symmetry that a real chain lacks. Each backbone unit is not a sphere but closer to a coin, a flat object with a preferred orientation, because of the planar peptide bond and the direction of the side chain. In the continuum limit, a chain of spheres becomes a line of zero thickness, while a chain of coins becomes a tube of finite thickness. A pair potential between points of a curve is singular: nearby points along the chain are always close, so the potential needs a cut-off, i.e. a fundamental length the model lacks.

The thickness of a curve is captured by three-body interactions (Gonzalez and Maddocks, 1999). For any three points \(s, s', s''\) of the curve, let \(R(s, s', s'')\) be the radius of the circle through them. As the points merge it tends to the local radius of curvature. The thickness of the curve is \[\Delta = \min_{s,s',s''}R(s, s', s''),\] the radius of the largest tube that can be inflated around the curve without self-intersection. A tube of thickness \(\Delta\) is described by forbidding conformations with any \(R(s, s', s'') < \Delta\): a non-singular, three-body constraint that respects the symmetry of the chain.

18.3.2 Optimal packing: the magic helix

What shapes does a compact tube adopt? Maximise compactness at fixed thickness, the polymer analogue of Kepler’s sphere packing. The answer is a helix with a specific geometry: the densest packed single helix has pitch-to-radius ratio \(p/r \simeq 2.512\), within \(5\%\) of real \(\alpha\)-helices (Maritan, Micheletti, Trovato, Banavar, Nature 2000). The densest double helix has \(p/r = 2\pi\), within \(4\%\) of B-DNA (\(6.03\)). Other optimal configurations are planar zig-zag sheets, which pack side by side like \(\beta\)-sheets. Secondary structures emerge from geometry, not from the chemical details of hydrogen bonds, which then fit into them.

18.3.3 The marginally compact phase

Add a solvophobic attraction proportional to the buried surface (energy \(\simeq -\) area of the tube not exposed to solvent), with a range comparable to the thickness. As temperature and the ratio of interaction range to thickness vary, the tube has a swollen phase, a compact phase, and between them a marginally compact phase. There the tube is just compact enough to bury its surface but still constrained by its thickness. In this phase:

  • the number of low-energy conformations is small: the degeneracy that plagues standard polymers is lifted;
  • the ground states are rich in helices and sheets, assembled into a few thousand distinct topologies resembling real folds;
  • the free-energy landscape is pre-sculpted by geometry and symmetry, and the sequence only has to choose one of a few competing minima. This explains robustness to mutations, fast folding, and the fact that unrelated sequences share folds.

Proteins appear to sit near a phase transition, in the marginally compact phase. The same structures make them prone to aggregation (amyloids, a generic sheet-rich state) and give intrinsically disordered proteins a natural place: sequences that do not select any minimum.

18.4 Outlook: from cells to communities

The course projects connect the three modules:

  • Clonal stem-cell dynamics. Stem cells divide symmetrically or asymmetrically and differentiate stochastically. Master equations for clone sizes, like those of Chapters 2–3, predict clone-size distributions to compare with lineage-tracing data (Greulich, 2019).
  • The Waddington landscape. Cell types as attractors of gene regulatory networks, modelled as Hopfield networks whose stored patterns are expression profiles (Chapters 11 and 16). Differentiation is a descent in a landscape, and reprogramming a forced jump between basins.
  • Microbiomes. Human gut microbial communities show macroecological patterns (abundance distributions, Taylor’s law, abundance fluctuations) captured by stochastic logistic models with environmental noise (Chapter 2) and by disordered Lotka–Volterra models (Chapter 6). Changes of their parameters can describe the transition to dysbiosis.
  • Eco-evolutionary dynamics. Strains within a species interact and evolve on ecological time scales; consumer–resource models with adaptive metabolism (Chapter 7) connect metabolic networks to community assembly.

A guest lecture (A. Forli) illustrated the neuroscience frontier. In freely flying Egyptian fruit bats, hippocampal neurons encode the bat’s own position and also social information about group members. Ensembles of place cells “replay” flight trajectories in time-compressed form during rest. Event-based cameras enable microsecond imaging of neural activity. Comparative studies across species test whether mechanisms such as theta sequences, believed to be universal, really are.

18.5 Exercises

(Ideal chain) Show that for an ideal chain of \(n = L/\ell_p\) independent segments of length \(\ell_p\) with isotropic directions, \(\langle R^2\rangle = n\ell_p^2\), and derive the entropic force needed to hold the ends at distance \(R\).

Solution. \(\mathbf R = \sum_k\mathbf b_k\) with \(|\mathbf b_k| = \ell_p\) and \(\langle\mathbf b_k\cdot\mathbf b_l\rangle = \ell_p^2\delta_{kl}\), so \(\langle R^2\rangle = n\ell_p^2 = L\ell_p\). By the central limit theorem \(P(\mathbf R) \propto e^{-3R^2/(2n\ell_p^2)}\), and \(F = -k_BT\ln P = \frac{3k_BT}{2n\ell_p^2}R^2 + \text{const}\). The force is \(f = \partial F/\partial R = \frac{3k_BT}{n\ell_p^2}R\): a Hookean spring with stiffness proportional to temperature. Because it is entropic, the chain gets stiffer when heated.

(Flory exponent) Minimise \(F/k_BT = R^2/(L\ell_p) + vL^2/R^d\) with respect to \(R\) and derive \(\nu_F\). Evaluate it in \(d = 1, 2, 3, 4\) and compare with exact values.

Solution. \(\partial_RF = 2R/(L\ell_p) - dvL^2/R^{d+1} = 0\) gives \(R^{d+2} \propto L^3\), so \(\nu_F = 3/(d + 2)\): \(1\) in \(d = 1\) (exact, a stretched chain), \(3/4\) in \(d = 2\) (exact), \(3/5\) in \(d = 3\) (exact \(0.588\)), \(1/2\) in \(d = 4\) (exact, mean field from here on). Flory’s argument works so well because errors in the two terms nearly cancel.

(Levinthal’s paradox) Estimate the time for an exhaustive search of the conformations of a \(100\)-residue protein with \(3\) states per residue, sampling \(10^{13}\) conformations per second. How does a funnel resolve the paradox?

Solution. \(3^{100} = 5.2\times10^{47}\) conformations, so the search time is \(5.2\times10^{34}\) s \(\approx 1.6\times10^{27}\) years, against a real folding time of milliseconds. In a funnel each residue’s conformation is stabilised as soon as it is locally correct: errors are not frozen but corrected, so the search is biased toward the native state. If each residue finds its local state independently in time \(\tau\), folding takes \(\sim\tau\ln L\) instead of \(\sim\tau\,3^L\). A modest energetic bias per residue (\(\sim k_BT\)) suffices.

(Compact-state entropy) A compact polymer of \(L\) monomers has \(\mathcal N \sim\mu^L\) conformations with \(\mu \approx 1.4\) (cubic lattice). Compare the number of compact conformations of a \(100\)-residue chain with the number of distinct protein folds. What does this say about standard polymer models?

Solution. \(1.4^{100} \simeq 4\times10^{14}\) compact conformations, all of comparable energy in a homopolymer model, against about \(10^3\) observed folds. A standard compact homopolymer has an enormous ground-state degeneracy and no reason to prefer helices or sheets. The tube model reduces the number of competing low-energy structures to a few, rich in secondary motifs, matching the observed “menu” of folds.
