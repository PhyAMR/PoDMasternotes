10  Firing-rate networks and Wilson–Cowan dynamics

Detailed spiking models do not scale to networks of many neurons. Firing-rate models describe each neuron (or population) by a continuous rate, obtained by averaging over spikes, and connect them through synaptic weights. This chapter derives the rate equations, studies two interacting populations, excitatory and inhibitory (the neural analogue of predator and prey), and their stochastic version, which shows the stochastic amplification of Chapter 5. It then analyses linear recurrent networks, which amplify selected input patterns and integrate signals, and stochastic networks of binary units, the bridge to the Hopfield and Boltzmann machines of Chapters 11–12.

10.1 From spikes to rates

Consider a neuron receiving inputs from \(N_u\) presynaptic neurons \(b\) with firing rates \(u_b\) through synaptic weights \(w_b\) (positive for excitatory, negative for inhibitory synapses). A presynaptic spike at time \(0\) produces in the postsynaptic soma a current \(w_bK_s(t)\), where the synaptic kernel \(K_s\) (normalised, \(\int_0^\infty K_s = 1\)) describes the opening and closing of synaptic conductances. Summing over spikes and inputs, assuming linear superposition, \[I_s(t) = \sum_bw_b\int_{-\infty}^tK_s(t - \tau)\rho_b(\tau)\,d\tau \simeq \sum_bw_b\int_{-\infty}^tK_s(t - \tau)u_b(\tau)\,d\tau,\] where the spike trains are replaced by their rates, which is accurate with many inputs. For an exponential kernel \(K_s(t) = e^{-t/\tau_s}/\tau_s\), differentiation gives \[\tau_s\frac{dI_s}{dt} = -I_s + \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u .\] The output rate follows the input current through a static nonlinearity \(\psi\) (the f–I curve of Chapter 9), low-pass filtered by the membrane: \[\tau_r\frac{dv}{dt} = -v + \psi\bigl(I_s(t)\bigr).\] These two equations form the firing-rate model. If \(\tau_s \ll \tau_r\), the current is always at equilibrium, \(I_s = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u\), and \[\tau_r\frac{dv}{dt} = -v + \psi(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u);\] if instead \(\tau_r \ll \tau_s\), \(v = \psi(I_s)\) and the current carries the dynamics. At stationarity \(v_\infty = \psi(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u)\): the perceptron’s integration rule, now derived from synaptic physiology.

10.2 Excitatory and inhibitory populations

Dale’s law: a neuron releases the same neurotransmitter at all its synapses, so it is either excitatory (its outgoing weights are all positive) or inhibitory (all negative). Neurons therefore come in two “species”, like predators and prey. For one excitatory and one inhibitory unit with rates \(v_E\), \(v_I\) and external inputs \(h_E\), \(h_I\), \[\tau_E\frac{dv_E}{dt} = -v_E + \psi\bigl(h_E + M_{EE}v_E + M_{EI}v_I\bigr), \qquad \tau_I\frac{dv_I}{dt} = -v_I + \psi\bigl(h_I + M_{IE}v_E + M_{II}v_I\bigr),\] with \(M_{EE}, M_{IE} > 0\) and \(M_{EI}, M_{II} < 0\), and a rectifying activation \(\psi(x) = [x]_+\).

Take \(M_{EE} = 1.25\), \(M_{IE} = 1\), \(M_{EI} = -1\), \(M_{II} = 0\), \(h_E = 10\) Hz, \(h_I = -10\) Hz, \(\tau_E = 10\) ms, with \(\tau_I\) as control parameter. With both rates positive, the fixed point solves a linear system: \(v_E^* = 26.7\) Hz, \(v_I^* = 16.7\) Hz, independent of \(\tau_I\). The Jacobian is \[J = \begin{pmatrix}(M_{EE} - 1)/\tau_E & M_{EI}/\tau_E\\ M_{IE}/\tau_I & -1/\tau_I\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}J = \frac{0.25}{\tau_E} - \frac{1}{\tau_I}, \qquad \det J = \frac{0.75}{\tau_E\tau_I} > 0 .\] Its eigenvalues are complex. For \(\tau_I < 40\) ms the trace is negative and the fixed point is a stable focus: rates relax through damped oscillations. At \(\tau_I = 40\) ms the eigenvalues cross the imaginary axis (\(\pm 43i\,\text{s}^{-1}\), about \(7\) Hz), a Hopf bifurcation. For \(\tau_I > 40\) ms the system settles on a limit cycle: sustained oscillations, confined by the rectification. Slow inhibition destabilises the network, as delayed negative feedback does in any control system.

The nullclines \(\dot v_E = 0\) and \(\dot v_I = 0\) divide the phase plane into regions with opposite flow and give the qualitative picture at a glance.

10.3 Wilson–Cowan models

10.3.1 Deterministic populations

Wilson and Cowan (1972) described large homogeneous populations by the fractions \(x\) and \(y\) of active excitatory and inhibitory neurons: \[\frac{dx}{dt} = -\alpha_Ex + f\bigl(w_{EE}x - w_{EI}y + h_E\bigr), \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = -\alpha_Iy + f\bigl(w_{IE}x - w_{II}y + h_I\bigr),\] with a sigmoidal activation \(f(s) = 1/(1 + e^{-s})\), decay rates \(\alpha_{E,I}\), and input \(h\). The model reproduces multistability, hysteresis and oscillations of cortical tissue.

10.3.2 A microscopic stochastic model

To include finite-size fluctuations, build a reaction scheme on two populations of \(V\) neurons each, where \(\Phi\) denotes an inactive neuron (Benayoun et al., 2010; de Santo et al., 2018): \[\Phi \xrightarrow{f(s_x)} x, \quad \Phi \xrightarrow{f(s_y)} y \quad\text{(activation)}, \qquad x \xrightarrow{\alpha_E} \Phi, \quad y \xrightarrow{\alpha_I} \Phi \quad\text{(decay)}.\] Choose the simplest symmetric case: \(\alpha_E = \alpha_I = 1\), no self-coupling (\(w_{EE} = w_{II} = 0\)), \(|w_{EI}| = |w_{IE}| = r\), and inputs that centre the activations: \[s_x = -r\Bigl(\frac{n_y}{V} - \frac12\Bigr), \qquad s_y = r\Bigl(\frac{n_x}{V} - \frac12\Bigr).\] Inhibitory activity lowers excitatory activation, and excitatory activity raises inhibitory activation. With densities \(x = n_x/V\), \(y = n_y/V\), the transition rates are \(T(n_x \to n_x \mp 1) = n_x\) and \(T(n_x \to n_x + 1) = Vf(s_x)\), and similarly for \(y\). The Kramers–Moyal expansion gives the Langevin equations \[\dot x = -x + f\bigl(-r(y - \tfrac12)\bigr) + \frac1{\sqrt V}\sqrt{x + f\bigl(-r(y - \tfrac12)\bigr)}\,\eta_x,\] \[\dot y = -y + f\bigl(r(x - \tfrac12)\bigr) + \frac1{\sqrt V}\sqrt{y + f\bigl(r(x - \tfrac12)\bigr)}\,\eta_y,\] with independent white noises. For \(V \to \infty\) the fixed point is \(x^* = y^* = 1/2\), with Jacobian \[J = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -r/4\\ r/4 & -1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = -1 \pm i\frac r4 .\] The fixed point is a stable focus: the deterministic model predicts damped oscillations.

10.3.3 Stochastic amplification

Following Chapter 5, write \(x = x^* + \xi_1/\sqrt V\), \(y = y^* + \xi_2/\sqrt V\). The linear noise approximation gives \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi} = J\boldsymbol\xi + \boldsymbol\mu\), with diffusion matrix \(B = \operatorname{diag}(x^* + f(0), y^* + f(0)) = \mathbb 1\). Its power spectrum is \[P_1(\omega) = \bigl[(i\omega - J)^{-1}B(-i\omega - J^\top)^{-1}\bigr]_{11} = \frac{1 + r^2/16 + \omega^2}{\bigl(\omega^2 - 1 - r^2/16\bigr)^2 + 4\omega^2}.\] For \(r > 4/\sqrt3 \simeq 2.3\) it has a maximum at a non-zero frequency, approaching \(r/4\) for large \(r\): for \(r = 8\) the peak is at \(\omega \simeq 2.0\), about \(2.6\) times its zero-frequency value. Intrinsic noise sustains quasi-cycles around a stable state, a mechanism proposed for gamma oscillations in cortex. A related point: \(J\) is not symmetric (it is non-normal when \(w_{EI} \neq w_{IE}\)), and non-normal dynamics transiently amplify perturbations even when all eigenvalues are stable. This makes fluctuations much larger than the eigenvalues suggest.

10.3.4 Avalanches in the Wilson–Cowan model

In the variables \(\Sigma = x + y\) (total activity) and \(\Delta = x - y\) (imbalance), the stochastic model with excitatory–inhibitory balance produces bursts of activity separated by quiescence. Avalanche sizes and durations are power-law distributed, with exponents \(\tau \simeq 3/2\), \(\alpha \simeq 2\) and \(\langle S\rangle(T) \sim T^{\gamma}\), \(\gamma \simeq 2\), close to those of a critical branching process. They arise in a regime where the network is not at a phase transition but is dominated by inhibition with a weak, noisy excitatory drive (de Santo et al., 2018): another warning that power laws alone do not prove criticality.

10.3.5 Balanced networks

How do cortical neurons, each receiving thousands of inputs, fire irregularly at low rates? Let each neuron receive \(K\) excitatory and \(K\) inhibitory inputs, each a Poisson train at rate \(\nu_0\), with synaptic strength \(W_0\). The mean excitatory and inhibitory currents are \(\langle I_E\rangle = KW_0\nu_0\) and \(\langle I_I\rangle = -KW_0\nu_0\), while their fluctuations have variance \(\propto KW_0^2\nu_0\). If \(W_0 = O(1)\), the means diverge with \(K\) and the neuron saturates. If \(W_0 \sim 1/K\), fluctuations vanish and the neuron is deterministic. With the balanced scaling \(W_0 = J_0/\sqrt K\), the means are each \(O(\sqrt K)\) but nearly cancel, \(\langle I_E\rangle + \langle I_I\rangle = O(1)\), and the fluctuations are \(O(1)\), \(\sigma^2(I) = J_0^2\nu_0\). Neurons operate in a fluctuation-driven regime and fire irregularly. Balance is maintained dynamically by the network (van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky, 1996), possibly helped by homeostatic plasticity.

10.4 Recurrent networks

10.4.1 Feedforward and recurrent architectures

In a feedforward network, output neurons \(\mathbf v\) receive inputs \(\mathbf u\) through a weight matrix \(W\): \(\tau_r\dot{\mathbf v} = -\mathbf v + \psi(W\mathbf u)\). In a recurrent network the outputs are also coupled to each other by \(M\): \[\tau_r\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = -\mathbf v + \psi(\mathbf h + M\mathbf v), \qquad \mathbf h = W\mathbf u .\] With separate excitatory and inhibitory populations \(M\) has a block structure. Analytical work often assumes \(M\) symmetric, which violates Dale’s law but can be justified when inhibition is fast. If \(\tau_I \to 0\) and \(M_{II} = 0\), then \(\mathbf v_I = M_{IE}\mathbf v_E\), and the effective excitatory network has the (possibly symmetric) matrix \(M_{EE} + M_{EI}M_{IE}\).

10.4.2 Linear recurrent networks

For a linear activation and symmetric \(M\) with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf e_\mu\) and eigenvalues \(\lambda_\mu\), expand \(\mathbf v(t) = \sum_\mu c_\mu(t)\mathbf e_\mu\): \[\tau_r\frac{dc_\mu}{dt} = -(1 - \lambda_\mu)c_\mu + \mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf h .\] For constant input, \[c_\mu(t) = \frac{\mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf h}{1 - \lambda_\mu}\Bigl(1 - e^{-t(1 - \lambda_\mu)/\tau_r}\Bigr) + c_\mu(0)\,e^{-t(1 - \lambda_\mu)/\tau_r}.\]

  • If some \(\lambda_\mu > 1\) the network is unstable: activity grows exponentially.
  • If all \(\lambda_\mu < 1\), \(\mathbf v_\infty = \sum_\mu\frac{\mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf h}{1 - \lambda_\mu}\mathbf e_\mu\): each input component is multiplied by \(1/(1 - \lambda_\mu)\). Eigenvalues close to \(1\) give selective amplification of the corresponding input patterns, and the network acts as a filter that picks out features.
  • If \(\lambda_1 = 1\) exactly (marginal), the network integrates its input: \(\tau_r\dot c_1 = \mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf h\), so \(c_1(t) = c_1(0) + \frac1{\tau_r}\int_0^t\mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf h(t')dt'\). After a brief pulse, activity persists indefinitely: a memory. The oculomotor integrator, which converts bursts of velocity commands into a sustained eye position, is thought to work this way. Its fine tuning (\(\lambda_1\) must equal \(1\) very precisely) is a classic problem.

Neurons in visual cortex labelled by their preferred angle \(\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]\), with density \(\rho_\theta\), coupled by a translation-invariant kernel \(M(\theta - \theta')\): \[\tau_r\frac{dv(\theta)}{dt} = -v(\theta) + h(\theta) + \rho_\theta\int_{-\pi}^\pi d\theta'\,M(\theta - \theta')\,v(\theta').\] The eigenfunctions of a convolution on the circle are Fourier modes, \(e_0 = (2\pi\rho_\theta)^{-1/2}\), \(e_\mu \propto \cos\mu\theta, \sin\mu\theta\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_\mu = \rho_\theta\int M(\theta)\cos(\mu\theta)d\theta\). For \(M(\theta) = \frac{\lambda_1}{\pi\rho_\theta}\cos\theta\) only \(\mu = 1\) is amplified: a noisy, weakly tuned input becomes a smooth, sharply tuned output peaked at the input’s preferred angle. With the rectification \([\cdot]_+\) the network stays stable even for \(\lambda_1 > 1\) (e.g. \(1.9\)), and the tuning curve becomes independent of the input contrast.

10.5 Stochastic recurrent networks

Let each unit be binary, \(v_a \in \{0, 1\}\), with total input \(I_a = h_a + \sum_{a'}M_{aa'}v_{a'}\), symmetric weights (\(M_{aa'} = M_{a'a}\)) and \(M_{aa} = 0\). At each step a random unit is updated stochastically: \[P[v_a = 1] = F(I_a) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-I_a}} .\] This Markov chain (Glauber dynamics) satisfies detailed balance with respect to the Boltzmann distribution \[P[\mathbf v] = \frac{e^{-E(\mathbf v)}}{Z}, \qquad E(\mathbf v) = -\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf v - \frac12\mathbf v\cdot M\mathbf v,\] which is therefore its unique stationary distribution. Flipping unit \(a\) changes the energy by \(\Delta E = -I_a\,\Delta v_a\), and \(F(I_a)/(1 - F(I_a)) = e^{I_a}\) is exactly the Boltzmann ratio. A mean-field version replaces the units by their average activity, \(\tau_s\dot{\mathbf I} = -\mathbf I + \mathbf h + M\mathbf F(\mathbf I)\), with factorised distribution \(Q[\mathbf v] = \prod_aF(I_a)^{v_a}(1 - F(I_a))^{1 - v_a}\). The Kullback–Leibler divergence \(D_{KL}(Q\|P)\) is a Lyapunov function of these dynamics: they converge to the mean-field approximation of \(P\). This is the starting point of Boltzmann machines (Chapter 12).

10.6 Exercises

(E–I pair) For the example network, (a) verify the fixed point \((26.7, 16.7)\) Hz; (b) show that the Hopf bifurcation occurs at \(\tau_I = 40\) ms and find the oscillation frequency there; (c) what happens for \(\tau_I = 50\) ms?

Solution.

  1. \(v_E = 1.25v_E - v_I + 10\) and \(v_I = v_E - 10\) give \(0.25v_E = v_I - 10 = v_E - 20\), so \(v_E = 26.7\) and \(v_I = 16.7\). (b) \(\operatorname{tr}J = 0.025 - 1/\tau_I\) (in ms\(^{-1}\)) vanishes at \(\tau_I = 40\) ms, where \(\det J = 0.75/(10\times40) = 1.875\times10^{-3}\,\text{ms}^{-2}\), so \(\omega = \sqrt{\det J} = 0.0433\,\text{ms}^{-1} = 43\,\text{s}^{-1}\), i.e. \(f = 6.9\) Hz. (c) At \(\tau_I = 50\) ms the eigenvalues are \((2.5 \pm 38.6i)\,\text{s}^{-1}\): unstable focus. Trajectories spiral out until the rectification \([\cdot]_+\) clips \(v_I\) at zero, producing a stable limit cycle of about \(6\) Hz with large amplitude.

(Stochastic Wilson–Cowan spectrum) For the symmetric stochastic Wilson–Cowan model at \(x^* = y^* = 1/2\), derive the noise matrix \(B\), the power spectrum \(P_1(\omega)\), and the condition for a peak at \(\omega > 0\). Evaluate for \(r = 1, 4, 8\).

Solution. At the fixed point the activation and decay rates both equal \(1/2\) for each population, and \(B_{xx} = x^* + f(0) = 1\), \(B_{yy} = 1\), \(B_{xy} = 0\) (the reactions change one population each). With \(J = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -a\\ a & -1\end{pmatrix}\), \(a = r/4\): \(|\det(i\omega - J)|^2 = (1 + a^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\omega^2\) and the numerator is \(B_{11}(\omega^2 + 1) + B_{22}a^2 = 1 + a^2 + \omega^2\). Writing \(c = 1 + a^2\) and \(u = \omega^2\), \(P_1 = (c + u)/[(c - u)^2 + 4u]\) increases at \(u = 0\) iff \(3c^2 - 4c > 0\), i.e. \(c > 4/3\): a peak at \(\omega > 0\) exists iff \(a > 1/\sqrt3\), i.e. \(r > 4/\sqrt3 \simeq 2.3\). Numerically: \(r = 1\), maximum at \(\omega = 0\); \(r = 4\), a broad peak at \(\omega \simeq 0.9\), \(1.2\) times \(P_1(0)\); \(r = 8\), a clear peak at \(\omega \simeq 2.0\), \(2.6\) times \(P_1(0)\). Strong E–I coupling turns noise into quasi-periodic oscillations at the imaginary part \(r/4\) of the eigenvalues.

(Selective amplification) A symmetric linear network has eigenvalues \(0.9\), \(0.5\) and \(0\) with eigenvectors \(\mathbf e_1\), \(\mathbf e_2\), \(\mathbf e_3\), and receives \(\mathbf h = \mathbf e_1 + \mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_3\). Find \(\mathbf v_\infty\) and the relaxation times of each mode with \(\tau_r = 10\) ms.

Solution. \(\mathbf v_\infty = \frac{1}{0.1}\mathbf e_1 + \frac1{0.5}\mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_3 = 10\mathbf e_1 + 2\mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_3\). The first pattern is amplified tenfold relative to the input. Relaxation times \(\tau_r/(1 - \lambda_\mu)\): \(100\) ms, \(20\) ms, \(10\) ms. Amplification comes with slowing down: modes near \(\lambda = 1\) are both large and slow, the hallmark of a system close to an instability (critical slowing down, Chapter 13).

(Neural integrator) With \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and \(\tau_r = 10\) ms, an input pulse \(\mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf h = 5\) Hz lasting \(50\) ms is applied from \(c_1(0) = 0\). What is \(c_1\) after the pulse? If instead \(\lambda_1 = 0.99\), how long does the memory last?

Solution. \(c_1 = \frac{1}{10}\times5\times50 = 25\) Hz, and it stays there after the pulse: persistent activity. With \(\lambda_1 = 0.99\) the mode decays with time constant \(\tau_r/(1 - \lambda_1) = 1\) s, so the memory fades within a second or two. To hold a memory for \(\sim 10\) s (as the oculomotor system does) requires \(|1 - \lambda_1| \lesssim 10^{-3}\): fine tuning, which motivates models with plasticity or bistable units.

(Glauber dynamics and detailed balance) For the stochastic binary network, show that the update rule \(P[v_a = 1] = 1/(1 + e^{-I_a})\) satisfies detailed balance with respect to \(P \propto e^{-E}\).

Solution. Let \(\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf v'\) differ only in unit \(a\), with \(v_a = 0\) and \(v'_a = 1\). Then \(E(\mathbf v') - E(\mathbf v) = -h_a - \sum_{a'}M_{aa'}v_{a'} = -I_a\) (using symmetry and \(M_{aa} = 0\)). The ratio of transition probabilities is \(\frac{W(\mathbf v \to \mathbf v')}{W(\mathbf v' \to \mathbf v)} = \frac{F(I_a)}{1 - F(I_a)} = e^{I_a} = e^{-(E(\mathbf v') - E(\mathbf v))} = \frac{P(\mathbf v')}{P(\mathbf v)}\), which is detailed balance. The Boltzmann distribution is stationary, and the chain is ergodic, so it is the unique stationary state.

## From spikes to rates

Consider a neuron receiving inputs from $N_u$ presynaptic neurons $b$ with firing rates $u_b$ through synaptic weights $w_b$ (positive for excitatory, negative for inhibitory synapses). A presynaptic spike at time $0$ produces in the postsynaptic soma a current $w_bK_s(t)$, where the **synaptic kernel** $K_s$ (normalised, $\int_0^\infty K_s = 1$) describes the opening and closing of synaptic conductances. Summing over spikes and inputs, assuming linear superposition,
$$I_s(t) = \sum_bw_b\int_{-\infty}^tK_s(t - \tau)\rho_b(\tau)\,d\tau \simeq \sum_bw_b\int_{-\infty}^tK_s(t - \tau)u_b(\tau)\,d\tau,$$
where the spike trains are replaced by their rates, which is accurate with many inputs. For an exponential kernel $K_s(t) = e^{-t/\tau_s}/\tau_s$, differentiation gives
$$\tau_s\frac{dI_s}{dt} = -I_s + \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u .$$
The output rate follows the input current through a static nonlinearity $\psi$ (the f–I curve of Chapter 9), low-pass filtered by the membrane:
$$\tau_r\frac{dv}{dt} = -v + \psi\bigl(I_s(t)\bigr).$$
These two equations form the **firing-rate model**. If $\tau_s \ll \tau_r$, the current is always at equilibrium, $I_s = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u$, and
$$\tau_r\frac{dv}{dt} = -v + \psi(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u);$$
if instead $\tau_r \ll \tau_s$, $v = \psi(I_s)$ and the current carries the dynamics. At stationarity $v_\infty = \psi(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u)$: the perceptron's integration rule, now derived from synaptic physiology.

Neurons therefore come in two "species", like predators and prey. For one excitatory and one inhibitory unit with rates $v_E$, $v_I$ and external inputs $h_E$, $h_I$,
$$\tau_E\frac{dv_E}{dt} = -v_E + \psi\bigl(h_E + M_{EE}v_E + M_{EI}v_I\bigr), \qquad \tau_I\frac{dv_I}{dt} = -v_I + \psi\bigl(h_I + M_{IE}v_E + M_{II}v_I\bigr),$$
with $M_{EE}, M_{IE} > 0$ and $M_{EI}, M_{II} < 0$, and a rectifying activation $\psi(x) = [x]_+$.

## Wilson–Cowan models

### Deterministic populations

Wilson and Cowan (1972) described large homogeneous populations by the fractions $x$ and $y$ of active excitatory and inhibitory neurons:
$$\frac{dx}{dt} = -\alpha_Ex + f\bigl(w_{EE}x - w_{EI}y + h_E\bigr), \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = -\alpha_Iy + f\bigl(w_{IE}x - w_{II}y + h_I\bigr),$$
with a sigmoidal activation $f(s) = 1/(1 + e^{-s})$, decay rates $\alpha_{E,I}$, and input $h$. The model reproduces multistability, hysteresis and oscillations of cortical tissue.

### A microscopic stochastic model

To include finite-size fluctuations, build a reaction scheme on two populations of $V$ neurons each, where $\Phi$ denotes an inactive neuron (Benayoun et al., 2010; de Santo et al., 2018):
$$\Phi \xrightarrow{f(s_x)} x, \quad \Phi \xrightarrow{f(s_y)} y \quad\text{(activation)}, \qquad x \xrightarrow{\alpha_E} \Phi, \quad y \xrightarrow{\alpha_I} \Phi \quad\text{(decay)}.$$
Choose the simplest symmetric case: $\alpha_E = \alpha_I = 1$, no self-coupling ($w_{EE} = w_{II} = 0$), $|w_{EI}| = |w_{IE}| = r$, and inputs that centre the activations:
$$s_x = -r\Bigl(\frac{n_y}{V} - \frac12\Bigr), \qquad s_y = r\Bigl(\frac{n_x}{V} - \frac12\Bigr).$$
Inhibitory activity lowers excitatory activation, and excitatory activity raises inhibitory activation. With densities $x = n_x/V$, $y = n_y/V$, the transition rates are $T(n_x \to n_x \mp 1) = n_x$ and $T(n_x \to n_x + 1) = Vf(s_x)$, and similarly for $y$. The Kramers–Moyal expansion gives the Langevin equations
$$\dot x = -x + f\bigl(-r(y - \tfrac12)\bigr) + \frac1{\sqrt V}\sqrt{x + f\bigl(-r(y - \tfrac12)\bigr)}\,\eta_x,$$
$$\dot y = -y + f\bigl(r(x - \tfrac12)\bigr) + \frac1{\sqrt V}\sqrt{y + f\bigl(r(x - \tfrac12)\bigr)}\,\eta_y,$$
with independent white noises. For $V \to \infty$ the fixed point is $x^* = y^* = 1/2$, with Jacobian
$$J = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -r/4\\ r/4 & -1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = -1 \pm i\frac r4 .$$
The fixed point is a stable focus: the deterministic model predicts damped oscillations.

### Stochastic amplification

Following Chapter 5, write $x = x^* + \xi_1/\sqrt V$, $y = y^* + \xi_2/\sqrt V$. The linear noise approximation gives $\dot{\boldsymbol\xi} = J\boldsymbol\xi + \boldsymbol\mu$, with diffusion matrix $B = \operatorname{diag}(x^* + f(0), y^* + f(0)) = \mathbb 1$. Its power spectrum is
$$P_1(\omega) = \bigl[(i\omega - J)^{-1}B(-i\omega - J^\top)^{-1}\bigr]_{11} = \frac{1 + r^2/16 + \omega^2}{\bigl(\omega^2 - 1 - r^2/16\bigr)^2 + 4\omega^2}.$$
For $r > 4/\sqrt3 \simeq 2.3$ it has a maximum at a non-zero frequency, approaching $r/4$ for large $r$: for $r = 8$ the peak is at $\omega \simeq 2.0$, about $2.6$ times its zero-frequency value. Intrinsic noise sustains **quasi-cycles** around a stable state, a mechanism proposed for gamma oscillations in cortex. A related point: $J$ is not symmetric (it is *non-normal* when $w_{EI} \neq w_{IE}$), and non-normal dynamics transiently amplify perturbations even when all eigenvalues are stable. This makes fluctuations much larger than the eigenvalues suggest.

### Avalanches in the Wilson–Cowan model

In the variables $\Sigma = x + y$ (total activity) and $\Delta = x - y$ (imbalance), the stochastic model with excitatory–inhibitory balance produces bursts of activity separated by quiescence. Avalanche sizes and durations are power-law distributed, with exponents $\tau \simeq 3/2$, $\alpha \simeq 2$ and $\langle S\rangle(T) \sim T^{\gamma}$, $\gamma \simeq 2$, close to those of a critical branching process. They arise in a regime where the network is *not* at a phase transition but is dominated by inhibition with a weak, noisy excitatory drive (de Santo et al., 2018): another warning that power laws alone do not prove criticality.

### Balanced networks

How do cortical neurons, each receiving thousands of inputs, fire irregularly at low rates? Let each neuron receive $K$ excitatory and $K$ inhibitory inputs, each a Poisson train at rate $\nu_0$, with synaptic strength $W_0$. The mean excitatory and inhibitory currents are $\langle I_E\rangle = KW_0\nu_0$ and $\langle I_I\rangle = -KW_0\nu_0$, while their fluctuations have variance $\propto KW_0^2\nu_0$. If $W_0 = O(1)$, the means diverge with $K$ and the neuron saturates. If $W_0 \sim 1/K$, fluctuations vanish and the neuron is deterministic. With the **balanced scaling** $W_0 = J_0/\sqrt K$, the means are each $O(\sqrt K)$ but nearly cancel, $\langle I_E\rangle + \langle I_I\rangle = O(1)$, and the fluctuations are $O(1)$, $\sigma^2(I) = J_0^2\nu_0$. Neurons operate in a fluctuation-driven regime and fire irregularly. Balance is maintained dynamically by the network (van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky, 1996), possibly helped by homeostatic plasticity.

## Recurrent networks

### Feedforward and recurrent architectures

In a **feedforward** network, output neurons $\mathbf v$ receive inputs $\mathbf u$ through a weight matrix $W$: $\tau_r\dot{\mathbf v} = -\mathbf v + \psi(W\mathbf u)$. In a **recurrent** network the outputs are also coupled to each other by $M$:
$$\tau_r\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = -\mathbf v + \psi(\mathbf h + M\mathbf v), \qquad \mathbf h = W\mathbf u .$$
With separate excitatory and inhibitory populations $M$ has a block structure. Analytical work often assumes $M$ symmetric, which violates Dale's law but can be justified when inhibition is fast. If $\tau_I \to 0$ and $M_{II} = 0$, then $\mathbf v_I = M_{IE}\mathbf v_E$, and the effective excitatory network has the (possibly symmetric) matrix $M_{EE} + M_{EI}M_{IE}$.

### Linear recurrent networks

For a linear activation and symmetric $M$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf e_\mu$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_\mu$, expand $\mathbf v(t) = \sum_\mu c_\mu(t)\mathbf e_\mu$:
$$\tau_r\frac{dc_\mu}{dt} = -(1 - \lambda_\mu)c_\mu + \mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf h .$$
For constant input,
$$c_\mu(t) = \frac{\mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf h}{1 - \lambda_\mu}\Bigl(1 - e^{-t(1 - \lambda_\mu)/\tau_r}\Bigr) + c_\mu(0)\,e^{-t(1 - \lambda_\mu)/\tau_r}.$$

- If some $\lambda_\mu > 1$ the network is unstable: activity grows exponentially.
- If all $\lambda_\mu < 1$, $\mathbf v_\infty = \sum_\mu\frac{\mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf h}{1 - \lambda_\mu}\mathbf e_\mu$: each input component is multiplied by $1/(1 - \lambda_\mu)$. Eigenvalues close to $1$ give **selective amplification** of the corresponding input patterns, and the network acts as a filter that picks out features.
- If $\lambda_1 = 1$ exactly (marginal), the network **integrates** its input: $\tau_r\dot c_1 = \mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf h$, so $c_1(t) = c_1(0) + \frac1{\tau_r}\int_0^t\mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf h(t')dt'$. After a brief pulse, activity persists indefinitely: a **memory**. The oculomotor integrator, which converts bursts of velocity commands into a sustained eye position, is thought to work this way. Its fine tuning ($\lambda_1$ must equal $1$ very precisely) is a classic problem.

::: {.example name="Orientation tuning by recurrence"}
Neurons in visual cortex labelled by their preferred angle $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$, with density $\rho_\theta$, coupled by a translation-invariant kernel $M(\theta - \theta')$:
$$\tau_r\frac{dv(\theta)}{dt} = -v(\theta) + h(\theta) + \rho_\theta\int_{-\pi}^\pi d\theta'\,M(\theta - \theta')\,v(\theta').$$
The eigenfunctions of a convolution on the circle are Fourier modes, $e_0 = (2\pi\rho_\theta)^{-1/2}$, $e_\mu \propto \cos\mu\theta, \sin\mu\theta$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_\mu = \rho_\theta\int M(\theta)\cos(\mu\theta)d\theta$. For $M(\theta) = \frac{\lambda_1}{\pi\rho_\theta}\cos\theta$ only $\mu = 1$ is amplified: a noisy, weakly tuned input becomes a smooth, sharply tuned output peaked at the input's preferred angle. With the rectification $[\cdot]_+$ the network stays stable even for $\lambda_1 > 1$ (e.g. $1.9$), and the tuning curve becomes independent of the input contrast.
:::

## Stochastic recurrent networks

Let each unit be binary, $v_a \in \{0, 1\}$, with total input $I_a = h_a + \sum_{a'}M_{aa'}v_{a'}$, symmetric weights ($M_{aa'} = M_{a'a}$) and $M_{aa} = 0$. At each step a random unit is updated stochastically:
$$P[v_a = 1] = F(I_a) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-I_a}} .$$
This Markov chain (Glauber dynamics) satisfies detailed balance with respect to the Boltzmann distribution
$$P[\mathbf v] = \frac{e^{-E(\mathbf v)}}{Z}, \qquad E(\mathbf v) = -\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf v - \frac12\mathbf v\cdot M\mathbf v,$$
which is therefore its unique stationary distribution. Flipping unit $a$ changes the energy by $\Delta E = -I_a\,\Delta v_a$, and $F(I_a)/(1 - F(I_a)) = e^{I_a}$ is exactly the Boltzmann ratio. A **mean-field** version replaces the units by their average activity, $\tau_s\dot{\mathbf I} = -\mathbf I + \mathbf h + M\mathbf F(\mathbf I)$, with factorised distribution $Q[\mathbf v] = \prod_aF(I_a)^{v_a}(1 - F(I_a))^{1 - v_a}$. The Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{KL}(Q\|P)$ is a Lyapunov function of these dynamics: they converge to the mean-field approximation of $P$. This is the starting point of Boltzmann machines (Chapter 12).

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(E–I pair)** For the example network, (a) verify the fixed point $(26.7, 16.7)$ Hz; (b) show that the Hopf bifurcation occurs at $\tau_I = 40$ ms and find the oscillation frequency there; (c) what happens for $\tau_I = 50$ ms?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $v_E = 1.25v_E - v_I + 10$ and $v_I = v_E - 10$ give $0.25v_E = v_I - 10 = v_E - 20$, so $v_E = 26.7$ and $v_I = 16.7$. (b) $\operatorname{tr}J = 0.025 - 1/\tau_I$ (in ms$^{-1}$) vanishes at $\tau_I = 40$ ms, where $\det J = 0.75/(10\times40) = 1.875\times10^{-3}\,\text{ms}^{-2}$, so $\omega = \sqrt{\det J} = 0.0433\,\text{ms}^{-1} = 43\,\text{s}^{-1}$, i.e. $f = 6.9$ Hz. (c) At $\tau_I = 50$ ms the eigenvalues are $(2.5 \pm 38.6i)\,\text{s}^{-1}$: unstable focus. Trajectories spiral out until the rectification $[\cdot]_+$ clips $v_I$ at zero, producing a stable limit cycle of about $6$ Hz with large amplitude.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Stochastic Wilson–Cowan spectrum)** For the symmetric stochastic Wilson–Cowan model at $x^* = y^* = 1/2$, derive the noise matrix $B$, the power spectrum $P_1(\omega)$, and the condition for a peak at $\omega > 0$. Evaluate for $r = 1, 4, 8$.
:::

::: {.solution}
At the fixed point the activation and decay rates both equal $1/2$ for each population, and $B_{xx} = x^* + f(0) = 1$, $B_{yy} = 1$, $B_{xy} = 0$ (the reactions change one population each). With $J = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & -a\\ a & -1\end{pmatrix}$, $a = r/4$: $|\det(i\omega - J)|^2 = (1 + a^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\omega^2$ and the numerator is $B_{11}(\omega^2 + 1) + B_{22}a^2 = 1 + a^2 + \omega^2$. Writing $c = 1 + a^2$ and $u = \omega^2$, $P_1 = (c + u)/[(c - u)^2 + 4u]$ increases at $u = 0$ iff $3c^2 - 4c > 0$, i.e. $c > 4/3$: a peak at $\omega > 0$ exists iff $a > 1/\sqrt3$, i.e. $r > 4/\sqrt3 \simeq 2.3$. Numerically: $r = 1$, maximum at $\omega = 0$; $r = 4$, a broad peak at $\omega \simeq 0.9$, $1.2$ times $P_1(0)$; $r = 8$, a clear peak at $\omega \simeq 2.0$, $2.6$ times $P_1(0)$. Strong E–I coupling turns noise into quasi-periodic oscillations at the imaginary part $r/4$ of the eigenvalues.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Selective amplification)** A symmetric linear network has eigenvalues $0.9$, $0.5$ and $0$ with eigenvectors $\mathbf e_1$, $\mathbf e_2$, $\mathbf e_3$, and receives $\mathbf h = \mathbf e_1 + \mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_3$. Find $\mathbf v_\infty$ and the relaxation times of each mode with $\tau_r = 10$ ms.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\mathbf v_\infty = \frac{1}{0.1}\mathbf e_1 + \frac1{0.5}\mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_3 = 10\mathbf e_1 + 2\mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_3$. The first pattern is amplified tenfold relative to the input. Relaxation times $\tau_r/(1 - \lambda_\mu)$: $100$ ms, $20$ ms, $10$ ms. Amplification comes with slowing down: modes near $\lambda = 1$ are both large and slow, the hallmark of a system close to an instability (critical slowing down, Chapter 13).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Neural integrator)** With $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\tau_r = 10$ ms, an input pulse $\mathbf e_1\cdot\mathbf h = 5$ Hz lasting $50$ ms is applied from $c_1(0) = 0$. What is $c_1$ after the pulse? If instead $\lambda_1 = 0.99$, how long does the memory last?
:::

::: {.solution}
$c_1 = \frac{1}{10}\times5\times50 = 25$ Hz, and it stays there after the pulse: persistent activity. With $\lambda_1 = 0.99$ the mode decays with time constant $\tau_r/(1 - \lambda_1) = 1$ s, so the memory fades within a second or two. To hold a memory for $\sim 10$ s (as the oculomotor system does) requires $|1 - \lambda_1| \lesssim 10^{-3}$: fine tuning, which motivates models with plasticity or bistable units.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Glauber dynamics and detailed balance)** For the stochastic binary network, show that the update rule $P[v_a = 1] = 1/(1 + e^{-I_a})$ satisfies detailed balance with respect to $P \propto e^{-E}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Let $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf v'$ differ only in unit $a$, with $v_a = 0$ and $v'_a = 1$. Then $E(\mathbf v') - E(\mathbf v) = -h_a - \sum_{a'}M_{aa'}v_{a'} = -I_a$ (using symmetry and $M_{aa} = 0$). The ratio of transition probabilities is $\frac{W(\mathbf v \to \mathbf v')}{W(\mathbf v' \to \mathbf v)} = \frac{F(I_a)}{1 - F(I_a)} = e^{I_a} = e^{-(E(\mathbf v') - E(\mathbf v))} = \frac{P(\mathbf v')}{P(\mathbf v)}$, which is detailed balance. The Boltzmann distribution is stationary, and the chain is ergodic, so it is the unique stationary state.
:::
