4  Multiple species and neutral theory

Real ecosystems host many species at once, and a lot of ecology consists in trying to explain the macroecological patterns — species-abundance distributions, species–area relationships, β-diversity across sites — that are seen empirically in almost every biome. This chapter introduces the classical neutral theory of Hubbell, in which those patterns are generated without any biological interaction: only birth, death and immigration acting on ecologically equivalent species.

4.1 Motivation: from bacteria to forests

A striking empirical fact is that the shape of the species-abundance distribution (SAD) — the histogram of the number of species with \(k\) individuals in a community — is remarkably similar across systems as different as tropical forests, coral reefs, and microbial communities. This suggests either (i) a fine-tuning of biological parameters across all ecosystems (unlikely), or (ii) an emergent statistical mechanism that is largely insensitive to the details.

An ecological community satisfies the neutral hypothesis when all species share identical per-capita birth, death, and dispersal rates. Individuals are drawn from a common label pool but are otherwise interchangeable.

Remark (Physical meaning). The neutral hypothesis is a null model in the same sense that an ideal gas is a null model for a fluid. Every deviation from neutral predictions is a signature of biology (differential fitness, niche structure, competition).

4.2 The Hubbell model

Fix a community of exactly \(J\) individuals. At every time step: one individual dies at random, and it is replaced by (a) a copy of another individual drawn uniformly with probability \(1 - m\), or (b) a fresh migrant drawn from an external metacommunity with probability \(m\). This is a zero-sum Moran-like process with immigration.

In the neutral zero-sum model with metacommunity biodiversity number \(\theta\) and immigration rate \(m\), the expected number of species with \(k\) individuals in the local community of size \(J\) is given by \[\langle S_k \rangle = \theta \, \frac{J!\,\Gamma\bigl(k + I\bigr)\,\Gamma\bigl(J - k + I(1 - m^{-1})\bigr)}{k!\,(J - k)!\,\Gamma\bigl(I\bigr)\,\Gamma\bigl(J + I\bigr)}\cdot \frac{\Gamma\bigl(\theta + I - \theta\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\theta\bigr)} ,\] where \(I = m(J-1)/(1-m)\). The functional form is a zero-sum multinomial whose limiting shape for \(m \to 0\) is Fisher’s log-series, and for \(m \to 1\) is a truncated log-series with heavier abundant-tail.

The result is somewhat unwieldy but its message is clean: two adjustable parameters \((\theta, m)\) are enough to reproduce SADs across many empirical datasets. That is a strong argument for neutral dynamics as an organising principle, though not for its literal truth.

The 50-hectare forest plot on Barro Colorado Island (Panama) contains \(\sim 21{,}000\) trees of \(\sim 300\) species. Fitting the Hubbell SAD to the observed abundance histogram gives \(\theta \approx 50\), \(m \approx 0.09\); the fit is essentially indistinguishable from a much more detailed niche-based model. This is the empirical result that made neutral theory famous.

4.3 Scaling and criticality

The neutral community sits at a critical point in the parameter space: fluctuations in species abundances are power-law-tailed, and the correlation length (the typical range over which two individuals are related) diverges as \(m \to 0\). The physicist’s diagnostic is that observables scale as power laws in system size.

A quantity \(Q\) scales with system size \(L\) if \(Q(L) \sim L^\gamma\) for some exponent \(\gamma\) in the asymptotic regime \(L \to \infty\). Different observables sharing a small number of exponents that satisfy consistency relations define a universality class.

Under neutral dynamics on a lattice with dispersal length \(\ell\), the number of species found in an area \(A\) scales as \[S(A) \sim A^z, \qquad z \approx 0.25 \pm 0.1,\] independent of the microscopic diffusion. The exponent \(z\) is a critical exponent — it depends on spatial dimensionality and the tail of the dispersal kernel, not on the specific species.

The number of species endemic to (i.e. found only in) an area \(A\) scales as \(E(A) \sim A^{z'}\) with \(z' \sim 1 - z\). This is the theoretical prediction that lets conservation biologists estimate how many species would be lost if a patch of habitat of area \(A\) were destroyed.

4.4 Phenomenological renormalisation group

A powerful test that a system is at a critical point is that its macroscopic distribution is scale invariant: coarse-graining leaves the shape of the SAD unchanged, only rescaled. The phenomenological RG procedure of Suweis et al. (2020) is to (i) partition a large empirical community into \(b\) sub-communities of equal size, (ii) compute the SAD of a randomly chosen sub-community, (iii) rescale abundances by \(\langle n \rangle\) and species counts by \(\langle S\rangle\). If the resulting distributions collapse across values of \(b\), the community is scale-invariant — as is observed for tropical forests and reef fish.

If the SAD \(P(n)\) collapses under the coarse-graining \(n \to n/b\), \(P \to b P\), then it is a power law: \(P(n) \sim n^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha\) determined by the way \(\langle n\rangle\) scales with \(b\).

4.5 Inferring biodiversity from presence–absence data

In many ecological surveys the raw datum is not abundance but presence–absence: for each of \(N\) sites and each of \(S\) species, one bit says whether the species was seen at the site. Recovering the full SAD from presence–absence is a classical inference problem.

Under the neutral assumption, the probability that species \(\sigma\) is present at a site of size \(J\) given it has abundance \(n_\sigma\) in the metacommunity is \[p_\sigma^{pres} = 1 - \bigl(1 - n_\sigma / J_M\bigr)^J .\] Inverting the relation with a beta prior on \(n_\sigma / J_M\) recovers the underlying \(\theta\) from presence–absence alone. This is how tropical biodiversity is estimated from herbarium records.

4.6 Local dynamics and migration

Neutral theory can be extended to a spatial community, where individuals disperse over a distance drawn from a kernel \(K(\mathbf x)\). The kinetic equation for the density \(\rho_\sigma(\mathbf x, t)\) of species \(\sigma\) is a spatial birth–death–diffusion process. In the mean-field limit, \[\partial_t \rho_\sigma = D \nabla^2 \rho_\sigma + (b - d)\rho_\sigma - c\,\rho_\sigma\,\sum_{\sigma'} \rho_{\sigma'} + m\,\bar\rho_\sigma ,\] with the last term representing migration from the metacommunity mean \(\bar\rho_\sigma\).

For a single species with \(b > d\), transformed variables allow the extinction time \(T_{ext}\) to be computed as a first-passage problem for a biased random walk. In the large-\(K\) limit, \[\langle T_{ext}\rangle \sim K \exp\bigl(2K(b-d)/(b+d)\bigr),\] i.e. exponential in the carrying capacity — extinction is rare but not impossible.

4.7 Grand canonical formulation

Just as in statistical mechanics one goes from the microcanonical to the canonical to the grand-canonical ensemble by relaxing conservation laws, neutral theory can be reformulated with a fluctuating total community size \(J\). The grand-canonical partition function admits a saddle-point evaluation and reproduces all the closed-form results above from a single generating functional.

Remark. The grand-canonical route is not mere formalism: it is what lets you compare SADs across datasets of different total abundance, and to define intensive quantities (diversity per unit area, species-per-individual ratio) that are the actual observables reported in the field.

4.8 Exercises

The metacommunity in the Hubbell model has \(S_M\) species and total abundance \(J_M\). Show that the fundamental biodiversity number \(\theta = J_M / \ln(J_M/S_M)\) in the limit \(J_M \to \infty\) with \(S_M \to \infty\) fixed by the ratio.

Solution. Assume the metacommunity SAD is a Fisher log-series with parameter \(\theta\): \(\langle S_k\rangle = \theta x^k / k\) where \(x\) is chosen so that \(\sum_k k \langle S_k\rangle = J_M\). Sum the geometric series to obtain \(J_M = \theta x / (1 - x)\) and total species count \(S_M = -\theta \ln(1 - x)\). Eliminating \(x\) under \(x \to 1\) gives \(\theta = J_M / \ln(J_M / S_M)\) to leading order.

Verify that the species–area exponent \(z = 0.25\) predicted by neutral theory in \(d = 2\) is consistent with a random dispersal kernel of Gaussian form. What changes if the kernel is instead long-tailed (fat-tail exponent \(\mu\))?

Solution. With a Gaussian dispersal kernel of variance \(\ell^2\), the effective community size in an area \(A\) is \(A/\ell^2\), and the neutral scaling of the SAD gives \(S \sim (A/\ell^2)^{1/4}\). For a power-law kernel \(K(r) \sim r^{-1-\mu}\) with \(\mu < 2\) the diffusion becomes anomalous and the exponent generalises to \(z = \mu/(2\mu + 4)\), matching observation for oceanic communities where \(\mu \approx 1\).

4.9 Callouts

Neutral theory is a null model. When it fits, we cannot conclude that species are ecologically equivalent — only that whatever differences exist are not visible in the SAD.

When analysing a new biodiversity dataset, always fit the neutral model first. A model of niches, competition, or environmental filtering is only worth building if it out-performs the two-parameter neutral baseline.

Neutral theory correctly predicts SADs but not the correlations between species. When a study reports strong species-species correlations, non-neutral mechanisms are usually needed.

The scaling exponent \(z\) is robust — it takes similar values across datasets — but the fitted \(\theta\) and \(m\) are not: they depend on the sampling scheme. Report both when publishing.
