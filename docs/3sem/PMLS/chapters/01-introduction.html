1  Introduction: living systems as complex systems

Biology offers systems in which many simple units interact: individuals in an ecosystem, neurons in a brain, genes and proteins in a cell. The behaviour of the whole is not written in any single unit, yet it is often surprisingly simple and reproducible across systems that share little microscopic detail. This course uses the tools of statistical physics to explain such regularities: master equations, mean-field limits, phase transitions, random matrices and criticality. This chapter sets out the programme, the empirical patterns we want to explain, and the modelling philosophy behind them.

1.1 Three systems, one framework

The course has three modules, each studied with the same sequence of questions:

Ecological systems Neural networks Genetic circuits
one unit a single species a single neuron a single gene
two units predator and prey excitatory and inhibitory populations a regulator and its target
many units communities, food webs recurrent networks, the whole brain regulatory networks

For each we ask how the units evolve, first deterministically (ordinary differential equations for mean quantities) and then stochastically (master equations, Fokker–Planck and Langevin equations), and which collective, emergent properties appear when many units interact.

A complex system is made of many units whose interactions, rather than whose individual properties, determine the macroscopic behaviour. A property is emergent if it belongs to the system as a whole and cannot be deduced from any single component taken in isolation.

Emergent behaviour would be of little use if it were as intricate as the microscopic details. The reason a physicist can contribute is that, very often, it is not. Interacting units produce simple patterns (power laws, universal distributions, sharp transitions) that recur across systems with completely different microscopic rules. That recurrence suggests common principles that a minimal model can capture. Throughout the course we look for the null model: the simplest mechanism that reproduces an observed pattern. Departures from it then tell us which biological detail actually matters.

The 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared by G. Parisi “for the discovery of the interplay of disorder and fluctuations in physical systems from atomic to planetary scales”, recognised precisely this programme. Several tools of this course (random matrices, replica calculations, spin-glass-like energy landscapes) come from that tradition.

1.2 Two motivating examples

The catch record of Atlantic cod over the twentieth century oscillates with the reproductive cycle and shows a huge peak in the 1960s, driven by industrial fishing. After a smaller secondary maximum, the fishery collapsed abruptly in the early 1990s and never recovered, even after fishing stopped. The collapse is not a smooth decline proportional to the fishing effort. It is a critical transition, a jump between two qualitatively different states of the ecosystem.

Deaths from malaria are distributed very unevenly across the globe. Their statistics are strongly heterogeneous, with a few areas carrying most of the burden. Homogeneous fluctuations are well described by Gaussian statistics with a typical scale. Heterogeneous ones are typically described by power-law (scale-free) distributions, in which no single scale dominates. Which mechanism produces one shape or the other is a recurring theme of the course.

1.3 Emergent ecological patterns

Ecology offers the cleanest examples of universal patterns. They are measured by counting individuals of each species in plots of increasing size.

The SAR \(S(A)\) is the mean number of species found in an area \(A\). Over a wide range of intermediate scales it is well fitted by a power law \[S(A) = c\,A^{z}, \qquad z \approx 0.2\text{–}0.3,\] for plants, birds, amphibians and mammals, on every continent: the curves differ in the prefactor \(c\) but share the shape.

The EAR \(E(A)\) is the number of species found only inside an area \(A\) (endemic to it). It rises slowly at small areas and steeply near the size of the whole region. Since destroying an area \(A\) wipes out the species endemic to it, the EAR is the right tool to estimate extinctions from habitat loss (Chapter 4).

The RSA, or species abundance distribution (SAD), \(P(n)\), is the fraction of species represented by \(n\) individuals. Empirical RSAs have a characteristic shape: many rare species and few very abundant ones. They are classically fitted by Fisher’s log-series or Preston’s log-normal, and neutral theory predicts them from first principles (Chapter 3).

A second celebrated regularity concerns metabolism.

Across organisms whose masses span more than twenty orders of magnitude, from bacteria to whales, the metabolic rate \(B\) scales with body mass \(M\) as \[B = B_0\,M^{a}, \qquad a \simeq 3/4 .\] Many other traits follow from it as power laws of \(M\): lifespan \(\propto M^{1/4}\), heartbeat frequency \(\propto M^{-1/4}\), and population density \(\propto M^{-3/4}\).

Since a \(3/4\) exponent differs from the “surface-to-volume” value \(2/3\), its explanation (West, Brown and Enquist’s optimal transport networks, or Banavar, Maritan and Rinaldo’s argument on supply networks) is a classic problem in biological physics. We return to metabolic scaling and its consequences for coexistence in Chapter 7.

1.4 A short history of population dynamics

Population modelling is as old as quantitative science:

  • Fibonacci (1202) counted rabbits, producing the first growth law with a characteristic ratio.
  • Euler (1748) wrote exponential growth, \(\dot N = rN\).
  • Malthus (1798) argued that unchecked populations grow geometrically while resources grow at most arithmetically.
  • Verhulst (1838) added self-limitation, \(\dot N = rN(1 - N/K)\), the logistic equation, which predicts a steady state.

Chapter 2 shows how these laws can be derived, rather than postulated, from symmetry and from resource dynamics.

1.5 Critical transitions and alternative stable states

The cod collapse illustrates a mechanism we will meet repeatedly: a system can have two stable states for the same environmental conditions, and slow changes in a parameter can make one of them disappear.

A resource (vegetation, fish stock) grows logistically and is consumed by a predator whose consumption saturates: \[\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\Bigl(1 - \frac{N}{K}\Bigr) - c\,\frac{N^2}{N_0^2 + N^2}.\] For small consumption pressure \(c\) there is a single, high-abundance equilibrium. Over an intermediate range of \(c\) a second, low-abundance stable equilibrium appears, separated from the first by an unstable one. Increasing \(c\) beyond a threshold \(c_2\) makes the high branch collide with the unstable branch and vanish (a saddle-node, or fold, bifurcation): the population drops to the low branch. To recover the high state one must reduce \(c\) well below \(c_2\), down to the other fold \(c_1 < c_2\) (hysteresis).

Figure: equilibrium abundance against consumption pressure. Solid: stable branches; dashed: unstable branch. Crossing a fold produces a jump; recovery requires going back to the other fold.

Near a fold the stabilising eigenvalue tends to zero, so perturbations decay more and more slowly. This critical slowing down gives measurable early-warning signals of an approaching collapse: rising variance and rising autocorrelation of fluctuations. Chapter 5 adds the stochastic side of the story, where demographic noise near a weakly stable equilibrium is amplified into large, coherent oscillations.

1.7 Exercises

(Scaling from a SAR) Suppose \(S(A) = cA^z\) with \(z = 0.25\). (a) By what factor does the number of species grow when the area grows tenfold? (b) If a habitat loses half its area, what fraction of species does the SAR predict to be lost? (c) Why is (b) only a rough estimate of extinctions?

Solution.

  1. \(10^{0.25} = 1.78\): a tenfold area holds about \(78\%\) more species. (b) \(S(A/2)/S(A) = 2^{-0.25} = 0.84\), so about \(16\%\) of species are “lost”. (c) The SAR counts species present in a sample of area \(A/2\); species that also live in the destroyed half survive there. The species that disappear are those endemic to the destroyed part, which the EAR measures (Chapter 4). Using the SAR overestimates extinctions caused by habitat loss.

(Kleiber scaling) With \(B \propto M^{3/4}\), compare the metabolic rate per unit mass of a \(3\) g shrew and a \(3000\) kg elephant. How does the energy needed per kilogram compare?

Solution. \(B/M \propto M^{-1/4}\). The mass ratio is \(10^6\), so the shrew needs \((10^6)^{1/4} \approx 32\) times more energy per kilogram than the elephant. Small animals live “faster”: with lifespan \(\propto M^{1/4}\), the total energy spent per kilogram over a lifetime, \(\propto M^{-1/4}M^{1/4}\), is roughly independent of size.

(Fisher’s log-series) Suppose the number of species with \(n\) individuals is \(\phi_n = \theta\,x^n/n\), with \(0 < x < 1\). Compute the number of species \(S\) and individuals \(J\). Show that for \(J \gg \theta\), \(S \simeq \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)\).

Solution. \(S = \sum_{n\geq1}\theta x^n/n = -\theta\ln(1 - x)\) and \(J = \sum_{n\geq1}n\phi_n = \theta\sum_{n\geq1}x^n = \theta\,\frac{x}{1-x}\). From the second, \(1 - x = \theta/(J + \theta)\), so \(S = \theta\ln\bigl(1 + J/\theta\bigr)\), exactly. Species richness grows only logarithmically with the number of sampled individuals. \(\theta\), the fundamental biodiversity number, sets the rate. Neutral theory gives it a meaning (Chapter 3).

(Fold bifurcation) For \(\dot x = r + x^2\), find the equilibria and their stability as a function of \(r\). Show that the relaxation time near the stable equilibrium diverges as \(r \to 0^-\), and give the exponent.

Solution. For \(r < 0\) the equilibria are \(x_\pm = \pm\sqrt{-r}\). The derivative of the right-hand side is \(2x\), so \(x_- = -\sqrt{-r}\) is stable (eigenvalue \(-2\sqrt{-r}\)) and \(x_+\) unstable. For \(r > 0\) there are none. Linearising, perturbations decay as \(e^{-2\sqrt{-r}\,t}\), with relaxation time \(\tau = 1/(2\sqrt{|r|})\), which diverges with exponent \(1/2\). This is critical slowing down. Any noise-driven fluctuations then have a variance \(\propto \tau\) that grows as the fold approaches: an early-warning signal.

(Hysteresis in the grazing model) In May’s grazing model set \(r = N_0 = 1\), so \(\dot N = N(1 - N/K) - cN^2/(1 + N^2)\). (a) Show that the positive equilibria satisfy \(c = c(N) \equiv (1 - N/K)(1 + N^2)/N\). (b) For \(K = 1\) and \(K = 10\), find the range of \(c\) with three positive equilibria.

Solution.

  1. Dividing \(\dot N = 0\) by \(N > 0\) gives \(1 - N/K = cN/(1 + N^2)\). Each horizontal line $c = $ const crosses the curve \(c(N)\) once per equilibrium. (b) For \(K = 1\), \(c(N)\) is monotonically decreasing on \((0, 1)\): one equilibrium for every \(c\), and no bistability. For \(K = 10\), \(c(N)\) has a local minimum \(c_1 \approx 1.79\) (at \(N \approx 1.14\)) and a local maximum \(c_2 \approx 2.60\) (at \(N \approx 4.78\)). For \(c_1 < c < c_2\) there are three positive equilibria: the lowest and highest are stable, the middle one unstable. Alternative stable states need a separation of scales between the carrying capacity \(K\) and the saturation scale \(N_0\) of consumption.
