2  Introduction

This chapter follows the opening of Prof. Suweis’s notes. It uses two motivating examples — the collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery and the geographic distribution of malaria mortality — to introduce the vocabulary the rest of the course will rely on: emergent property, critical transition, power law, and universality.

2.1 Two motivating examples

The record of cod catches in the Atlantic Ocean over the twentieth century shows regular oscillations that track reproduction cycles, plus one enormous peak in the mid-1960s driven by industrial fishing. That peak is followed by a smaller local maximum and then a sharp collapse in the early 1990s — the population never recovered. The abruptness of the collapse (rather than the smooth decrease a naive linear model would predict) is what makes this a critical transition. Explaining such abrupt changes is one of the main threads of the course.

A worldwide map of deaths caused by malaria shows a strongly non-uniform, patchy geography. Two possible statistical models describe such spatial data. A Gaussian model captures approximately uniform distributions with concentrated variance. A power-law model captures strongly heterogeneous distributions with fat tails. Understanding which parameters push a population toward one shape or the other is a recurring question in ecology.

2.2 Emergent ecological patterns

An emergent property of an ecosystem is a universal feature of the system as a whole — not deducible from any single species or component — that arises as a collective behaviour of the entire structure. When the same pattern is observed for many taxa across many continents it typically signals a universal law underlying the property.

Three empirical patterns dominate the field:

The species–area relationship is the empirical scaling \(S(A) \propto A^{z}\), where \(S(A)\) is the number of species observed in an area \(A\) and \(z\) is a dimensionless exponent. The relationship holds across taxa (amphibians, birds, mammals) and across continents; only the multiplicative prefactor changes, not \(z\).

The endemic–area relationship is the analogous scaling \(E(A) \propto A^{z'}\) for the number of endemic species — those found only in the sampled area \(A\). Its exponent \(z'\) is close to \(1 - z\) in most datasets.

The relative species abundance is the empirical distribution of species abundances in a community. Preston’s log-normal and Fisher’s log-series are the two standard fits; either shape is remarkably robust across ecosystems.

Remark. The universality of SAR, EAR and RSA across ecosystems is one of the empirical strongholds of theoretical ecology. It is the reason why neutral theory (Chapter 3) — which reproduces all three patterns from a single mechanism — was received as a paradigm shift when it was proposed in the early 2000s.

2.3 Scope of the course

The scientific goal is threefold: (i) understand why regular patterns emerge in systems with heterogeneous components and noisy dynamics; (ii) identify the fundamental mechanisms responsible; (iii) predict how the patterns respond to perturbations (climate, extinction, disease).

The methodological answer is the toolbox of statistical physics — master equations, mean-field limits, phase transitions, random matrices, criticality — applied to biological systems. Chapters 2–4 build the ecology half of the toolbox, and Chapters 5–10 build the neural systems half.

A physics-of-biology model is likely to be predictive when the system satisfies three conditions:

  1. many degrees of freedom (\(N \gg 1\)),
  2. local, sparse, or mean-field interactions,
  3. some form of noise (thermal, demographic, synaptic) that makes ensemble averages well defined.

2.4 Critical transitions

The cod fishery collapse is one instance of a general phenomenon: some systems undergo qualitative changes in their macroscopic behaviour as a control parameter is varied, and the change can be discontinuous.

A critical transition is a qualitative change in the macroscopic behaviour of a system that occurs at a critical value of some control parameter. At the transition point observables (order parameters, correlation lengths, response functions) show universal scaling, and small perturbations can have disproportionately large effects.

Remark (Physical meaning). Critical transitions in ecology (fishery collapse, forest–savanna shift, coral bleaching) mirror those in physics (magnetisation, superfluid transition). This parallel is not merely rhetorical: near a critical point the systems really are in the same universality class, share the same critical exponents, and can be analysed with the same renormalisation-group tools.

2.5 Book supplements

The single most useful supplementary reference is Volkov, Banavar, Hubbell & Maritan, Neutral theory and relative species abundance in ecology, Nature 424, 1035 (2003), which lays out all three empirical patterns and shows how one mechanism explains them. On critical transitions in ecology, Marten Scheffer’s Critical Transitions in Nature and Society (Princeton, 2009) is a comprehensive treatment.

2.6 Exercises

Verify that a SAR of the form \(S(A) \propto A^z\) implies a species–individual relationship \(S(N) \propto N^{z}\) if abundance is proportional to area. State the assumptions.

Solution. If \(N \propto A\) (density is spatially constant), then substituting \(A \propto N\) into \(S \propto A^z\) gives \(S \propto N^z\) with the same exponent. The result relies on the assumption of spatial homogeneity of the abundance density; when that fails (as in strongly clumped populations) the two exponents diverge.

For the Fisher log-series RSA, the number of species with \(k\) individuals is \(\phi_k = \theta x^k / k\) with parameters \(\theta, x < 1\). Show that the total number of species is \(S = -\theta \ln(1 - x)\) and the total number of individuals is \(J = \theta x/(1 - x)\).

Solution. \(S = \sum_{k \ge 1} \theta x^k/k = -\theta \ln(1 - x)\) (Taylor series of \(\ln\)). \(J = \sum_{k \ge 1} k \phi_k = \theta \sum_{k \ge 1} x^k = \theta x/(1 - x)\) (geometric series). Eliminating \(x\) recovers the fundamental biodiversity number \(\theta = J / \ln(J/S)\) for large \(J/S\).

2.7 Callouts

The introduction is largely phenomenological. Definitions of SAR, EAR, RSA are made precise here so the mathematical developments in Chapters 3–4 can be tested against them.

Whenever you see a scaling law in the field, check its exponent first: universal exponents point to universal mechanisms; system-specific exponents point to system-specific biology. This is the physicist’s first triage step.

The cod-fishery example is a paradigm of a regime shift, not a smooth decline. Statistical tests for regime shifts (Cusum, Bayesian change-point) differ from tests for gradual trends. Applying trend tests to shift data misses the phenomenon.
