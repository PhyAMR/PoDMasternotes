16  Gene regulation and genetic circuits

Genes do not act alone: transcription factors produced by some genes regulate the expression of others, forming circuits with recognisable functions. This chapter builds the standard models of transcriptional regulation from binding equilibria (Hill functions), and analyses the basic network motifs: negative autoregulation, which gives homeostasis, faster responses and noise reduction; delayed negative feedback, which gives oscillations; and positive feedback and mutual repression, which give bistable switches and cellular memory. It ends with a combined transport–metabolism circuit (Homework 6) and the generalisation to whole regulatory networks.

16.1 Regulator dynamics

16.1.1 Binding of a regulator

A transcription factor \(R\) binds an operator site \(O\) near a promoter: \(R + O \underset{k_-}{\overset{k_+}{\rightleftharpoons}} RO\). At equilibrium \[\frac{[R][O]}{[RO]} = \frac{k_-}{k_+} = K,\] the dissociation constant (the concentration of \(R\) at which the site is occupied half the time). Since the total number of sites is fixed, \([O] + [RO] = [O_T]\), the occupied fraction is \[f_B([R]) = \frac{[RO]}{[O_T]} = \frac{[R]}{K + [R]}, \qquad f_U = 1 - f_B = \frac{K}{K + [R]}.\]

Often \(h\) regulator molecules bind cooperatively (e.g. as a multimer or at several adjacent sites). For \(hR + O \rightleftharpoons R_hO\) the occupied fraction becomes the Hill function \[f_B = \frac{(R/K)^h}{1 + (R/K)^h}, \qquad f_U = \frac{1}{1 + (R/K)^h},\] with Hill coefficient \(h\) (typically \(1\)–\(4\)). The larger \(h\), the more switch-like the response: for \(h \to \infty\) it becomes a step at \(R = K\).

16.1.2 Activators and repressors

If \(R\) is an activator, the gene is transcribed when the operator is occupied, and its production rate is \(\gamma f_B\). If \(R\) is a repressor, transcription happens when the operator is free, at rate \(\gamma f_U\). Including a basal “leaky” production \(l\) and removal (degradation and dilution) with lifetime \(\tau\), a protein \(X\) regulated by \(R\) obeys \[\frac{dX}{dt} = l + \frac{\gamma}{1 + (R/K)^h} - \frac X\tau \quad\text{(repression)}, \qquad \frac{dX}{dt} = l + \gamma\frac{(R/K)^h}{1 + (R/K)^h} - \frac X\tau \quad\text{(activation)}.\] This is a deterministic, mean-field description. Chapter 14 showed how noise is added.

16.2 Negative autoregulation

16.2.1 Homeostasis

The most common motif in E. coli (about \(40\%\) of transcription factors) is negative autoregulation (NAR): a repressor represses its own gene, \[\frac{dR}{dt} = l + \frac{\gamma}{1 + (R/K)^h} - \frac R\tau .\] For \(h = 1\) the steady state solves a quadratic, \[R^* = \frac12\Bigl[l\tau - K + \sqrt{K^2 + \tau(l^2\tau + 2Kl + 4K\gamma)}\Bigr],\] and for \(l = 0\), \(R^* \simeq \sqrt{K\gamma\tau}\) when \(\gamma\tau \gg K\). Without regulation \(R^* = (l + \gamma)\tau\), proportional to the production rate \(\gamma\), which depends on the environment (growth conditions, polymerase availability). With NAR it grows only as \(\sqrt\gamma\) for \(h = 1\), and more slowly for larger \(h\): as \(h \to \infty\), \(R^* \to K\) whatever \(\gamma\). The protein level becomes robust to changes in the environment.

With \(l = 0.1\), \(K = \tau = 1\), increasing \(\gamma\) from \(1\) to \(100\) changes the unregulated level from \(1.1\) to \(100\), the \(h = 1\) level from \(0.69\) to \(9.6\), and the \(h = 3\) level only from \(0.78\) to \(3.2\).

16.2.2 Faster responses

NAR also speeds up the response. A gene switched on at \(t = 0\) without regulation reaches half its final level in \(t_{1/2} = \tau\ln2\), independently of the production rate. With NAR the production rate is initially at its maximum (no repressor yet) and is then throttled as \(R\) approaches \(K\), so the steady state is reached much faster. With \(l = 0.5\), \(\gamma = 5\), \(K = \tau = 1\), the time to reach half of \(R^*\) is \(0.34\), \(0.22\), \(0.14\), \(0.12\) for \(h = 1, 2, 5, 8\), against \(0.69\) without regulation. NAR also reduces noise, because fluctuations above the set point lower production and fluctuations below it raise it.

16.2.3 Stress response

Negative feedback through a metabolite gives adaptive stress responses. Let a repressor \(R\) (constant total \(R^*\)) be inactivated by a signal molecule \(S\), \(R_{\text{free}} = R^*/(1 + S)\), and repress an enzyme \(E\) that removes \(S\): \[\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{1 + R_{\text{free}}^2} - \frac{E}{\tau_E}, \qquad \frac{dS}{dt} = \gamma_r - \alpha ES .\] A sudden increase of the stress input \(\gamma_r\) raises \(S\), frees the gene from repression, and produces the enzyme that brings \(S\) back down. The response is a transient peak followed by adaptation.

16.2.4 Delayed negative feedback: oscillations

Transcription, translation, maturation and transport take time. If the repression acts with a delay \(t_d\), \[\frac{dR}{dt} = l + \frac{\gamma}{1 + \bigl(R(t - t_d)/K\bigr)^h} - \frac R\tau,\] the system overshoots: by the time the repressor acts, too much has been made, then too little. For a sufficiently long delay and steep repression the fixed point loses stability through a Hopf bifurcation and sustained oscillations appear. With \(l = 0.1\), \(\gamma = 10\), \(K = \tau = 1\), \(h = 4\): for \(t_d \leq 0.5\) the level settles at \(1.56\); for \(t_d = 1\) it oscillates between \(0.9\) and \(3.5\); for \(t_d = 2\) between \(0.5\) and \(7.5\). Delayed negative feedback underlies circadian clocks, the p53–Mdm2 oscillations after DNA damage, and the synthetic repressilator (three genes repressing each other in a ring; Elowitz and Leibler, 2000).

16.3 Positive feedback and bistability

16.3.1 The toggle switch

Two repressors \(R\) and \(L\) that repress each other form a double-negative, i.e. effectively positive, feedback loop: \[\frac{dR}{dt} = l_R + \frac{\gamma}{1 + L^{h_L}} - \frac{R}{\tau_R}, \qquad \frac{dL}{dt} = l_L + \frac{\gamma}{1 + R^{h_R}} - \frac{L}{\tau_L},\] in units where \(K = 1\). For \(h = 1\) there is a single symmetric steady state. For \(h > 1\) and strong enough repression there are three: two stable states, one with \(R\) high and \(L\) low and one with the opposite, separated by an unstable symmetric state. For example, with \(l = 0\), \(\gamma = 4\), \(\tau = 1\), \(h = 2\) the steady states are \((3.73, 0.27)\), \((0.27, 3.73)\) and \((1.38, 1.38)\).

The circuit is a genetic switch: a transient signal that pushes the system across the separatrix flips it to the other state, which then persists after the signal is gone. The cell has a memory of past signals, inherited by its daughters, an epigenetic state not encoded in the DNA sequence. The phage λ lysis–lysogeny decision is the classic natural example (the cI and Cro repressors), and Gardner, Cantor and Collins (2000) built a synthetic toggle switch in E. coli. Cooperativity (\(h > 1\)) is essential: without it the nullclines cross only once.

16.3.2 Self-activation

A gene that activates itself, \(\dot X = l + \gamma\frac{X^h}{1 + X^h} - X/\tau\), is bistable for \(h > 1\) in a range of parameters: an “off” state at \(X \simeq l\tau\) and an “on” state near \((l + \gamma)\tau\). Positive feedback converts graded inputs into all-or-none, irreversible decisions (differentiation, sporulation, competence), and noise can trigger spontaneous switching between the states (Chapter 14).

16.4 Combining loops: transport and metabolism

Real circuits combine both kinds of feedback. Consider a cell taking up a nutrient \(S\) (e.g. lactose) through a transport protein \(T\) and consuming it with an enzyme \(E\), both repressed by a regulator \(R\) that is inactivated by \(S\), \(R_{\text{free}} = R^*/(1 + S)\): \[\frac{dS}{dt} = \sigma T - \gamma ES - S, \qquad \frac{dT}{dt} = l + \frac{1}{1 + (R_{\text{free}}/K_T)^h} - T, \qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = l + \frac{1}{1 + (R_{\text{free}}/K_E)^h} - E .\] The transport loop is positive: more \(S\) frees more repressor, which makes more transporter, which imports more \(S\). The metabolic loop is negative: more \(S\) makes more enzyme, which consumes \(S\). The positive loop makes the response to the external supply \(\sigma\) steep and possibly bistable (the “all-or-none” induction of the lac operon observed by Novick and Weiner, 1957). The negative loop keeps the internal concentration of \(S\) in check. With \(R^* = 10\), \(K_T = K_E = 1\), \(h = 2\), \(l = 0.1\), \(\gamma = 1\): for \(\sigma = 1\) and \(10\) the circuit stays almost uninduced (\(T = E \simeq 0.11\)–\(0.15\)), while for \(\sigma = 100\) it is fully induced (\(T = E \simeq 1.07\), \(S \simeq 52\)).

16.5 From motifs to networks

For many genes the same approach gives a generalised dynamics \[\frac{dx_i}{dt} = -x_i + \sum_{j=1}^NA_{ij}\frac{x_j^h}{1 + x_j^h},\] where \(A_{ij}\) encodes the gene regulatory network (activation \(A_{ij} > 0\), repression \(A_{ij} < 0\)). This is formally a recurrent neural network (Chapter 10) or a generalised Lotka–Volterra system (Chapter 6). Its many stable states can be identified with cell types, an idea going back to Kauffman and formalised as a Hopfield network whose attractors are cell identities (Waddington’s epigenetic landscape). Chapter 17 asks the inverse question: how to infer \(A\) from measured expression patterns.

A transient pulse of inducer that lowers one repressor flips the switch permanently.

(HW 6.2) Combining loops. Combine two feedback loops (for instance the transport–metabolism network shown in class), simulate it, and describe what happens.

Solution. With the model of the text (\(R^* = 10\), \(K_T = K_E = 1\), \(h = 2\), \(l = 0.1\), \(\gamma = 1\)), integrating to steady state for increasing supply: \(\sigma = 1\), \((S, T, E) = (0.10, 0.11, 0.11)\); \(\sigma = 10\), \((1.31, 0.15, 0.15)\); \(\sigma = 100\), \((51.6, 1.07, 1.07)\). At low supply the repressor is mostly free and the genes are off. When enough nutrient enters, it titrates the repressor, transporter and enzyme are induced, and uptake jumps (positive loop), while the enzyme limits the accumulation of \(S\) (negative loop). The induction is switch-like in \(\sigma\). Making the transporter more sensitive than the enzyme (\(K_T < K_E\)) shifts the induction to lower supplies and can produce bistability and hysteresis: cells grown with the nutrient remain induced at supplies that cannot induce naive cells, a memory of past exposure.

(Hill functions) Show that the steepness of the Hill function at \(R = K\) is \(\frac{d f_B}{d\ln R} = h/4\), and compute the fold change in \(R\) needed to go from \(10\%\) to \(90\%\) occupancy.

Solution. \(f_B = \frac{x^h}{1 + x^h}\) with \(x = R/K\), so \(\frac{df_B}{d\ln R} = h f_B(1 - f_B) = h/4\) at \(x = 1\). \(f_B = 0.1\) at \(x^h = 1/9\) and \(0.9\) at \(x^h = 9\), so \(x_{90}/x_{10} = 81^{1/h}\): \(81\)-fold for \(h = 1\), \(9\)-fold for \(h = 2\), \(3\)-fold for \(h = 4\). Cooperativity turns a gradual response into a switch.

(Bistability of self-activation) For \(\dot X = \frac{X^2}{1 + X^2} - \frac X\tau\) (no leak, \(\gamma = K = 1\)), find the steady states and the range of \(\tau\) for bistability.

Solution. \(X = 0\) is always a steady state. Non-zero ones satisfy \(\frac{X}{1 + X^2} = \frac1\tau\), i.e. \(X^2 - \tau X + 1 = 0\), so \(X_\pm = \frac{\tau \pm\sqrt{\tau^2 - 4}}{2}\), real for \(\tau > 2\). For \(\tau > 2\) there are three states: \(0\) (stable), \(X_-\) (unstable) and \(X_+\) (stable). The cell is bistable once the protein lives long enough to sustain its own production. With \(h = 1\) instead, \(\frac{X}{1 + X} = \frac X\tau\) has only \(X = \tau - 1\) besides \(0\), so there is no bistability.

With the model of the text ($R^* = 10$, $K_T = K_E = 1$, $h = 2$, $l = 0.1$, $\gamma = 1$), integrating to steady state for increasing supply: $\sigma = 1$, $(S, T, E) = (0.10, 0.11, 0.11)$; $\sigma = 10$, $(1.31, 0.15, 0.15)$; $\sigma = 100$, $(51.6, 1.07, 1.07)$. At low supply the repressor is mostly free and the genes are off. When enough nutrient enters, it titrates the repressor, transporter and enzyme are induced, and uptake jumps (positive loop), while the enzyme limits the accumulation of $S$ (negative loop). The induction is switch-like in $\sigma$. Making the transporter more sensitive than the enzyme ($K_T < K_E$) shifts the induction to lower supplies and can produce bistability and hysteresis: cells grown with the nutrient remain induced at supplies that cannot induce naive cells, a memory of past exposure.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Hill functions)** Show that the steepness of the Hill function at $R = K$ is $\frac{d f_B}{d\ln R} = h/4$, and compute the fold change in $R$ needed to go from $10\%$ to $90\%$ occupancy.
:::

::: {.solution}
$f_B = \frac{x^h}{1 + x^h}$ with $x = R/K$, so $\frac{df_B}{d\ln R} = h f_B(1 - f_B) = h/4$ at $x = 1$. $f_B = 0.1$ at $x^h = 1/9$ and $0.9$ at $x^h = 9$, so $x_{90}/x_{10} = 81^{1/h}$: $81$-fold for $h = 1$, $9$-fold for $h = 2$, $3$-fold for $h = 4$. Cooperativity turns a gradual response into a switch.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Bistability of self-activation)** For $\dot X = \frac{X^2}{1 + X^2} - \frac X\tau$ (no leak, $\gamma = K = 1$), find the steady states and the range of $\tau$ for bistability.
:::

::: {.solution}
$X = 0$ is always a steady state. Non-zero ones satisfy $\frac{X}{1 + X^2} = \frac1\tau$, i.e. $X^2 - \tau X + 1 = 0$, so $X_\pm = \frac{\tau \pm\sqrt{\tau^2 - 4}}{2}$, real for $\tau > 2$. For $\tau > 2$ there are three states: $0$ (stable), $X_-$ (unstable) and $X_+$ (stable). The cell is bistable once the protein lives long enough to sustain its own production. With $h = 1$ instead, $\frac{X}{1 + X} = \frac X\tau$ has only $X = \tau - 1$ besides $0$, so there is no bistability.
:::
