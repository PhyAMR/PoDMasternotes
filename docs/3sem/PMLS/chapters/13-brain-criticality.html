13  Whole-brain models and brain criticality

Can the large-scale anatomy of the brain predict its spontaneous activity? And why would the brain operate close to a phase transition? This chapter combines the two questions. It starts from the theory of critical branching processes, which explains the exponents of neuronal avalanches. It then presents a minimal stochastic whole-brain model, the Haimovici–Tagliazucchi–Chialvo (HTC) model, which runs on the human connectome measured by DTI and reproduces resting-state functional connectivity best near its critical point. A mean-field analysis with the tools of Part I shows the transition. Applications to individual subjects and stroke patients suggest that criticality may be a biomarker of brain health.

13.1 The criticality hypothesis

Many systems with many interacting components show collective phases, and changes in parameters (temperature, coupling) can induce phase transitions between them. At a continuous transition, correlations extend over the whole system, fluctuations are maximal, responses to perturbations are largest, and observables follow power laws with universal exponents. The criticality hypothesis proposes that the brain, and perhaps other living systems, is poised near such a point, because criticality would confer:

  • a large dynamic range: sensitivity to stimuli spanning many orders of magnitude (Kinouchi and Copelli, 2006);
  • maximal information transmission and storage;
  • a rich repertoire of metastable states, and fast, coordinated global responses.

The experimental support comes from two observations: scale-free avalanches, with sizes distributed as \(s^{-3/2}\) and durations as \(T^{-2}\), found in cortical slices, rats, monkeys and humans (MEG, EEG, fMRI); and long-range temporal correlations in the amplitude fluctuations of neural oscillations (\(1/f\) spectra). Chapter 8 showed that power laws alone are not proof: what matters is the full set of critical signatures (exponent relations, data collapse, scaling with system size).

13.2 Critical branching processes

The simplest model of activity propagation is a branching process. Each active unit independently activates \(k\) units in the next time step, with \(k\) drawn from an offspring distribution \(p_k\) with mean \(\sigma\), the branching ratio. An avalanche starts from one active unit and ends when activity dies out.

  • \(\sigma < 1\) (subcritical): activity dies quickly; avalanche sizes have an exponential cut-off.
  • \(\sigma > 1\) (supercritical): with finite probability activity explodes.
  • \(\sigma = 1\) (critical): activity always dies out, but avalanches of every size occur.

At \(\sigma = 1\), with finite offspring variance, the size and duration distributions decay as \[P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}, \qquad P(T) \sim T^{-2},\] and the mean size of avalanches of duration \(T\) scales as \(\langle s\rangle_T \sim T^{2}\).

Sketch. Let \(g(z) = \sum_kp_kz^k\) be the offspring generating function and \(Q(z) = \sum_sP(s)z^s\) that of the total size. An avalanche is the root plus independent sub-avalanches from each child, so \(Q(z) = z\,g(Q(z))\). Near \(z = 1\), with \(g(1) = 1\), \(g'(1) = 1\) and \(g''(1) = b > 0\), expand \(Q = 1 - \epsilon\): \(1 - \epsilon \simeq z(1 - \epsilon + \frac b2\epsilon^2)\), so \(\epsilon \simeq \sqrt{2(1 - z)/b}\). A square-root singularity of \(Q\) at \(z = 1\) corresponds to \(P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}\). For durations, the survival probability \(u_t\) obeys \(1 - u_{t+1} = g(1 - u_t)\), so \(u_{t+1} \simeq u_t - \frac b2u_t^2\), giving \(u_t \simeq 2/(bt)\) and a duration density \(\sim t^{-2}\). \(\square\)

These are the mean-field exponents observed by Beggs and Plenz. They satisfy the crackling-noise relation \(\frac{\alpha - 1}{\tau - 1} = \gamma\), where \(P(s) \sim s^{-\tau}\), \(P(T) \sim T^{-\alpha}\) and \(\langle s\rangle_T \sim T^\gamma\): \((2 - 1)/(3/2 - 1) = 2\). Experimental tests of criticality check this relation and the collapse of avalanche shapes, not only the exponents. The branching ratio can be estimated from data as the mean number of active units following each active unit; values close to \(1\) are found in cortical recordings, with refinements needed for subsampling.

13.3 Whole-brain models

13.3.1 From structure to function

Structural connectivity (SC): DTI tractography gives a weighted network \(W_{ij}\) between \(N\) cortical regions, its weights proportional to the number of fibres. Functional connectivity (FC): the Pearson correlation matrix of resting-state BOLD signals, \[F_{ij} = \frac{\langle x_ix_j\rangle - \langle x_i\rangle\langle x_j\rangle}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}.\] FC is organised into resting-state networks (default mode, visual, sensorimotor, attention…) that are reproducible across subjects. Whole-brain models put a dynamical system on each node, couple the nodes through the connectome, and compare simulated with empirical FC. “Make everything as simple as possible, but not simpler”: the HTC model uses the simplest possible node dynamics.

13.3.2 The HTC model

Each node \(i\) is in one of three states: quiescent \(Q\), excited \(E\) (written \(s_i = 1\)), or refractory \(R\) (\(s_i = 0\) for \(Q\) and \(R\)). Time is discrete and updates are synchronous, following a stochastic Greenberg–Hastings excitable automaton: \[Q \to E \;\text{ if } \sum_jW_{ij}s_j > T, \text{ or spontaneously with probability } r_1; \qquad E \to R \;\text{ with probability } 1; \qquad R \to Q \;\text{ with probability } r_2 .\] A node fires if enough of its neighbours fire (weighted by the connectome) or spontaneously. It then becomes refractory and recovers after a geometric time with mean \(1/r_2\). The threshold \(T\) is the control parameter. Typical values are \(r_1 = 2/N\) (rare spontaneous activation) and \(r_2 = r_1^{1/5}\).

Observables: the mean activity \(\langle A\rangle\), with \(A(t) = \frac1N\sum_is_i(t)\), its standard deviation \(\sigma(A)\), and the average sizes of the largest and second-largest clusters \(\langle S_1\rangle\), \(\langle S_2\rangle\). Clusters are sets of structurally connected nodes that are simultaneously active.

13.3.3 Two phases and a transition

  • Small \(T\) (supercritical): even weak connections propagate activity. Activity is high and sustained, fluctuations are fast and uncorrelated, and one giant cluster dominates.
  • Large \(T\) (subcritical): only the strongest connections transmit. Activity is low and sparse, made of short-lived local events.
  • Intermediate \(T_c\): a continuous transition. \(\langle S_1\rangle\) drops, \(\sigma(A)\) peaks and \(\langle S_2\rangle\) peaks, the signature of a percolation-like transition, used to locate \(T_c\). At \(T_c\) the cluster-size distribution is a truncated power law, \(P(S) \sim S^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \simeq 3/2\).

Comparing with data: the Pearson correlation between simulated and empirical FC, \(\rho(F_m, F_e)\), and the similarity of simulated resting-state networks to empirical templates are both maximal near \(T_c\). A model of the healthy brain matches its spontaneous activity best at criticality.

13.3.4 Homeostatic plasticity

In the raw model, activity is dominated by hubs, nodes with large total input strength \(W_i = \sum_jW_{ij}\). Rocha et al. (2018) proposed a homeostatic normalisation that equalises the input of every region, \[\widetilde W_{ij} = \frac{W_{ij}}{\sum_jW_{ij}},\] so every node has unit in-strength, as if synapses had adjusted to keep each region’s excitability constant. With \(\widetilde W\):

  • the transition becomes sharper and the \(\langle S_2\rangle\) peak more pronounced;
  • the cluster-size power law extends about three times further (cut-off \(\sim 15\) instead of \(\sim 5\)) with \(\alpha = 3/2\) (against \(\alpha \simeq 1.75\) without normalisation);
  • the match with empirical FC improves by a factor of about \(1.5\) at the group level and up to \(2\) for individuals.

13.3.5 Personalised models

Using the structural and functional matrices of \(43\) healthy subjects (\(N = 264\) regions, Rudie et al., 2012), each individual connectome can be simulated. Without normalisation each subject has a different critical threshold, but the ratio \(T_c/\langle W\rangle \simeq 0.16\) is nearly constant, as predicted by mean field (\(T_c\) scales with the mean weight). With normalisation all subjects collapse onto the same curves with \(T_c \simeq 0.15\): homeostasis makes the critical point universal across individuals. This makes personalised modelling feasible.

13.3.6 Stroke and recovery

Applied to stroke patients (Corbetta et al., 2015), personalised models show that lesions push the brain away from criticality. Activity and cluster statistics of patients deviate from those of controls, with a weaker transition (lower \(\sigma(A)\) and \(\langle S_2\rangle\) peaks). During recovery, from 3 months to 1 year after the stroke, patients move back toward critical behaviour. The changes in these criticality measures correlate with the recovery of behavioural scores across subjects (Rocha et al., 2022). Criticality is a candidate biomarker of healthy brain dynamics.

13.4 Mean-field theory of the HTC model

13.4.1 Master equation

Consider the fully connected, normalised case, \(\widetilde W_{ij} = 1/N\). The input to a node is the fraction of active nodes, \(\sum_j\widetilde W_{ij}s_j - T = x - T\). Let \(n_x\), \(n_y\) be the numbers of excited and refractory nodes (\(n_Q = N - n_x - n_y\)). In continuous time the transitions are \[(n_x, n_y) \to (n_x + 1, n_y) \;\text{at rate } (N - n_x - n_y)\,r_{\text{act}}, \qquad r_{\text{act}} = r_1 + (1 - r_1)\Theta(x - T),\] \[(n_x, n_y) \to (n_x - 1, n_y + 1) \;\text{at rate } n_x, \qquad (n_x, n_y) \to (n_x, n_y - 1) \;\text{at rate } r_2n_y .\] With densities \(x = n_x/N\), \(y = n_y/N\), the Kramers–Moyal expansion (Chapters 2 and 5) gives a Fokker–Planck equation with drift and diffusion \[A_1 = (1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}} - x, \qquad A_2 = x - r_2y,\] \[B_{11} = (1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}} + x, \qquad B_{22} = r_2y + x, \qquad B_{12} = B_{21} = -x,\] the off-diagonal term coming from the \(E \to R\) reaction, which moves one unit from \(x\) to \(y\).

13.4.2 Equilibria in the thermodynamic limit

For \(N \to \infty\), \(\dot x = A_1\), \(\dot y = A_2\). Two regimes arise from the step function.

  • Supercritical branch (\(x > T\), \(r_{\text{act}} = 1\)): \(y_+ = \frac{1}{2r_2 + 1}\), \(x_+ = r_2y_+\). It exists iff \(x_+ > T\), i.e. \(T < T_+ \equiv \frac{r_2}{2r_2 + 1}\). The activity is independent of \(r_1\): it is self-sustained by interactions.
  • Subcritical branch (\(x \leq T\), \(r_{\text{act}} = r_1\)): \(y_- = \frac{r_1}{r_2 + (r_2 + 1)r_1}\), \(x_- = r_2y_-\). It exists iff \(T \geq T_- \equiv x_-\).

Since \(r_1 < 1\), \(T_- < T_+\). Three regions follow: for \(T < T_-\) only the active phase exists, for \(T > T_+\) only the quiescent phase, and for \(T_- < T < T_+\) both are stable, each with its own basin of attraction. In mean field the transition is therefore discontinuous (first order), with hysteresis: increasing \(T\) the system jumps down at \(T_+\), decreasing it jumps up at \(T_-\). On real, sparse and heterogeneous connectomes the transition is smoothed into the continuous-looking transition seen in simulations.

13.4.3 Stability

At the supercritical point, \[J_+ = \begin{pmatrix}-2 & -1\\ 1 & -r_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_+ = \frac{-(2 + r_2) \pm\sqrt{r_2^2 - 4r_2}}{2}:\] always stable, a focus for \(r_2 < 4\) and a node for \(r_2 > 4\). At the subcritical point, \[J_- = \begin{pmatrix}-1 - r_1 & -r_1\\ 1 & -r_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_- = \frac{-(1 + r_1 + r_2) \pm\sqrt{(1 + r_1 + r_2)^2 - 4(r_1 + r_2 + r_1r_2)}}{2}:\] always stable, and a focus iff \(r_1 - 2\sqrt{r_1} + 1 < r_2 < r_1 + 2\sqrt{r_1} + 1\).

13.4.4 Fluctuations and oscillations

Around a stable equilibrium the linear noise approximation gives \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} = J\boldsymbol\zeta + \boldsymbol\eta\), with \(\langle\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\eta^\top\rangle = B\) evaluated at the fixed point, and the power spectrum \[P_x(\omega) = \frac{\alpha + \beta\omega^2}{(\omega^2 - \Omega_0^2)^2 + \Gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \alpha = B_{11}J_{22}^2 - 2B_{12}J_{12}J_{22} + B_{22}J_{12}^2, \quad \beta = B_{11}, \quad \Omega_0^2 = \det J, \quad \Gamma = -\operatorname{tr}J,\] the formula of Chapter 5. In the fully connected model the foci are strongly damped (\(\operatorname{Re}\lambda \simeq -1\)) and spectral peaks are weak. Network structure changes this: on connectomes and random graphs, near the critical threshold the model shows collective oscillations and a peak in the power spectrum. Activity propagates as waves on Erdős–Rényi graphs and as localised patterns on the connectome. Criticality and structure jointly drive emergent oscillations (Barzon et al., 2022).

13.5 Exercises

(Branching ratio) In a branching process each active neuron activates each of \(K = 10\) downstream neurons independently with probability \(p\). (a) What is the branching ratio? (b) For which \(p\) is the process critical? (c) For \(p = 0.08\), what is the mean avalanche size?

Solution.

  1. \(\sigma = Kp\). (b) \(p_c = 1/K = 0.1\). (c) For \(\sigma < 1\) the mean total size is \(\sum_t\sigma^t = 1/(1 - \sigma) = 1/(1 - 0.8) = 5\) neurons. It diverges as \(\sigma \to 1\) with exponent \(1\), the susceptibility of this mean-field transition.

(Critical avalanche sizes) For binary branching (each active unit has \(0\) or \(2\) offspring with probability \(1/2\)), solve \(Q(z) = z\,g(Q(z))\) exactly and verify \(P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}\).

Solution. \(g(z) = \frac12(1 + z^2)\), so \(Q = \frac z2(1 + Q^2)\), i.e. \(zQ^2 - 2Q + z = 0\) and \(Q(z) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - z^2}}{z}\), taking the root regular at \(z = 0\). Expanding \(\sqrt{1 - z^2} = 1 - \sum_{k\geq1}\frac{(2k - 3)!!}{2^kk!}z^{2k}\) gives non-zero probabilities only for odd sizes, \(P(s = 2k - 1) = \frac{(2k - 3)!!}{2^kk!}\). By Stirling \((2k - 3)!!/(2^kk!) \sim \frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}k^{-3/2}\), so \(P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}\). The square-root singularity of \(Q\) at \(z = 1\) is the generating-function signature of the exponent \(3/2\).

(HTC mean field) For \(r_1 = r_2 = 0.1\), compute \(x_\pm\), \(T_\pm\) and the nature of both equilibria. What happens to the activity if \(T\) is slowly increased from \(0\) to \(0.1\) and then decreased back?

Solution. \(y_+ = 1/1.2 = 0.833\), \(x_+ = 0.0833 = T_+\); \(y_- = 0.1/(0.1 + 0.11) = 0.476\), \(x_- = 0.0476 = T_-\). \(J_+\) has eigenvalues \(-1.05 \pm 0.31i\) (stable focus); \(J_-\) has \(-0.99\) and \(-0.21\) (stable node, since \(r_2 = 0.1\) is outside \((1 - 2\sqrt{0.1} + 0.1,\ 1 + 2\sqrt{0.1} + 0.1) = (0.47, 1.73)\)). Increasing \(T\) from \(0\), the system stays on the active branch (\(x \simeq 0.083\)) until \(T_+ = 0.083\), then drops to \(x_- \simeq 0.048\). Decreasing \(T\), it stays low until \(T_- = 0.048\) and then jumps back up. The hysteresis loop spans \([0.048, 0.083]\), the region of bistability.

(Homeostatic normalisation) Why does the normalisation \(\widetilde W_{ij} = W_{ij}/\sum_jW_{ij}\) make the critical threshold the same for all subjects in mean field, and what does it change for hubs?

Solution. After normalisation every node’s total input weight is \(1\), so a node’s input is a weighted average of its neighbours’ states, bounded by \(1\), whatever the subject’s overall fibre density. In mean field the activation condition is \(x > T\) for every node and every subject, hence a universal \(T_c\). Without normalisation the input of node \(i\) is \(\sim W_ix\), so \(T_c \propto \langle W\rangle\), which differs between subjects (hence the constant ratio \(T_c/\langle W\rangle\)). For hubs, normalisation reduces their dominance: before it, high-strength nodes are almost always active and enslave the dynamics; after it, every region contributes similarly, which enriches the repertoire of patterns and improves agreement with functional data.

(Diffusion matrix of the HTC model) Derive \(B\) from the three reactions and evaluate it at the supercritical equilibrium for \(r_2 = 0.1\).

Solution. Reactions with state changes and rates per \(N\): activation \((+1, 0)\) at \((1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}}\); excited to refractory \((-1, +1)\) at \(x\); recovery \((0, -1)\) at \(r_2y\). Then \(B = \sum_j\mathbf v_j\mathbf v_j^\top a_j\): \(B_{11} = (1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}} + x\), \(B_{22} = x + r_2y\), \(B_{12} = -x\). At \(x_+ = 0.083\), \(y_+ = 0.833\), \(r_{\text{act}} = 1\): \(B_{11} = 0.083 + 0.083 = 0.167\), \(B_{22} = 0.083 + 0.083 = 0.167\), \(B_{12} = -0.083\). The negative correlation reflects that each \(E \to R\) event lowers \(x\) and raises \(y\) together.

- the match with empirical FC improves by a factor of about $1.5$ at the group level and up to $2$ for individuals.

### Personalised models

Using the structural and functional matrices of $43$ healthy subjects ($N = 264$ regions, Rudie et al., 2012), each individual connectome can be simulated. Without normalisation each subject has a different critical threshold, but the ratio $T_c/\langle W\rangle \simeq 0.16$ is nearly constant, as predicted by mean field ($T_c$ scales with the mean weight). With normalisation all subjects collapse onto the same curves with $T_c \simeq 0.15$: homeostasis makes the critical point universal across individuals. This makes personalised modelling feasible.

### Stroke and recovery

Applied to stroke patients (Corbetta et al., 2015), personalised models show that lesions push the brain away from criticality. Activity and cluster statistics of patients deviate from those of controls, with a weaker transition (lower $\sigma(A)$ and $\langle S_2\rangle$ peaks). During recovery, from 3 months to 1 year after the stroke, patients move back toward critical behaviour. The changes in these criticality measures correlate with the recovery of behavioural scores across subjects (Rocha et al., 2022). Criticality is a candidate **biomarker** of healthy brain dynamics.

## Mean-field theory of the HTC model

### Master equation

Consider the fully connected, normalised case, $\widetilde W_{ij} = 1/N$. The input to a node is the fraction of active nodes, $\sum_j\widetilde W_{ij}s_j - T = x - T$. Let $n_x$, $n_y$ be the numbers of excited and refractory nodes ($n_Q = N - n_x - n_y$). In continuous time the transitions are
$$(n_x, n_y) \to (n_x + 1, n_y) \;\text{at rate } (N - n_x - n_y)\,r_{\text{act}}, \qquad r_{\text{act}} = r_1 + (1 - r_1)\Theta(x - T),$$
$$(n_x, n_y) \to (n_x - 1, n_y + 1) \;\text{at rate } n_x, \qquad (n_x, n_y) \to (n_x, n_y - 1) \;\text{at rate } r_2n_y .$$
With densities $x = n_x/N$, $y = n_y/N$, the Kramers–Moyal expansion (Chapters 2 and 5) gives a Fokker–Planck equation with drift and diffusion
$$A_1 = (1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}} - x, \qquad A_2 = x - r_2y,$$
$$B_{11} = (1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}} + x, \qquad B_{22} = r_2y + x, \qquad B_{12} = B_{21} = -x,$$
the off-diagonal term coming from the $E \to R$ reaction, which moves one unit from $x$ to $y$.

### Equilibria in the thermodynamic limit

For $N \to \infty$, $\dot x = A_1$, $\dot y = A_2$. Two regimes arise from the step function.

- **Supercritical branch** ($x > T$, $r_{\text{act}} = 1$): $y_+ = \frac{1}{2r_2 + 1}$, $x_+ = r_2y_+$. It exists iff $x_+ > T$, i.e. $T < T_+ \equiv \frac{r_2}{2r_2 + 1}$. The activity is independent of $r_1$: it is self-sustained by interactions.
- **Subcritical branch** ($x \leq T$, $r_{\text{act}} = r_1$): $y_- = \frac{r_1}{r_2 + (r_2 + 1)r_1}$, $x_- = r_2y_-$. It exists iff $T \geq T_- \equiv x_-$.

Since $r_1 < 1$, $T_- < T_+$. Three regions follow: for $T < T_-$ only the active phase exists, for $T > T_+$ only the quiescent phase, and for $T_- < T < T_+$ **both** are stable, each with its own basin of attraction. In mean field the transition is therefore discontinuous (first order), with **hysteresis**: increasing $T$ the system jumps down at $T_+$, decreasing it jumps up at $T_-$. On real, sparse and heterogeneous connectomes the transition is smoothed into the continuous-looking transition seen in simulations.

### Stability

At the supercritical point,
$$J_+ = \begin{pmatrix}-2 & -1\\ 1 & -r_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_+ = \frac{-(2 + r_2) \pm\sqrt{r_2^2 - 4r_2}}{2}:$$
always stable, a focus for $r_2 < 4$ and a node for $r_2 > 4$. At the subcritical point,
$$J_- = \begin{pmatrix}-1 - r_1 & -r_1\\ 1 & -r_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_- = \frac{-(1 + r_1 + r_2) \pm\sqrt{(1 + r_1 + r_2)^2 - 4(r_1 + r_2 + r_1r_2)}}{2}:$$
always stable, and a focus iff $r_1 - 2\sqrt{r_1} + 1 < r_2 < r_1 + 2\sqrt{r_1} + 1$.

### Fluctuations and oscillations

Around a stable equilibrium the linear noise approximation gives $\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} = J\boldsymbol\zeta + \boldsymbol\eta$, with $\langle\boldsymbol\eta\boldsymbol\eta^\top\rangle = B$ evaluated at the fixed point, and the power spectrum
$$P_x(\omega) = \frac{\alpha + \beta\omega^2}{(\omega^2 - \Omega_0^2)^2 + \Gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \alpha = B_{11}J_{22}^2 - 2B_{12}J_{12}J_{22} + B_{22}J_{12}^2, \quad \beta = B_{11}, \quad \Omega_0^2 = \det J, \quad \Gamma = -\operatorname{tr}J,$$
the formula of Chapter 5. In the fully connected model the foci are strongly damped ($\operatorname{Re}\lambda \simeq -1$) and spectral peaks are weak. Network structure changes this: on connectomes and random graphs, near the critical threshold the model shows collective oscillations and a peak in the power spectrum. Activity propagates as waves on Erdős–Rényi graphs and as localised patterns on the connectome. Criticality and structure jointly drive emergent oscillations (Barzon et al., 2022).

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(Branching ratio)** In a branching process each active neuron activates each of $K = 10$ downstream neurons independently with probability $p$. (a) What is the branching ratio? (b) For which $p$ is the process critical? (c) For $p = 0.08$, what is the mean avalanche size?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $\sigma = Kp$. (b) $p_c = 1/K = 0.1$. (c) For $\sigma < 1$ the mean total size is $\sum_t\sigma^t = 1/(1 - \sigma) = 1/(1 - 0.8) = 5$ neurons. It diverges as $\sigma \to 1$ with exponent $1$, the susceptibility of this mean-field transition.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Critical avalanche sizes)** For binary branching (each active unit has $0$ or $2$ offspring with probability $1/2$), solve $Q(z) = z\,g(Q(z))$ exactly and verify $P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$g(z) = \frac12(1 + z^2)$, so $Q = \frac z2(1 + Q^2)$, i.e. $zQ^2 - 2Q + z = 0$ and $Q(z) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - z^2}}{z}$, taking the root regular at $z = 0$. Expanding $\sqrt{1 - z^2} = 1 - \sum_{k\geq1}\frac{(2k - 3)!!}{2^kk!}z^{2k}$ gives non-zero probabilities only for odd sizes, $P(s = 2k - 1) = \frac{(2k - 3)!!}{2^kk!}$. By Stirling $(2k - 3)!!/(2^kk!) \sim \frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}k^{-3/2}$, so $P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}$. The square-root singularity of $Q$ at $z = 1$ is the generating-function signature of the exponent $3/2$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(HTC mean field)** For $r_1 = r_2 = 0.1$, compute $x_\pm$, $T_\pm$ and the nature of both equilibria. What happens to the activity if $T$ is slowly increased from $0$ to $0.1$ and then decreased back?
:::

::: {.solution}
$y_+ = 1/1.2 = 0.833$, $x_+ = 0.0833 = T_+$; $y_- = 0.1/(0.1 + 0.11) = 0.476$, $x_- = 0.0476 = T_-$. $J_+$ has eigenvalues $-1.05 \pm 0.31i$ (stable focus); $J_-$ has $-0.99$ and $-0.21$ (stable node, since $r_2 = 0.1$ is outside $(1 - 2\sqrt{0.1} + 0.1,\ 1 + 2\sqrt{0.1} + 0.1) = (0.47, 1.73)$). Increasing $T$ from $0$, the system stays on the active branch ($x \simeq 0.083$) until $T_+ = 0.083$, then drops to $x_- \simeq 0.048$. Decreasing $T$, it stays low until $T_- = 0.048$ and then jumps back up. The hysteresis loop spans $[0.048, 0.083]$, the region of bistability.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Homeostatic normalisation)** Why does the normalisation $\widetilde W_{ij} = W_{ij}/\sum_jW_{ij}$ make the critical threshold the same for all subjects in mean field, and what does it change for hubs?
:::

::: {.solution}
After normalisation every node's total input weight is $1$, so a node's input is a weighted average of its neighbours' states, bounded by $1$, whatever the subject's overall fibre density. In mean field the activation condition is $x > T$ for every node and every subject, hence a universal $T_c$. Without normalisation the input of node $i$ is $\sim W_ix$, so $T_c \propto \langle W\rangle$, which differs between subjects (hence the constant ratio $T_c/\langle W\rangle$). For hubs, normalisation reduces their dominance: before it, high-strength nodes are almost always active and enslave the dynamics; after it, every region contributes similarly, which enriches the repertoire of patterns and improves agreement with functional data.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Diffusion matrix of the HTC model)** Derive $B$ from the three reactions and evaluate it at the supercritical equilibrium for $r_2 = 0.1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Reactions with state changes and rates per $N$: activation $(+1, 0)$ at $(1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}}$; excited to refractory $(-1, +1)$ at $x$; recovery $(0, -1)$ at $r_2y$. Then $B = \sum_j\mathbf v_j\mathbf v_j^\top a_j$: $B_{11} = (1 - x - y)r_{\text{act}} + x$, $B_{22} = x + r_2y$, $B_{12} = -x$. At $x_+ = 0.083$, $y_+ = 0.833$, $r_{\text{act}} = 1$: $B_{11} = 0.083 + 0.083 = 0.167$, $B_{22} = 0.083 + 0.083 = 0.167$, $B_{12} = -0.083$. The negative correlation reflects that each $E \to R$ event lowers $x$ and raises $y$ together.
:::
