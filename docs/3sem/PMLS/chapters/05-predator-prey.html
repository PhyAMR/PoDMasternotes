5  Two interacting species: predators, prey and noise

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

Introducing a single new species into an ecosystem (wolves into Yellowstone, a predatory fish into a lagoon) can reorganise the whole community through a trophic cascade. To understand how, we start with two species. This chapter treats the predator–prey problem twice. Deterministically, the Lotka–Volterra equations and their variants explain damped or sustained oscillations. Stochastically, a microscopic individual-based model shows that demographic noise, far from being a small correction, is resonantly amplified into large, persistent cycles. The machinery developed here (reaction schemes, multivariate master equations, the van Kampen system-size expansion, power spectra) is reused for neurons (Chapter 10) and for genes (Chapter 16).

5.1 The Lotka–Volterra equations

5.1.1 Historical model and its conserved quantity

Lotka (1920) and Volterra (1926) wrote, independently, \[\dot x = ax - bxy, \qquad \dot y = -cy + dxy,\] for prey \(x\) and predators \(y\). Volterra was prompted by his son-in-law, the biologist Umberto D’Ancona, who had noticed that the fraction of predatory fish in the markets of Trieste, Fiume and Venice rose during the First World War, when fishing stopped. Prey grow exponentially at rate \(a\) when alone; predators die at rate \(c\) without prey; encounters (mass action, rate \(\propto xy\)) convert prey into predators.

Besides the trivial equilibrium, there is a coexistence point \((x^*, y^*) = (c/d, a/b)\). The model has a conserved quantity \[H(x, y) = dx - c\ln x + by - a\ln y, \qquad \dot H = 0,\] so trajectories are closed orbits around \((x^*, y^*)\), whose amplitude is fixed by the initial condition: neutral cycles. The Jacobian at \((x^*, y^*)\) has purely imaginary eigenvalues \(\pm i\sqrt{ac}\), giving small oscillations of period \(2\pi/\sqrt{ac}\). This is a structurally unstable situation: any perturbation of the equations (self-limitation, saturation, noise) turns the centres into spirals.

Volterra’s model also explains D’Ancona’s observation. Fishing adds mortality \(f\) to both species, \(a \to a - f\) and \(c \to c + f\), so the time-averaged abundances (equal to the equilibrium values on a closed orbit) become \(\langle x\rangle = (c + f)/d\), \(\langle y\rangle = (a - f)/b\). Less fishing means fewer prey and more predators: Volterra’s principle.

5.1.2 Self-limited prey

Adding logistic self-limitation to the prey gives \[\dot x = \lambda x\Bigl(1 - \frac xk\Bigr) - pxy, \qquad \dot y = -\eta y + pxy .\] The equilibria are \(z_1^* = (0, 0)\), \(z_3^* = (k, 0)\) and the coexistence point \[z_2^* = \Bigl(\frac\eta p,\ \frac\lambda p\Bigl(1 - \frac{\eta}{pk}\Bigr)\Bigr),\] which is feasible (positive) iff \(pk > \eta\): the prey at carrying capacity must sustain the predators. The Jacobian \(J = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda(1 - 2x/k) - py & -px\\ py & px - \eta\end{pmatrix}\) gives:

  • at \(z_1^*\), eigenvalues \(\lambda > 0\) and \(-\eta\): a saddle (unstable);
  • at \(z_3^*\), eigenvalues \(-\lambda\) and \(pk - \eta > 0\): a saddle, unstable when coexistence is feasible;
  • at \(z_2^*\), \[J_2 = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\lambda\eta}{kp} & -\eta\\ \lambda\bigl(1 - \frac{\eta}{pk}\bigr) & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vartheta_{1,2} = \frac{1}{2kp}\Bigl[-\lambda\eta \pm \sqrt{\lambda\eta\bigl(\lambda\eta - 4kp(kp - \eta)\bigr)}\Bigr].\]

Since \(\operatorname{tr}J_2 < 0\) and \(\det J_2 = \lambda\eta(1 - \eta/pk) > 0\), \(z_2^*\) is always stable. For large \(k\) the discriminant is negative and the eigenvalues are complex: populations approach the equilibrium through damped oscillations with frequency \(\simeq\sqrt{\lambda\eta(1 - \eta/pk)}\) and damping rate \(\lambda\eta/(2kp)\), which is small when \(k\) is large.

5.1.3 From biological assumptions to stability: a worked example

Instead of starting from a given equation, build the model from what is known. An ecosystem contains prey \(N_1(t)\) and predators \(N_2(t)\):

  • without predators, prey grow logistically with rate \(a_1 = 1\) and carrying capacity \(K = 100\);
  • without prey, predators decline exponentially with rate \(a_2 = -0.5\);
  • predator–prey encounters are random, so their frequency is proportional to \(N_1N_2\);
  • the coefficients controlling prey loss and predator gain are \(\alpha = \beta = 2\).

Is there a state in which prey and predators coexist, and with which abundances? If the system sits there and the predators are suddenly increased by \(5\%\), what happens next, and what mathematical information is needed to say?

The simplest model consistent with these assumptions is \[\dot N_1 = a_1N_1\Bigl(1 - \frac{N_1}{K}\Bigr) - \frac\alpha KN_1N_2, \qquad \dot N_2 = a_2N_2 + \frac\beta KN_1N_2 .\] Dividing the encounter terms by \(K\) keeps \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) on the same time scale as \(a_1\) and \(a_2\). In general \(\alpha \neq \beta\), because one prey eaten does not make exactly one new predator. In the densities \(x = N_1/K\), \(y = N_2/K\), \[\dot x = x\bigl[a_1(1 - x) - \alpha y\bigr], \qquad \dot y = y\bigl[a_2 + \beta x\bigr],\] which for our numbers reads \(\dot x = x(1 - x - 2y)\), \(\dot y = y(-0.5 + 2x)\).

Fixed points. Besides extinction, \((0, 0)\), and the prey-only state \((1, 0)\) (i.e. \(N_1 = K\), \(N_2 = 0\)), coexistence with \(x^*, y^* > 0\) requires \(a_2 + \beta x^* = 0\) and \(a_1(1 - x^*) = \alpha y^*\): \[x^* = -\frac{a_2}{\beta} = 0.25, \qquad y^* = \frac{a_1}{\alpha}\Bigl(1 + \frac{a_2}{\beta}\Bigr) = 0.375,\] i.e. \(N_1^* = 25\) prey and \(N_2^* = 37.5\) predators. Abundances are continuous variables in a deterministic model, so a non-integer value is not a problem. Finding the fixed point answers where can the system remain stationary? It does not answer what happens after a small perturbation?

TipConcept check: fixed point versus stability

Two predator–prey models have exactly the same coexistence equilibrium \((x^*, y^*)\). Is this enough to conclude that they respond in the same way to a small perturbation around it?

  • A. Yes, because the equilibrium populations are identical.
  • B. Yes, provided that the perturbation is sufficiently small.
  • C. No: the response also depends on the local derivatives of the dynamics around the equilibrium.
  • D. No prediction is possible without solving the complete nonlinear dynamics.

Answer: C. A fixed point specifies the location of a stationary state. The dynamics near it depends on how the vector field changes around it, that is, on the Jacobian. Option B is tempting, but small perturbations are exactly the ones governed by the Jacobian, which can differ between models with the same fixed point. Option D goes too far: for small perturbations the linearisation is enough, unless an eigenvalue has zero real part.

Stability. As in Chapter 2, write \(\mathbf x = \mathbf x^* + \delta\mathbf x\). The local expansion gives \(\dot{\delta\mathbf x} = J^*\delta\mathbf x\), with the Jacobian in the role that \(F'(N^*)\) played for one species: \[J(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} a_1(1 - 2x) - \alpha y & -\alpha x\\ \beta y & a_2 + \beta x\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J^* = \begin{pmatrix} -0.25 & -0.5\\ 0.75 & 0\end{pmatrix}.\] The eigenvalues solve \(\det(J^* - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 0.25\,\lambda + 0.375 = 0\): \[\lambda_\pm = -0.125 \pm 0.599\,i \equiv -\gamma \pm i\omega_0 .\] Since \(\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm < 0\), perturbations decay. Since \(\operatorname{Im}\lambda_\pm \neq 0\), the return is oscillatory. The coexistence point is a stable focus, with \(\delta\mathbf x(t) \sim e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)\), damping rate \(\gamma \simeq 0.125\) and angular frequency \(\omega_0 \simeq 0.599\).

After the \(5\%\) increase in predators, \(y(0) = 1.05\,y^*\) and \(x(0) = x^*\), the populations do not return monotonically. The extra predators first depress the prey, which bottom out at \(N_1 \simeq 23.9\) around \(t \simeq 2.3\). The predators then decline, the prey recover, and so on. The oscillation has period \(2\pi/\omega_0 \simeq 10.5\), and the predator excess shrinks by a factor \(e^{-2\pi\gamma/\omega_0} \simeq 0.27\) per cycle (a numerical integration of the full nonlinear equations agrees, Figure 5.1). A fixed point does not determine its own stability; the Jacobian does.

5.1.4 Real cycles

Real predator–prey systems often oscillate for decades: the snowshoe hare and lynx pelts of the Hudson’s Bay Company, rodents, plankton in chemostats. The deterministic model above predicts that the oscillations die out. There are two classical ways out.

  1. Limit cycles. If predators saturate (Holling type II functional response, \(pxy \to \frac{pxy}{1 + hx}\)), the coexistence equilibrium can lose stability through a Hopf bifurcation when the carrying capacity is increased. A stable limit cycle is born, with amplitude independent of initial conditions. Enriching the environment destabilises it: Rosenzweig’s paradox of enrichment (1971).
  2. Stochastic amplification. Even when the deterministic equilibrium is stable, the discreteness of individuals produces noise that excites the weakly damped oscillatory mode. The result is large, irregular but persistent cycles with a well-defined dominant frequency. This mechanism requires no fine tuning, and is the subject of the rest of the chapter.

5.2 From reactions to master equations

The deterministic predator–prey equations bundle several biological processes: prey reproduction, limitation of prey growth, predator mortality, encounters, and predator reproduction associated with predation. Real populations are finite, and each process happens as discrete random events, not as a continuous flow. To turn a term into an event, identify the individuals involved, the change it produces in predator and prey numbers, and how its rate depends on the current abundances. The deterministic equations do not determine this microscopic description uniquely.

Individual-based models are best written as reaction schemes, as in chemistry. Consider \(N_s\) types of “molecules” (individuals of different species, or empty sites) and \(M\) reactions \(R_j\). Each reaction has:

  • a state-change vector \(\mathbf v_j\): the change in the vector of abundances \(\mathbf x\) when \(R_j\) fires (e.g. \(B \to A + A\) has \(\mathbf v = (+2, -1)\));
  • a propensity \(a_j(\mathbf x)\): \(a_j\,\Delta t\) is the probability that \(R_j\) fires in \([t, t + \Delta t]\).

For a unimolecular reaction \(A \to \cdots\) with rate constant \(C_j\), \(a_j = C_jx_A\). For a bimolecular one, \(A + B \to \cdots\), \(a_j = C_jx_Ax_B/\Omega\), where \(\Omega\) is the system size (volume, number of sites). Two molecules must meet, and in a larger volume they are less likely to. In general, a reaction involving \(k\) reactants has \(a_j \propto x_1\cdots x_k/\Omega^{k-1}\), so propensities are extensive: \(a_j = \Omega\,\alpha_j(\mathbf x/\Omega)\). The master equation balances inflow from \(\mathbf x - \mathbf v_j\) and outflow from \(\mathbf x\): \[\frac{\partial P(\mathbf x, t)}{\partial t} = \sum_{j=1}^M\bigl[a_j(\mathbf x - \mathbf v_j)P(\mathbf x - \mathbf v_j, t) - a_j(\mathbf x)P(\mathbf x, t)\bigr],\] and can be sampled exactly with the Gillespie algorithm of Chapter 2.

5.3 A stochastic predator–prey model

5.3.1 Microscopic rules

Following McKane and Newman (2005), consider \(N\) sites, each empty (\(E\)), occupied by a predator (\(A\)) or by a prey (\(B\)). Let \(n\), \(m\) and \(e = N - n - m\) be their numbers. Individuals interact as if well mixed (spatial effects neglected), through five reactions: \[A \xrightarrow{d_1} E, \quad B + E \xrightarrow{b} B + B, \quad B \xrightarrow{d_2} E, \quad A + B \xrightarrow{p_2} A + E, \quad A + B \xrightarrow{p_1} A + A .\] Predators die. Prey reproduce into empty sites and die. Predation either just removes the prey (\(p_2\)) or also converts it into a new predator (\(p_1\)). Choosing pairs at random, with a factor \(2\) from the two orderings, the propensities are \[T(n - 1, m \mid n, m) = d_1n, \qquad T(n, m + 1 \mid n, m) = 2b\,\frac{m\,e}{N},\] \[T(n, m - 1 \mid n, m) = d_2m + 2p_2\frac{nm}{N}, \qquad T(n + 1, m - 1 \mid n, m) = 2p_1\frac{nm}{N}.\] This is one microscopic realisation of the ecological ingredients, not the only one. Numerical factors from the pair-sampling rule are a convention and can be absorbed into the rate constants. The 2026 lectures (Suweis 2026), for instance, write \(b\,me/N\), \(p_1nm/N\) and \(p_2nm/N\) without the factor \(2\); every formula below then holds with \(2b \to b\), \(2p_i \to p_i\).

5.3.2 Drift and diffusion

With densities \(x = n/N\) (predators) and \(y = m/N\) (prey), each propensity is \(N\) times a function of \((x, y)\). Expanding the master equation to second order in \(1/N\) (with shift operators \(\mathbb E^\pm_A\), \(\mathbb E^\pm_B\) as in Chapter 2) gives a two-dimensional Fokker–Planck equation \[\partial_tP = -\sum_i\partial_i\bigl[A_iP\bigr] + \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\bigl[B_{ij}P\bigr].\] The drift is the vector of average changes: \[A_1 = 2p_1xy - d_1x, \qquad A_2 = 2by(1 - x - y) - d_2y - 2(p_1 + p_2)xy .\] The diffusion matrix collects, for each reaction, \(\mathbf v_j\mathbf v_j^\top\) weighted by its rate: \[B = \begin{pmatrix} d_1x + 2p_1xy & -2p_1xy\\ -2p_1xy & 2by(1 - x - y) + d_2y + 2(p_1 + p_2)xy\end{pmatrix}.\] The off-diagonal element is negative because the conversion reaction changes \(n\) and \(m\) in opposite directions, \(\mathbf v = (+1, -1)\). Noise in the two populations is correlated.

As \(N \to \infty\) the noise vanishes and the drift gives the mean-field equations \[\dot x = 2p_1xy - d_1x, \qquad \dot y = ry\Bigl(1 - \frac yk\Bigr) - 2(p_1 + p_2 + b)\,xy, \qquad r = 2b - d_2, \quad k = \frac{2b - d_2}{2b},\] a predator–prey system with logistic prey. Expanding \(2by(1 - x - y) = 2by(1 - y) - 2bxy\) shows where the logistic term comes from. Because reproduction needs an empty site, the same reaction generates both prey self-limitation and part of the effective predator–prey interaction. Microscopic events produce effective deterministic interactions, and the mapping onto Lotka–Volterra parameters depends on the microscopic realisation. Its interior equilibrium \[y^* = \frac{d_1}{2p_1}, \qquad x^* = \frac{2b(1 - y^*) - d_2}{2(b + p_1 + p_2)}\] is a stable focus, so the mean-field dynamics predicts damped oscillations and no cycles.

5.3.3 Langevin equations

The equivalent Langevin system is From biological assumptions to stability: a worked example\(\dot x = A_1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_jg_{1j}\xi_j, \qquad \dot y = A_2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_jg_{2j}\xi_j, \qquad \langle\xi_i(t)\xi_j(t')\rangle = \delta_{ij}\delta(t - t'),\)$ where \(g\) is any matrix with \(gg^\top = B\). For instance \(g = S^\top\Lambda^{1/2}S\) from the diagonalisation \(B = S^\top\Lambda S\), or the Cholesky factor of \(B\). Only \(B\) is physical; different square roots give the same statistics.

5.3.4 The deterministic model does not determine the noise

For reactions \(r\) with scaled rates \(w_r(\mathbf x)\) and state changes \(\boldsymbol\nu_r\), the drift and the diffusion matrix are (see the exercises) \[\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \sum_r\boldsymbol\nu_r\,w_r(\mathbf x), \qquad B(\mathbf x) = \sum_r\boldsymbol\nu_r\boldsymbol\nu_r^\top\,w_r(\mathbf x).\] The drift depends only on the net effect of all channels. The noise depends on each channel separately. Different reaction schemes can therefore produce the same deterministic dynamics but different fluctuations. In the model above, predation with conversion is a single event, \(\boldsymbol\nu = (+1, -1)\), which makes \(B_{12} < 0\). Suppose instead that the predator gain and the prey loss occurred as two separate events, with the same rate \(2p_1xy\) and \(\boldsymbol\nu = (+1, 0)\) and \((0, -1)\). The drift would be unchanged, but \(B_{12}\) would vanish: noise in the two populations would be uncorrelated, and the power spectra below would change. This is the multidimensional version of the logistic example of Chapter 2, where splitting the same net growth into births and deaths in different ways changed the variance by a factor \(b/d\): \[\text{same deterministic dynamics} \;\not\Rightarrow\; \text{same stochastic dynamics}.\]

5.4 The van Kampen expansion and stochastic amplification

5.4.1 Linear noise approximation

Fluctuations around the deterministic equilibrium are \(O(N^{-1/2})\). Following van Kampen, write \[x(t) = x^* + \frac{\zeta_1(t)}{\sqrt N}, \qquad y(t) = y^* + \frac{\zeta_2(t)}{\sqrt N}.\] Substituting and keeping the leading order, the drift contributes the Jacobian \(J = \partial\mathbf A/\partial\mathbf x\) at \((x^*, y^*)\), and the noise amplitude is frozen at its equilibrium value: \[\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} = J\boldsymbol\zeta + \boldsymbol\eta(t), \qquad \langle\eta_i(t)\eta_j(t')\rangle = B_{ij}(x^*, y^*)\,\delta(t - t').\] This is a multivariate Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. The fluctuations are Gaussian, of size independent of \(N\) in the rescaled variables, and their statistics follow from linear algebra. This is the linear noise approximation. It is exact to leading order in \(1/\sqrt N\) around a stable fixed point.

The Jacobian has two related roles. For a single deterministic perturbation it gives decay and oscillation, as in the worked example. With demographic noise it also controls the response to a continuous stream of random kicks, i.e. the fluctuation spectrum. The noise matrix \(B\) is evaluated at the fixed point and depends on the microscopic reactions.

TipConcept check: what does noise do to a stable focus?

The deterministic coexistence point is a stable focus, \(\lambda_\pm = -\gamma \pm i\omega_0\) with \(\gamma > 0\). Consider a large but finite population with demographic noise. What does a typical stochastic trajectory look like?

  • A. It converges exactly to the coexistence point and stays there.
  • B. It fluctuates randomly around coexistence, with no preferred time scale.
  • C. It shows noisy but persistent oscillations, with a characteristic frequency related to \(\omega_0\).
  • D. Any amount of demographic noise makes the coexistence point deterministically unstable.

Answer: C. The fixed point remains deterministically stable (so D is wrong). Noise keeps pushing the system away from it (so A is wrong) and repeatedly excites its damped oscillatory mode. The response is therefore not featureless (so B is wrong): it is concentrated near \(\omega_0\), as the power spectrum shows.

5.4.2 The power spectrum

Without noise, a perturbation of the stable focus behaves as \(e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)\) and disappears. With demographic noise, new perturbations are generated all the time, and the system responds to each of them through the same oscillatory mode. A damped oscillatory mode plus continuous noise gives persistent stochastic oscillations, called stochastic quasi-cycles. Their signature is best seen in the frequency domain.

Fourier transforming, \((i\omega - J)\tilde{\boldsymbol\zeta}(\omega) = \tilde{\boldsymbol\eta}(\omega)\) with \(\langle\tilde\eta_i(\omega)\tilde\eta^*_j(\omega')\rangle = B_{ij}\,2\pi\delta(\omega - \omega')\). Hence \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\zeta}(\omega) = H(\omega)\tilde{\boldsymbol\eta}(\omega)\), where \(H(\omega) = (i\omega - J)^{-1}\) is the linear response function. The power spectrum \(P_i(\omega) = \langle|\tilde\zeta_i(\omega)|^2\rangle\) (per unit frequency) is the diagonal of the spectral matrix \[\mathcal P(\omega) = H(\omega)\,B\,H^\dagger(\omega) = (i\omega - J)^{-1}\,B\,(-i\omega - J^\top)^{-1}.\] For two variables, with \(\Omega_0^2 = \det J\) and \(\Gamma = -\operatorname{tr}J\), the denominator is \(|\det(i\omega - J)|^2 = (\omega^2 - \Omega_0^2)^2 + \Gamma^2\omega^2\), and solving the \(2\times2\) system, \[P_1(\omega) = \frac{\alpha_1 + B_{11}\,\omega^2}{(\omega^2 - \Omega_0^2)^2 + \Gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \alpha_1 = B_{11}J_{22}^2 - 2B_{12}J_{12}J_{22} + B_{22}J_{12}^2,\] and similarly for \(P_2\) with indices exchanged. For our model \(J_{11} = 0\) at equilibrium (the predator equation is \(\dot x = x(2p_1y - d_1)\)), so \(\Omega_0^2 = J_{12}|J_{21}|\) and \(\Gamma = |J_{22}|\).

This is the response of a damped harmonic oscillator with natural frequency \(\Omega_0\) and damping \(\Gamma\), driven by white noise. When \(\Gamma \ll \Omega_0\) (weak damping, as for a large carrying capacity) the spectrum has a sharp peak near \(\Omega_0\), of height \(\propto 1/(\Gamma^2\Omega_0^2)\). Demographic noise has power at all frequencies; the system filters it and amplifies the frequency of its own damped mode. The deterministic dynamics acts as a frequency-selective filter, and the result is resonant amplification of demographic noise, without external forcing and without fine tuning. Do not confuse it with stochastic resonance in the strict sense. That term usually describes how a noisy nonlinear system responds to an additional external periodic forcing, and here there is no forcing at all.

Take \(b = 0.1\), \(d_1 = 0.1\), \(d_2 = 0\), \(p_1 = 0.25\), \(p_2 = 0.05\). Then \(x^* = y^* = 0.2\) and \[J = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0.10\\ -0.16 & -0.04\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix}0.040 & -0.020\\ -0.020 & 0.048\end{pmatrix}.\] The eigenvalues are \(-0.02 \pm 0.125i\): a weakly damped focus with \(\Omega_0 = 0.126\), \(\Gamma = 0.04\). The predator spectrum peaks at \(\omega \simeq 0.125\), about \(27\) times higher than at \(\omega = 0\). A direct simulation of the linear Langevin equations gives a peak at \(\omega \simeq 0.12\). Gillespie simulations of the full model with \(N\) in the thousands show noisy cycles with this period, \(2\pi/0.125 \approx 50\) time units, and amplitude \(\propto 1/\sqrt N\) in density, i.e. \(\propto\sqrt N\) in numbers of individuals.

Stochastic amplification is generic: whenever a stable fixed point of a large but finite system is a focus with weak damping, intrinsic noise produces quasi-cycles. The same mechanism explains oscillations in epidemics (measles, whooping cough), in biochemical networks (Chapter 16) and in neural populations (Chapter 10), where “noise-driven gamma oscillations” appear in Wilson–Cowan models.

5.4.3 Local stability is not persistence

Quasi-cycles do not mean that coexistence lasts forever in a finite population. The deterministic coexistence point can be locally stable while the stochastic process still reaches an absorbing boundary, \(N_1 = 0\) or \(N_2 = 0\). The cycles have an amplitude of order \(\sqrt N\) individuals around abundances of order \(N\). In small systems, or when the focus is weakly damped and the cycles are large, a trough of the cycle can touch zero and one species goes extinct. Local stability and extinction are different questions. The first is answered by the Jacobian. The second is a first-passage problem, like the persistence times of Chapter 4.

5.5 Two-species neutral competition

In the absence of interactions, two species competing for the same \(N\) sites with identical rates perform a neutral voter model (or Moran process). At each event an individual dies and is replaced by the offspring of a random individual. The number \(n\) of species \(A\) is a birth–death chain with \(W^+(n) = W^-(n) = n(N - n)/N\): no drift, only noise. In the continuum limit \(x = n/N\), \[\partial_tP = \frac{1}{N}\,\partial_x^2\bigl[x(1 - x)P\bigr],\] so one species always fixates. The probability that \(A\) wins is its initial fraction \(x_0\) (the process is a martingale), and the mean fixation time is \(-N\bigl[x_0\ln x_0 + (1 - x_0)\ln(1 - x_0)\bigr]\) generations. Neutral coexistence of two species is transient; long-term diversity requires speciation or immigration (Chapter 3) or stabilising interactions (Chapters 6–7).

5.6 Take-home messages

  • A model of interacting populations can be built directly from biological assumptions: prey growth + predator mortality + random encounters give a nonlinear two-dimensional system.
  • A fixed point says where the system can remain stationary; its response to small perturbations is set by the Jacobian, \(\dot{\delta\mathbf x} = J^*\delta\mathbf x\), the multidimensional version of \(F'(N^*)\). A fixed point is not the same as stability.
  • Complex eigenvalues \(\lambda_\pm = -\gamma \pm i\omega_0\) (a stable focus) mean damped oscillations after a single perturbation.
  • Elementary reactions generate the deterministic drift, \(\mathbf F = \sum_r\boldsymbol\nu_rw_r\), but the noise depends on each channel separately: the same deterministic model can correspond to different stochastic dynamics.
  • Around a stable point, \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} = J^*\boldsymbol\zeta + \boldsymbol\eta\): the same Jacobian, now driven by demographic noise, fixes the spectrum \(H B H^\dagger\). Continuous noise re-excites the damped mode, producing persistent quasi-cycles and a spectral peak near \(\omega_0\) (resonant amplification).
  • Local stability does not guarantee persistence: finite populations can still hit an absorbing boundary.

(Conserved quantity) Show that \(H = dx - c\ln x + by - a\ln y\) is conserved by the classical Lotka–Volterra equations, and that the time averages over a closed orbit equal the equilibrium values.

Solution. \(\dot H = (d - c/x)\dot x + (b - a/y)\dot y = (d - c/x)x(a - by) + (b - a/y)y(dx - c) = (dx - c)(a - by) + (by - a)(dx - c) = 0\). (Self-limited prey) For \(\lambda = 1\), \(\eta = 0.5\), \(p = 1\), \(k = 10\), find the coexistence equilibrium, its eigenvalues, and the period and decay time of the damped oscillations.

(Neutral fixation) For the two-species voter model, use the backward Kolmogorov equation \(\frac{x(1-x)}{N}\,\phi''(x) = 0\) to show that the fixation probability of \(A\) from fraction \(x_0\) is \(x_0\). Introducing a single new species into an ecosystem (wolves into Yellowstone, a predatory fish into a lagoon) can reorganise the whole community through a *trophic cascade*. To understand how, we start with two species. This chapter treats the predator–prey problem twice. Deterministically, the Lotka–Volterra equations and their variants explain damped or sustained oscillations. Stochastically, a microscopic individual-based model shows that demographic noise, far from being a small correction, is **resonantly amplified** into large, persistent cycles. The machinery developed here (reaction schemes, multivariate master equations, the van Kampen system-size expansion, power spectra) is reused for neurons (Chapter 10) and for genes (Chapter 16).

## The Lotka–Volterra equations

### Historical model and its conserved quantity

Lotka (1920) and Volterra (1926) wrote, independently,
$$\dot x = ax - bxy, \qquad \dot y = -cy + dxy,$$
for prey $x$ and predators $y$. Volterra was prompted by his son-in-law, the biologist Umberto D'Ancona, who had noticed that the fraction of predatory fish in the markets of Trieste, Fiume and Venice rose during the First World War, when fishing stopped. Prey grow exponentially at rate $a$ when alone; predators die at rate $c$ without prey; encounters (mass action, rate $\propto xy$) convert prey into predators.

Besides the trivial equilibrium, there is a coexistence point $(x^*, y^*) = (c/d, a/b)$. The model has a **conserved quantity**
$$H(x, y) = dx - c\ln x + by - a\ln y, \qquad \dot H = 0,$$
so trajectories are closed orbits around $(x^*, y^*)$, whose amplitude is fixed by the initial condition: **neutral cycles**. The Jacobian at $(x^*, y^*)$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues $\pm i\sqrt{ac}$, giving small oscillations of period $2\pi/\sqrt{ac}$. This is a structurally unstable situation: any perturbation of the equations (self-limitation, saturation, noise) turns the centres into spirals.

Volterra's model also explains D'Ancona's observation. Fishing adds mortality $f$ to both species, $a \to a - f$ and $c \to c + f$, so the time-averaged abundances (equal to the equilibrium values on a closed orbit) become $\langle x\rangle = (c + f)/d$, $\langle y\rangle = (a - f)/b$. Less fishing means *fewer* prey and *more* predators: **Volterra's principle**.

### Self-limited prey

Adding logistic self-limitation to the prey gives
$$\dot x = \lambda x\Bigl(1 - \frac xk\Bigr) - pxy, \qquad \dot y = -\eta y + pxy .$$
The equilibria are $z_1^* = (0, 0)$, $z_3^* = (k, 0)$ and the coexistence point
$$z_2^* = \Bigl(\frac\eta p,\ \frac\lambda p\Bigl(1 - \frac{\eta}{pk}\Bigr)\Bigr),$$
which is feasible (positive) iff $pk > \eta$: the prey at carrying capacity must sustain the predators. The Jacobian $J = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda(1 - 2x/k) - py & -px\\ py & px - \eta\end{pmatrix}$ gives:

- at $z_1^*$, eigenvalues $\lambda > 0$ and $-\eta$: a saddle (unstable);
- at $z_3^*$, eigenvalues $-\lambda$ and $pk - \eta > 0$: a saddle, unstable when coexistence is feasible;
- at $z_2^*$,
$$J_2 = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\lambda\eta}{kp} & -\eta\\ \lambda\bigl(1 - \frac{\eta}{pk}\bigr) & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vartheta_{1,2} = \frac{1}{2kp}\Bigl[-\lambda\eta \pm \sqrt{\lambda\eta\bigl(\lambda\eta - 4kp(kp - \eta)\bigr)}\Bigr].$$

Since $\operatorname{tr}J_2 < 0$ and $\det J_2 = \lambda\eta(1 - \eta/pk) > 0$, $z_2^*$ is **always stable**. For large $k$ the discriminant is negative and the eigenvalues are complex: populations approach the equilibrium through **damped oscillations** with frequency $\simeq\sqrt{\lambda\eta(1 - \eta/pk)}$ and damping rate $\lambda\eta/(2kp)$, which is small when $k$ is large.

### From biological assumptions to stability: a worked example

Instead of starting from a given equation, build the model from what is known. An ecosystem contains prey $N_1(t)$ and predators $N_2(t)$:

- without predators, prey grow logistically with rate $a_1 = 1$ and carrying capacity $K = 100$;
- without prey, predators decline exponentially with rate $a_2 = -0.5$;
- predator–prey encounters are random, so their frequency is proportional to $N_1N_2$;
- the coefficients controlling prey loss and predator gain are $\alpha = \beta = 2$.

Is there a state in which prey and predators coexist, and with which abundances? If the system sits there and the predators are suddenly increased by $5\%$, what happens next, and what mathematical information is needed to say?

The simplest model consistent with these assumptions is
$$\dot N_1 = a_1N_1\Bigl(1 - \frac{N_1}{K}\Bigr) - \frac\alpha KN_1N_2, \qquad \dot N_2 = a_2N_2 + \frac\beta KN_1N_2 .$$
Dividing the encounter terms by $K$ keeps $\alpha$ and $\beta$ on the same time scale as $a_1$ and $a_2$. In general $\alpha \neq \beta$, because one prey eaten does not make exactly one new predator. In the densities $x = N_1/K$, $y = N_2/K$,
$$\dot x = x\bigl[a_1(1 - x) - \alpha y\bigr], \qquad \dot y = y\bigl[a_2 + \beta x\bigr],$$
which for our numbers reads $\dot x = x(1 - x - 2y)$, $\dot y = y(-0.5 + 2x)$.

**Fixed points.** Besides extinction, $(0, 0)$, and the prey-only state $(1, 0)$ (i.e. $N_1 = K$, $N_2 = 0$), coexistence with $x^*, y^* > 0$ requires $a_2 + \beta x^* = 0$ and $a_1(1 - x^*) = \alpha y^*$:
$$x^* = -\frac{a_2}{\beta} = 0.25, \qquad y^* = \frac{a_1}{\alpha}\Bigl(1 + \frac{a_2}{\beta}\Bigr) = 0.375,$$
i.e. $N_1^* = 25$ prey and $N_2^* = 37.5$ predators. Abundances are continuous variables in a deterministic model, so a non-integer value is not a problem. Finding the fixed point answers *where can the system remain stationary?* It does not answer *what happens after a small perturbation?*

::: {.callout-tip title="Concept check: fixed point versus stability"}
Two predator–prey models have exactly the same coexistence equilibrium $(x^*, y^*)$. Is this enough to conclude that they respond in the same way to a small perturbation around it?

- **A.** Yes, because the equilibrium populations are identical.
- **B.** Yes, provided that the perturbation is sufficiently small.
- **C.** No: the response also depends on the local derivatives of the dynamics around the equilibrium.
- **D.** No prediction is possible without solving the complete nonlinear dynamics.

**Answer: C.** A fixed point specifies the location of a stationary state. The dynamics near it depends on how the vector field changes around it, that is, on the Jacobian. Option B is tempting, but small perturbations are exactly the ones governed by the Jacobian, which can differ between models with the same fixed point. Option D goes too far: for small perturbations the linearisation is enough, unless an eigenvalue has zero real part.
:::

**Stability.** As in Chapter 2, write $\mathbf x = \mathbf x^* + \delta\mathbf x$. The local expansion gives $\dot{\delta\mathbf x} = J^*\delta\mathbf x$, with the Jacobian in the role that $F'(N^*)$ played for one species:
$$J(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} a_1(1 - 2x) - \alpha y & -\alpha x\\ \beta y & a_2 + \beta x\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J^* = \begin{pmatrix} -0.25 & -0.5\\ 0.75 & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$
The eigenvalues solve $\det(J^* - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 0.25\,\lambda + 0.375 = 0$:
$$\lambda_\pm = -0.125 \pm 0.599\,i \equiv -\gamma \pm i\omega_0 .$$
Since $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm < 0$, perturbations decay. Since $\operatorname{Im}\lambda_\pm \neq 0$, the return is oscillatory. The coexistence point is a **stable focus**, with $\delta\mathbf x(t) \sim e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)$, damping rate $\gamma \simeq 0.125$ and angular frequency $\omega_0 \simeq 0.599$.

After the $5\%$ increase in predators, $y(0) = 1.05\,y^*$ and $x(0) = x^*$, the populations do not return monotonically. The extra predators first depress the prey, which bottom out at $N_1 \simeq 23.9$ around $t \simeq 2.3$. The predators then decline, the prey recover, and so on. The oscillation has period $2\pi/\omega_0 \simeq 10.5$, and the predator excess shrinks by a factor $e^{-2\pi\gamma/\omega_0} \simeq 0.27$ per cycle (a numerical integration of the full nonlinear equations agrees, @fig-pp-worked). A fixed point does not determine its own stability; the Jacobian does.

### Real cycles

Real predator–prey systems often oscillate for decades: the snowshoe hare and lynx pelts of the Hudson's Bay Company, rodents, plankton in chemostats. The deterministic model above predicts that the oscillations die out. There are two classical ways out.

1. **Limit cycles.** If predators saturate (Holling type II functional response, $pxy \to \frac{pxy}{1 + hx}$), the coexistence equilibrium can lose stability through a **Hopf bifurcation** when the carrying capacity is increased. A stable limit cycle is born, with amplitude independent of initial conditions. Enriching the environment destabilises it: Rosenzweig's *paradox of enrichment* [-@rosenzweig1971paradox].
2. **Stochastic amplification.** Even when the deterministic equilibrium is stable, the discreteness of individuals produces noise that excites the weakly damped oscillatory mode. The result is large, irregular but persistent cycles with a well-defined dominant frequency. This mechanism requires no fine tuning, and is the subject of the rest of the chapter.

## From reactions to master equations

The deterministic predator–prey equations bundle several biological processes: prey reproduction, limitation of prey growth, predator mortality, encounters, and predator reproduction associated with predation. Real populations are finite, and each process happens as discrete random events, not as a continuous flow. To turn a term into an event, identify the individuals involved, the change it produces in predator and prey numbers, and how its rate depends on the current abundances. The deterministic equations do *not* determine this microscopic description uniquely.

Individual-based models are best written as **reaction schemes**, as in chemistry. Consider $N_s$ types of "molecules" (individuals of different species, or empty sites) and $M$ reactions $R_j$. Each reaction has:

- a **state-change vector** $\mathbf v_j$: the change in the vector of abundances $\mathbf x$ when $R_j$ fires (e.g. $B \to A + A$ has $\mathbf v = (+2, -1)$);
- a **propensity** $a_j(\mathbf x)$: $a_j\,\Delta t$ is the probability that $R_j$ fires in $[t, t + \Delta t]$.

For a unimolecular reaction $A \to \cdots$ with rate constant $C_j$, $a_j = C_jx_A$. For a bimolecular one, $A + B \to \cdots$, $a_j = C_jx_Ax_B/\Omega$, where $\Omega$ is the system size (volume, number of sites). Two molecules must meet, and in a larger volume they are less likely to. In general, a reaction involving $k$ reactants has $a_j \propto x_1\cdots x_k/\Omega^{k-1}$, so propensities are **extensive**: $a_j = \Omega\,\alpha_j(\mathbf x/\Omega)$. The master equation balances inflow from $\mathbf x - \mathbf v_j$ and outflow from $\mathbf x$:
$$\frac{\partial P(\mathbf x, t)}{\partial t} = \sum_{j=1}^M\bigl[a_j(\mathbf x - \mathbf v_j)P(\mathbf x - \mathbf v_j, t) - a_j(\mathbf x)P(\mathbf x, t)\bigr],$$
and can be sampled exactly with the Gillespie algorithm of Chapter 2.

## A stochastic predator–prey model

### Microscopic rules

Following @mckane2005predator, consider $N$ sites, each empty ($E$), occupied by a predator ($A$) or by a prey ($B$). Let $n$, $m$ and $e = N - n - m$ be their numbers. Individuals interact as if well mixed (spatial effects neglected), through five reactions:
$$A \xrightarrow{d_1} E, \quad B + E \xrightarrow{b} B + B, \quad B \xrightarrow{d_2} E, \quad A + B \xrightarrow{p_2} A + E, \quad A + B \xrightarrow{p_1} A + A .$$
Predators die. Prey reproduce into empty sites and die. Predation either just removes the prey ($p_2$) or also converts it into a new predator ($p_1$). Choosing pairs at random, with a factor $2$ from the two orderings, the propensities are
$$T(n - 1, m \mid n, m) = d_1n, \qquad T(n, m + 1 \mid n, m) = 2b\,\frac{m\,e}{N},$$
$$T(n, m - 1 \mid n, m) = d_2m + 2p_2\frac{nm}{N}, \qquad T(n + 1, m - 1 \mid n, m) = 2p_1\frac{nm}{N}.$$
This is one microscopic realisation of the ecological ingredients, not the only one. Numerical factors from the pair-sampling rule are a convention and can be absorbed into the rate constants. The 2026 lectures [@suweis2026redesign], for instance, write $b\,me/N$, $p_1nm/N$ and $p_2nm/N$ without the factor $2$; every formula below then holds with $2b \to b$, $2p_i \to p_i$.

### Drift and diffusion

With densities $x = n/N$ (predators) and $y = m/N$ (prey), each propensity is $N$ times a function of $(x, y)$. Expanding the master equation to second order in $1/N$ (with shift operators $\mathbb E^\pm_A$, $\mathbb E^\pm_B$ as in Chapter 2) gives a two-dimensional Fokker–Planck equation
$$\partial_tP = -\sum_i\partial_i\bigl[A_iP\bigr] + \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\bigl[B_{ij}P\bigr].$$
The drift is the vector of average changes:
$$A_1 = 2p_1xy - d_1x, \qquad A_2 = 2by(1 - x - y) - d_2y - 2(p_1 + p_2)xy .$$
The diffusion matrix collects, for each reaction, $\mathbf v_j\mathbf v_j^\top$ weighted by its rate:
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} d_1x + 2p_1xy & -2p_1xy\\ -2p_1xy & 2by(1 - x - y) + d_2y + 2(p_1 + p_2)xy\end{pmatrix}.$$
The off-diagonal element is negative because the conversion reaction changes $n$ and $m$ in opposite directions, $\mathbf v = (+1, -1)$. Noise in the two populations is correlated.

As $N \to \infty$ the noise vanishes and the drift gives the **mean-field equations**
$$\dot x = 2p_1xy - d_1x, \qquad \dot y = ry\Bigl(1 - \frac yk\Bigr) - 2(p_1 + p_2 + b)\,xy, \qquad r = 2b - d_2, \quad k = \frac{2b - d_2}{2b},$$
a predator–prey system with logistic prey. Expanding $2by(1 - x - y) = 2by(1 - y) - 2bxy$ shows where the logistic term comes from. Because reproduction needs an empty site, the same reaction generates both prey self-limitation and part of the effective predator–prey interaction. Microscopic events produce effective deterministic interactions, and the mapping onto Lotka–Volterra parameters depends on the microscopic realisation. Its interior equilibrium
$$y^* = \frac{d_1}{2p_1}, \qquad x^* = \frac{2b(1 - y^*) - d_2}{2(b + p_1 + p_2)}$$
is a stable focus, so the mean-field dynamics predicts damped oscillations and no cycles.

### Langevin equations

The equivalent Langevin system is
From biological assumptions to stability: a worked example$\dot x = A_1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_jg_{1j}\xi_j, \qquad \dot y = A_2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_jg_{2j}\xi_j, \qquad \langle\xi_i(t)\xi_j(t')\rangle = \delta_{ij}\delta(t - t'),$$
where $g$ is any matrix with $gg^\top = B$. For instance $g = S^\top\Lambda^{1/2}S$ from the diagonalisation $B = S^\top\Lambda S$, or the Cholesky factor of $B$. Only $B$ is physical; different square roots give the same statistics.

### The deterministic model does not determine the noise

For reactions $r$ with scaled rates $w_r(\mathbf x)$ and state changes $\boldsymbol\nu_r$, the drift and the diffusion matrix are (see the exercises)
$$\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \sum_r\boldsymbol\nu_r\,w_r(\mathbf x), \qquad B(\mathbf x) = \sum_r\boldsymbol\nu_r\boldsymbol\nu_r^\top\,w_r(\mathbf x).$$
The drift depends only on the *net* effect of all channels. The noise depends on each channel separately. Different reaction schemes can therefore produce the same deterministic dynamics but different fluctuations. In the model above, predation with conversion is a single event, $\boldsymbol\nu = (+1, -1)$, which makes $B_{12} < 0$. Suppose instead that the predator gain and the prey loss occurred as two separate events, with the same rate $2p_1xy$ and $\boldsymbol\nu = (+1, 0)$ and $(0, -1)$. The drift would be unchanged, but $B_{12}$ would vanish: noise in the two populations would be uncorrelated, and the power spectra below would change. This is the multidimensional version of the logistic example of Chapter 2, where splitting the same net growth into births and deaths in different ways changed the variance by a factor $b/d$:
$$\text{same deterministic dynamics} \;\not\Rightarrow\; \text{same stochastic dynamics}.$$

## The van Kampen expansion and stochastic amplification

### Linear noise approximation

Fluctuations around the deterministic equilibrium are $O(N^{-1/2})$. Following van Kampen, write
$$x(t) = x^* + \frac{\zeta_1(t)}{\sqrt N}, \qquad y(t) = y^* + \frac{\zeta_2(t)}{\sqrt N}.$$
Substituting and keeping the leading order, the drift contributes the Jacobian $J = \partial\mathbf A/\partial\mathbf x$ at $(x^*, y^*)$, and the noise amplitude is frozen at its equilibrium value:
$$\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} = J\boldsymbol\zeta + \boldsymbol\eta(t), \qquad \langle\eta_i(t)\eta_j(t')\rangle = B_{ij}(x^*, y^*)\,\delta(t - t').$$
This is a multivariate Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. The fluctuations are Gaussian, of size independent of $N$ in the rescaled variables, and their statistics follow from linear algebra. This is the **linear noise approximation**. It is exact to leading order in $1/\sqrt N$ around a stable fixed point.

The Jacobian has two related roles. For a single deterministic perturbation it gives decay and oscillation, as in the worked example. With demographic noise it also controls the response to a continuous stream of random kicks, i.e. the fluctuation spectrum. The noise matrix $B$ is evaluated at the fixed point and depends on the microscopic reactions.

::: {.callout-tip title="Concept check: what does noise do to a stable focus?"}
The deterministic coexistence point is a stable focus, $\lambda_\pm = -\gamma \pm i\omega_0$ with $\gamma > 0$. Consider a large but finite population with demographic noise. What does a typical stochastic trajectory look like?

- **A.** It converges exactly to the coexistence point and stays there.
- **B.** It fluctuates randomly around coexistence, with no preferred time scale.
- **C.** It shows noisy but persistent oscillations, with a characteristic frequency related to $\omega_0$.
- **D.** Any amount of demographic noise makes the coexistence point deterministically unstable.

**Answer: C.** The fixed point remains deterministically stable (so D is wrong). Noise keeps pushing the system away from it (so A is wrong) and repeatedly excites its damped oscillatory mode. The response is therefore not featureless (so B is wrong): it is concentrated near $\omega_0$, as the power spectrum shows.
:::

### The power spectrum

Without noise, a perturbation of the stable focus behaves as $e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)$ and disappears. With demographic noise, new perturbations are generated all the time, and the system responds to each of them through the same oscillatory mode. A damped oscillatory mode plus continuous noise gives persistent stochastic oscillations, called **stochastic quasi-cycles**. Their signature is best seen in the frequency domain.

Fourier transforming, $(i\omega - J)\tilde{\boldsymbol\zeta}(\omega) = \tilde{\boldsymbol\eta}(\omega)$ with $\langle\tilde\eta_i(\omega)\tilde\eta^*_j(\omega')\rangle = B_{ij}\,2\pi\delta(\omega - \omega')$. Hence $\tilde{\boldsymbol\zeta}(\omega) = H(\omega)\tilde{\boldsymbol\eta}(\omega)$, where $H(\omega) = (i\omega - J)^{-1}$ is the **linear response function**. The **power spectrum** $P_i(\omega) = \langle|\tilde\zeta_i(\omega)|^2\rangle$ (per unit frequency) is the diagonal of the spectral matrix
$$\mathcal P(\omega) = H(\omega)\,B\,H^\dagger(\omega) = (i\omega - J)^{-1}\,B\,(-i\omega - J^\top)^{-1}.$$
For two variables, with $\Omega_0^2 = \det J$ and $\Gamma = -\operatorname{tr}J$, the denominator is $|\det(i\omega - J)|^2 = (\omega^2 - \Omega_0^2)^2 + \Gamma^2\omega^2$, and solving the $2\times2$ system,
$$P_1(\omega) = \frac{\alpha_1 + B_{11}\,\omega^2}{(\omega^2 - \Omega_0^2)^2 + \Gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \alpha_1 = B_{11}J_{22}^2 - 2B_{12}J_{12}J_{22} + B_{22}J_{12}^2,$$
and similarly for $P_2$ with indices exchanged. For our model $J_{11} = 0$ at equilibrium (the predator equation is $\dot x = x(2p_1y - d_1)$), so $\Omega_0^2 = J_{12}|J_{21}|$ and $\Gamma = |J_{22}|$.

This is the response of a **damped harmonic oscillator** with natural frequency $\Omega_0$ and damping $\Gamma$, driven by white noise. When $\Gamma \ll \Omega_0$ (weak damping, as for a large carrying capacity) the spectrum has a sharp peak near $\Omega_0$, of height $\propto 1/(\Gamma^2\Omega_0^2)$. Demographic noise has power at all frequencies; the system filters it and amplifies the frequency of its own damped mode. The deterministic dynamics acts as a frequency-selective filter, and the result is **resonant amplification of demographic noise**, without external forcing and without fine tuning. Do not confuse it with *stochastic resonance* in the strict sense. That term usually describes how a noisy nonlinear system responds to an additional external periodic forcing, and here there is no forcing at all.

### Local stability is not persistence

Quasi-cycles do not mean that coexistence lasts forever in a finite population. The deterministic coexistence point can be locally stable while the stochastic process still reaches an absorbing boundary, $N_1 = 0$ or $N_2 = 0$. The cycles have an amplitude of order $\sqrt N$ individuals around abundances of order $N$. In small systems, or when the focus is weakly damped and the cycles are large, a trough of the cycle can touch zero and one species goes extinct. Local stability and extinction are different questions. The first is answered by the Jacobian. The second is a first-passage problem, like the persistence times of Chapter 4.

## Two-species neutral competition

In the absence of interactions, two species competing for the same $N$ sites with identical rates perform a neutral **voter model** (or Moran process). At each event an individual dies and is replaced by the offspring of a random individual. The number $n$ of species $A$ is a birth–death chain with $W^+(n) = W^-(n) = n(N - n)/N$: no drift, only noise. In the continuum limit $x = n/N$,
$$\partial_tP = \frac{1}{N}\,\partial_x^2\bigl[x(1 - x)P\bigr],$$
so one species always fixates. The probability that $A$ wins is its initial fraction $x_0$ (the process is a martingale), and the mean fixation time is $-N\bigl[x_0\ln x_0 + (1 - x_0)\ln(1 - x_0)\bigr]$ generations. Neutral coexistence of two species is transient; long-term diversity requires speciation or immigration (Chapter 3) or stabilising interactions (Chapters 6–7).

## Take-home messages

- A model of interacting populations can be built directly from biological assumptions: prey growth + predator mortality + random encounters give a nonlinear two-dimensional system.
- A fixed point says where the system can remain stationary; its response to small perturbations is set by the Jacobian, $\dot{\delta\mathbf x} = J^*\delta\mathbf x$, the multidimensional version of $F'(N^*)$. A fixed point is not the same as stability.
- Complex eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm = -\gamma \pm i\omega_0$ (a stable focus) mean damped oscillations after a single perturbation.
- Elementary reactions generate the deterministic drift, $\mathbf F = \sum_r\boldsymbol\nu_rw_r$, but the noise depends on each channel separately: the same deterministic model can correspond to different stochastic dynamics.
- Around a stable point, $\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta} = J^*\boldsymbol\zeta + \boldsymbol\eta$: the same Jacobian, now driven by demographic noise, fixes the spectrum $H B H^\dagger$. Continuous noise re-excites the damped mode, producing persistent quasi-cycles and a spectral peak near $\omega_0$ (resonant amplification).
- Local stability does not guarantee persistence: finite populations can still hit an absorbing boundary.

::: {.solution}
