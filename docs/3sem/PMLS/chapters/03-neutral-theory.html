3  Many species: neutral theory and upscaling

From one population we move to communities of hundreds or thousands of species, such as the trees of a tropical forest. Writing a model with species-specific parameters for each of them is hopeless. Yet community-level patterns, above all the distribution of species abundances, are remarkably regular. This chapter shows that a minimal model, in which every species obeys the same stochastic birth–death dynamics (neutral theory), predicts these patterns. Its solution, the negative binomial distribution, has a scaling property that lets us infer the biodiversity of a whole forest from a small sample. Real forests turn out to sit close to a critical point of the model.

3.1 Species abundance distributions

A census of a plot records the abundance \(n_i\) of every species \(i = 1, \dots, S\). Two equivalent summaries are used:

  • the relative species abundance (RSA) \(\phi_n\): the number of species with exactly \(n\) individuals;
  • the species abundance distribution (SAD) \(P(n) = \phi_n/S\): the probability that a randomly chosen species has \(n\) individuals.

Empirical RSAs are dominated by rare species: many species have one or two individuals, and a few are hyper-abundant. Plotted on a linear scale they are hollow curves with very sparse tails. Ecologists therefore use Preston plots, which group abundances into octaves \([2^k, 2^{k+1})\) and plot the number of species per octave against \(k = \log_2 n\).

WarningBe careful with binning

An octave collects many more abundance values at large \(k\) than at small \(k\). The count in octave \(k\) is \(\sum_{n=2^k}^{2^{k+1}-1}\phi_n \simeq 2^k\phi_{2^k}\ln2\), which is \(n\,\phi_n\) rather than \(\phi_n\). A log-series \(\phi_n = \theta x^n/n\), monotonically decreasing in \(n\), therefore looks flat per octave (each octave holds about \(\theta\ln2\) species) up to \(n \sim 1/(1 - x)\), and then drops. A genuine interior maximum in a Preston plot indicates that \(n\phi_n\) increases, which the log-series cannot produce but a negative binomial with \(r > 1\) can. Always state the binning before interpreting a shape.

3.2 From individual dynamics to the negative binomial

3.2.1 Density-dependent birth and death

Let \(P^s_n(t)\) be the probability that species \(s\) has \(n\) individuals. If each species follows a one-step process with rates \(b^s_n\) and \(d^s_n\), the master equation is \[\frac{dP^s_n}{dt} = b^s_{n-1}P^s_{n-1} + d^s_{n+1}P^s_{n+1} - (b^s_n + d^s_n)P^s_n .\] The per-capita rates may depend on abundance. Expanding them for large \(n\), \[\frac{b^s_n}{n} = b_s + \frac{r_s}{n} + O(n^{-2}), \qquad \frac{d^s_n}{n} = d_s + \frac{d_1}{n} + O(n^{-2}).\] Such density dependence is observed: seedlings close to adults of the same species suffer more from specialised pests (the Janzen–Connell effect), so being rare is an advantage. The simplest choice keeping the leading terms is \[b^s_n = b_sn + r_s, \qquad d^s_n = d_sn,\] exactly the linear birth–death–immigration process of Chapter 2. The constant \(r_s\) plays the role of immigration (or speciation) and keeps rare species from going extinct.

Treating each species independently amounts to replacing the joint distribution \(P(n_1, \dots, n_S)\) by a product \(\prod_sP^s(n_s)\): interactions are absorbed into effective, mean-field rates.

3.2.2 The stationary solution

By the product formula of Chapter 2, \[P^{s*}_n = P^{s*}_0\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{b_{j-1}}{d_j} = P^{s*}_0\,\frac{\Gamma(n + r)}{\Gamma(r)\,n!}\,\xi^n, \qquad \xi = \frac bd, \quad r = \frac{r_s}{b_s},\] dropping the label \(s\). This is the negative binomial distribution \[P^{\mathrm{NB}}_n(r, \xi) = \binom{n + r - 1}{n}\xi^n(1 - \xi)^r, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,\] which requires \(\xi < 1\) (deaths outweigh births) and \(r > 0\). Its generating function is particularly simple: \[G(z) = \sum_{n\geq0}P^{\mathrm{NB}}_nz^n = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \xi}{1 - \xi z}\Bigr)^r,\] from which the mean \(\langle n\rangle = G'(1) = \frac{r\xi}{1 - \xi}\) and the variance \(\frac{r\xi}{(1 - \xi)^2}\) follow. In a census only species that are present are counted, so the RSA is the NB truncated to \(n \geq 1\): \[P^*_n(r, \xi) = \frac{1}{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}\binom{n + r - 1}{n}\xi^n(1 - \xi)^r, \qquad n \geq 1 .\]

As \(r \to 0\) the truncated NB tends to Fisher’s log-series \[P^*_n \to \frac{1}{-\ln(1 - \xi)}\,\frac{\xi^n}{n}.\]

Proof. \(\binom{n + r - 1}{n} = \frac{r(r + 1)\cdots(r + n - 1)}{n!} \simeq \frac{r\,(n - 1)!}{n!} = \frac rn\) and \(1 - (1 - \xi)^r \simeq -r\ln(1 - \xi)\) as \(r \to 0\). \(\square\)

3.3 Neutral theory

3.3.1 The neutrality hypothesis

Fitting species-specific \((r_s, \xi_s)\) to thousands of species would need thousands of parameters. Hubbell’s neutral theory (2001) assumes instead that species on the same trophic level (e.g. all tree species of a forest) are demographically equivalent: they have the same per-capita birth, death, dispersal and speciation rates. Different species are then different realisations of the same stochastic process, and their abundances differ only by chance (ecological drift), not by fitness.

Under neutrality the \(S\) species are \(S\) independent draws from one distribution, so the expected RSA is simply \(\phi_n = S\,P^*_n(r, \xi)\), with two parameters for the whole community. Fits of the NB to the RSA of large tropical forest plots (Barro Colorado Island, Pasoh, Korup, Lambir, Yasuní, Sinharaja) are excellent. Neutrality is surprising, since species obviously differ, and controversial. Its success says that, for the abundance distribution, differences between species average out in very diverse communities. It is a null model, and departures from it point to the biology that matters.

3.3.2 The voter model with speciation

Neutral theory can also be formulated in a spatially explicit, zero-sum way. A landscape (say, a lattice) is saturated by \(J\) individuals. At each step a random individual dies and is replaced either by the offspring of a neighbour (probability \(1 - \nu\)) or by an individual of a brand-new species (speciation, probability \(\nu\)). Without speciation this is the voter model of statistical physics: domains of the same “opinion” coarsen and one species eventually takes over. Speciation injects diversity and produces a statistically stationary community.

In the well-mixed (mean-field) version, the abundance of each species performs a birth–death walk with \(b \lesssim d\), and new species enter at rate \(\nu\). The resulting metacommunity RSA is the log-series \[\langle\phi_n\rangle = \theta\,\frac{x^n}{n}, \qquad \theta \simeq \frac{2J\nu}{1 - \nu},\] where \(\theta\) is Hubbell’s fundamental biodiversity number and \(x = b/d \to 1\) for large communities. The mean number of species found among \(J\) individuals is \(\langle S\rangle = \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)\) (Ewens’ sampling formula).

3.3.3 A local community fed by a metacommunity

Real plots are open: they receive immigrants from a large surrounding metacommunity, the “continent” to the plot’s “island”. Consider an island of fixed size \(J\) (zero-sum dynamics). At each event an individual dies, and the vacancy is filled with probability \(1 - m\) by local reproduction and with probability \(m\) by an immigrant drawn from the metacommunity, where species \(k\) has relative abundance \(\mu_k/J_M\). The rates for species \(k\) with \(n\) individuals on the island are \[b_{n,k} = (1 - m)\Bigl(1 - \frac nJ\Bigr)\frac{n}{J - 1} + m\Bigl(1 - \frac nJ\Bigr)\frac{\mu_k}{J_M},\] \[d_{n,k} = (1 - m)\frac nJ\,\frac{J - n}{J - 1} + m\,\frac nJ\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mu_k}{J_M}\Bigr).\] In \(b_{n,k}\) an individual of another species dies (probability \(1 - n/J\)) and is replaced by a local offspring of \(k\) or by an immigrant of \(k\). In \(d_{n,k}\) an individual of \(k\) dies and is replaced by another species. With \[\lambda_k = \frac{m}{1 - m}(J - 1)\frac{\mu_k}{J_M}, \qquad \theta_k = J + \frac{m}{1 - m}(J - 1)\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mu_k}{J_M}\Bigr),\] the rates become \(b_{n,k} = \frac{1 - m}{J}\frac{J - n}{J - 1}(n + \lambda_k)\) and \(d_{n,k} = \frac{1 - m}{J - 1}\frac nJ(\theta_k - n)\), and the stationary distribution is \[P^*_{n,k} = P_{0,k}\,\frac{J!}{n!\,(J - n)!}\,\frac{\Gamma(n + \lambda_k)}{\Gamma(\lambda_k)}\,\frac{\Gamma(\theta_k - n)}{\Gamma(\theta_k)} .\] On a log scale it resembles a log-normal, but it comes from a mechanism, which is why Volkov et al. (2003) prefer it to the phenomenological log-normal. The island RSA follows by averaging over the metacommunity abundances, \(\phi_n = S_M\sum_kP^*_{n,k}P(\mu_k)\). Strong dispersal limitation (small \(m\)) depletes rare species locally and produces the hump of empirical Preston plots.

3.4 Upscaling: from a sample to the whole forest

3.4.1 The problem

The Amazon holds about \(10^{11}\) trees; no census can count them. Typically, plots covering a fraction \(p = a/A \ll 1\) of a forest of area \(A\) are sampled. How many species does the whole forest contain? Rare species are mostly missed by small samples, so the RSA is scale dependent. At small \(p\) it is a hollow curve dominated by singletons. At larger \(p\), species accumulate individuals, mass moves out of the lowest abundance classes, and the curve develops an interior maximum. Linear extrapolation of \(S\) fails.

3.4.2 Binomial sampling and self-similarity

Assume individuals are spread over the forest independently of each other (random placement, or well-mixed). If a species has \(n\) individuals in total, the number \(k\) found in a region covering a fraction \(p\) of the area is binomial: \[P_p(k \mid n) = \binom nkp^k(1 - p)^{n - k}.\] The abundance distribution at scale \(p\) is then \[P_p(k) = \sum_{n\geq k}\binom nkp^k(1 - p)^{n-k}\,P_n(r, \xi).\]

If abundances at scale \(1\) are \(\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi)\), then abundances at scale \(p\) are \(\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi_p)\) with the same \(r\) and \[\xi_p = \frac{p\,\xi}{1 - \xi(1 - p)} .\]

Proof. Binomial thinning acts on generating functions as \(G_p(z) = G(1 - p + pz)\): each individual is kept with probability \(p\). For the NB, \[G_p(z) = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \xi}{1 - \xi + \xi p - \xi pz}\Bigr)^r = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \xi_p}{1 - \xi_pz}\Bigr)^r, \qquad 1 - \xi_p = \frac{1 - \xi}{1 - \xi(1 - p)},\] again an NB, with \(\xi_p\) as stated. \(\square\)

The NB is a fixed form under coarse-graining, like a renormalisation-group fixed point: only \(\xi\) flows with scale, \(r\) does not. Inverting, \[\xi = \frac{\xi_p}{p + \xi_p(1 - p)} .\]

CautionZero size probability of a species

At scale \(p\) there is a non-zero probability of having a zero size population species, since

\[ P_p(k)\equiv NB(r,\xi_p) \] Which normalization is: \[ |\sum_{i=0}^{n} NB(r,\xi_p)=1 \]

3.4.3 Estimating the number of species

A species is missed by the sample with probability \(P_p(0)\). If \(S^*\) species are observed at scale \(p\), then \(S^* = S/[1 - P_p(0)]\). For the NB truncated at scale \(1\), \(P_p(0) = \frac{(1 - \xi_p)^r - (1 - \xi)^r}{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}\), hence \[S = S^*\,\frac{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}{1 - (1 - \xi_p)^r}.\] The recipe: fit a truncated NB to the sample RSA to get \((r, \xi_p)\); compute \(\xi\) from self-similarity; compute \(S\). The same machinery predicts the RSA and the species–area relationship at any scale \(p' \in (p, 1]\).

The method was validated on computer-generated forests, where the truth is known, and on fully censused plots sub-sampled at random. It outperforms classical estimators based on the log-series or on non-parametric corrections (Chao). Applied to tropical forests (Tovo et al., 2017), it predicts, for instance, about \(366 \pm 15\) tree species at Barro Colorado from \(301\) observed, and an Amazonian richness of order \(10^4\) species. The large number of hyper-rare species implied (hyper-rarity), together with the dominance of a few hundred species (hyper-dominance), is the main ecological outcome.

3.4.4 How much data is enough?

A bootstrap answers whether a sample is large enough. Upscale the sample from \(p^*\) to \(1\) (estimate \(S\)); generate synthetic samples at a scale \(p_{\text{pred}}\) by binomial thinning; upscale them again and compare the new estimate \(S'\) with \(S\). The smallest \(p_{\text{pred}}\) for which \(S' \simeq S\) within the desired precision is the scale that encodes sufficient information. If \(p_{\text{pred}}/p^* > 1\) more fieldwork is needed; if it is below \(1\) the sample is more than sufficient.

3.5 Tropical forests are near a critical point

The relative fluctuations of a species’ abundance are measured by the coefficient of variation \[\mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sqrt{\langle(n - \langle n\rangle)^2\rangle}}{\langle n\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r\,\xi}}\quad \text{(untruncated NB)},\] and for the truncated NB it diverges in the limit \(\xi \to 1\), \(r \to 0\), where the distribution becomes the log-series with a cut-off \(1/(1 - \xi) \to \infty\). In that limit the system has no characteristic abundance scale: births balance deaths (\(b = d\)), the hallmark of a critical point. Fits of \((r, \xi)\) to real forests consistently give \(r \lesssim 1\) and \(\xi\) extremely close to \(1\) (\(\ln(1 - \xi) \sim -10\) to \(-20\)): tropical forests sit very near criticality, with large fluctuations of species abundances across forests.

3.6 A phenomenological renormalisation group

Random placement neglects spatial clustering: trees of the same species aggregate because seeds fall near their parents. Clustering is quantified by the pair correlation function \[g(r) = \frac{\langle n_{\mathbf x}\,n_{\mathbf y}\rangle}{\langle n\rangle^2}, \qquad r = |\mathbf x - \mathbf y|,\] where \(n_{\mathbf x}\) is the abundance in a small cell at \(\mathbf x\) and the average runs over positions and, by neutrality, over species. A spatially explicit neutral model with dispersal predicts \[g(r) = 1 + \frac1T\Bigl(\frac\rho\lambda\Bigr)^2K_0\Bigl(\frac r\lambda\Bigr),\] with \(K_0\) the modified Bessel function of the second kind, \(\lambda\) a correlation length set by dispersal and speciation, and \(T\), \(\rho\) model constants. Taking the continuum (Gamma) version of the NB as the RSA in a circle of radius \(R\), \[P(n \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{\beta\,\Gamma(\alpha)}\Bigl(\frac n\beta\Bigr)^{\alpha - 1}e^{-n/\beta},\] the parameters at each scale are fixed by matching the first two moments, \[\alpha(R) = \frac{\langle N(R)\rangle^2}{\sigma^2(R)}, \qquad \beta(R) = \frac{\sigma^2(R)}{\langle N(R)\rangle},\] with \(\langle N(R)\rangle = \pi R^2\langle n\rangle\) and \(\sigma^2(R)\) obtained by integrating \(g\) over pairs of points in the circle. The flow of \((\alpha, \beta)\) with \(R\) is an empirical renormalisation group: it predicts the RSA, and from it the SAR, at every scale, now including clustering (Azaele et al., 2015).

3.7 Biodiversity from presence–absence data

Abundances are expensive to measure; presence/absence on a grid of cells is cheaper. Two summaries replace the RSA:

  • the relative species occupancy (RSO): the number of species occupying \(c\) cells;
  • the species accumulation curve (SAC): the mean number of species \(\langle S_a\rangle\) found in randomly placed sub-areas of size \(a < a^*\), with \(a^*\) the surveyed area.

Under the NB model the probability that a species is absent from a cell of relative area \(p\) is \(P_p(0)\), computed above. The expected SAC is therefore \[\langle S_a\rangle = S\bigl[1 - P_{a/A}(0)\bigr],\] a closed form in \((r, \xi, S)\). Fitting it to the empirical SAC inside the surveyed area, and extrapolating to \(a = A\), estimates total biodiversity from presence–absence data alone (Tovo et al., 2019). The fitted parameters also predict the RSO, which provides an independent check.

3.8 Exercises

(Negative binomial from the master equation) For \(b_n = bn + r_s\), \(d_n = dn\), show that the stationary distribution is \(\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi)\) with \(r = r_s/b\), \(\xi = b/d\). Compute its mean and variance from the generating function.

Solution. \(P^*_n = P^*_0\prod_{j=1}^n\frac{b(j - 1) + r_s}{dj} = P^*_0\frac{\xi^n}{n!}\prod_{j=1}^n(j - 1 + r) = P^*_0\frac{\Gamma(n + r)}{\Gamma(r)\,n!}\xi^n\). Normalisation gives \(P^*_0 = (1 - \xi)^r\) by the binomial series \(\sum_n\binom{n + r - 1}n\xi^n = (1 - \xi)^{-r}\). With \(G(z) = ((1 - \xi)/(1 - \xi z))^r\): \(G'(1) = \frac{r\xi}{1 - \xi}\) and \(G''(1) = \frac{r(r + 1)\xi^2}{(1 - \xi)^2}\), so the variance is \(G'' + G' - G'^2 = \frac{r\xi}{(1 - \xi)^2}\). The variance-to-mean ratio \(1/(1 - \xi) > 1\) makes the distribution over-dispersed compared to a Poisson.

(Preston octaves of a log-series) For \(\phi_n = \theta x^n/n\) with \(x = 0.99\) and \(\theta = 50\), compute the number of species in octaves \(\{1\}, \{2, 3\}, \{4, \dots, 7\}, \dots\). Explain the shape.

Solution. For \(x \to 1\) each octave \(k \geq 1\) contains \(\theta\sum_{n=2^k}^{2^{k+1}-1}1/n \to \theta\ln2 \simeq 34.7\) species. The first octave has \(\theta x = 49.5\), the second \(\theta(x^2/2 + x^3/3) \simeq 40.7\). With \(x = 0.99\) the cut-off \(1/(1 - x) = 100\) makes octaves beyond \(n \sim 100\) (\(k \geq 7\)) decay exponentially. The Preston plot is a plateau followed by a fall, not a hump: a monotone \(\phi_n\) looks flat when binned in octaves.

(Self-similarity) Verify by direct summation, for \(r = 1\), that binomial sampling maps \(\mathrm{NB}(1, \xi)\) (a geometric distribution) into \(\mathrm{NB}(1, \xi_p)\). Then show that \(\xi_p\) decreases monotonically as \(p\) decreases, and interpret.

Solution. \(P_n = (1 - \xi)\xi^n\). \(P_p(k) = \sum_{n\geq k}\binom nkp^k(1 - p)^{n-k}(1 - \xi)\xi^n = (1 - \xi)(p\xi)^k\sum_{m\geq0}\binom{m + k}k[\xi(1 - p)]^m = \frac{(1 - \xi)(p\xi)^k}{[1 - \xi(1 - p)]^{k+1}}\), which is \((1 - \xi_p)\xi_p^k\) with \(\xi_p = \frac{p\xi}{1 - \xi(1 - p)}\). Since \(\frac{d\xi_p}{dp} = \frac{\xi(1 - \xi)}{[1 - \xi(1 - p)]^2} > 0\), smaller samples have smaller \(\xi_p\): the distribution is pushed toward low abundances, giving the hollow curves of small plots.

(Upscaling a forest) The file RSA_sampled_1percent.xlsx contains the abundances of the \(S^* = 4283\) species observed in a \(1\%\) random sample of a forest (total \(39\,779\) individuals). Fit a truncated NB by maximum likelihood, upscale it to \(p = 1\), and estimate the total number of species. Compare with the estimate from Fisher’s log-series, \(S(J) = \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)\), extrapolated to \(100\) times more individuals.

Solution. Maximising \(\sum_i\ln P^*_{n_i}(r, \xi_p)\) gives \(r = 0.82\) and \(\xi_p = 0.906\). Inverting self-similarity with \(p = 0.01\): \(\xi = \xi_p/(p + \xi_p(1 - p)) = 0.99897\). Then \(S = S^*\frac{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}{1 - (1 - \xi_p)^r} \simeq 4976\). The forest is a computer-generated one with \(4974\) species (Lecture 2), so the NB estimate is essentially exact. The log-series fit to the sample gives \(\theta = 1218\) and, extrapolated to \(100J\) individuals, \(S \simeq 9856\), a twofold overestimate. Fixing \(r = 0\) forces too many rare species.

(Criticality) For the untruncated \(\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi)\) show that \(\mathrm{CV}^2 = 1/(r\xi)\). For the truncated NB with \(r \to 0\) (log-series with parameter \(\xi\)), compute \(\langle n\rangle\) and \(\langle n^2\rangle\) and show that the CV diverges as \(\xi \to 1\).

Solution. Mean \(\frac{r\xi}{1-\xi}\), variance \(\frac{r\xi}{(1-\xi)^2}\), so \(\mathrm{CV}^2 = \frac{1}{r\xi}\). For the log-series \(P_n = \frac{\xi^n}{n\,L}\) with \(L = -\ln(1 - \xi)\): \(\langle n\rangle = \frac{\xi}{(1 - \xi)L}\) and \(\langle n^2\rangle = \frac{\xi}{(1 - \xi)^2L}\). Then \(\mathrm{CV}^2 = \frac{\langle n^2\rangle}{\langle n\rangle^2} - 1 = \frac{L}{\xi} - 1 \simeq -\ln(1 - \xi) \to \infty\). Fluctuations grow without bound as births and deaths balance: the critical point of the neutral model.

(Neutral community simulation) Simulate the mean-field neutral model: a community of \(J = 2000\) individuals; at each step one random individual dies and is replaced with probability \(1 - \nu\) by the offspring of a random individual, and with probability \(\nu\) by a new species. Run to stationarity for \(\nu = 0.005\) and compare the RSA with the log-series \(\theta x^n/n\), \(\theta = 2J\nu/(1 - \nu)\).

Solution. After a transient of order \(J/\nu\) deaths, the number of species fluctuates around Ewens’ \(\langle S\rangle = \sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\frac{\theta}{\theta + i} \simeq \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)\). With \(\theta \simeq 20.1\) this is about \(93\) species. The time-averaged RSA follows \(\theta x^n/n\), with \(x\) fixed by \(\sum_nn\phi_n = J\). Singletons are the most common abundance class, and a few species hold hundreds of individuals. Many rare species and few dominant ones arise from pure drift, without fitness differences.

## Species abundance distributions

A census of a plot records the abundance $n_i$ of every species $i = 1, \dots, S$. Two equivalent summaries are used:

- the **relative species abundance** (RSA) $\phi_n$: the number of species with exactly $n$ individuals;
- the **species abundance distribution** (SAD) $P(n) = \phi_n/S$: the probability that a randomly chosen species has $n$ individuals.

Empirical RSAs are dominated by **rare** species: many species have one or two individuals, and a few are hyper-abundant. Plotted on a linear scale they are hollow curves with very sparse tails. Ecologists therefore use **Preston plots**, which group abundances into octaves $[2^k, 2^{k+1})$ and plot the number of species per octave against $k = \log_2 n$.

An octave collects many more abundance values at large $k$ than at small $k$. The count in octave $k$ is $\sum_{n=2^k}^{2^{k+1}-1}\phi_n \simeq 2^k\phi_{2^k}\ln2$, which is $n\,\phi_n$ rather than $\phi_n$. A log-series $\phi_n = \theta x^n/n$, monotonically decreasing in $n$, therefore looks *flat* per octave (each octave holds about $\theta\ln2$ species) up to $n \sim 1/(1 - x)$, and then drops. A genuine interior maximum in a Preston plot indicates that $n\phi_n$ increases, which the log-series cannot produce but a negative binomial with $r > 1$ can. Always state the binning before interpreting a shape.
:::

## From individual dynamics to the negative binomial

### Density-dependent birth and death

Let $P^s_n(t)$ be the probability that species $s$ has $n$ individuals. If each species follows a one-step process with rates $b^s_n$ and $d^s_n$, the master equation is
$$\frac{dP^s_n}{dt} = b^s_{n-1}P^s_{n-1} + d^s_{n+1}P^s_{n+1} - (b^s_n + d^s_n)P^s_n .$$
The per-capita rates may depend on abundance. Expanding them for large $n$,
$$\frac{b^s_n}{n} = b_s + \frac{r_s}{n} + O(n^{-2}), \qquad \frac{d^s_n}{n} = d_s + \frac{d_1}{n} + O(n^{-2}).$$
Such **density dependence** is observed: seedlings close to adults of the same species suffer more from specialised pests (the *Janzen–Connell effect*), so being rare is an advantage. The simplest choice keeping the leading terms is
$$b^s_n = b_sn + r_s, \qquad d^s_n = d_sn,$$
exactly the linear birth–death–immigration process of Chapter 2. The constant $r_s$ plays the role of immigration (or speciation) and keeps rare species from going extinct.

Treating each species independently amounts to replacing the joint distribution $P(n_1, \dots, n_S)$ by a product $\prod_sP^s(n_s)$: interactions are absorbed into effective, mean-field rates.

### The stationary solution

By the product formula of Chapter 2,
$$P^{s*}_n = P^{s*}_0\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{b_{j-1}}{d_j} = P^{s*}_0\,\frac{\Gamma(n + r)}{\Gamma(r)\,n!}\,\xi^n, \qquad \xi = \frac bd, \quad r = \frac{r_s}{b_s},$$
dropping the label $s$. This is the **negative binomial** distribution
$$P^{\mathrm{NB}}_n(r, \xi) = \binom{n + r - 1}{n}\xi^n(1 - \xi)^r, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,$$
which requires $\xi < 1$ (deaths outweigh births) and $r > 0$. Its generating function is particularly simple:
$$G(z) = \sum_{n\geq0}P^{\mathrm{NB}}_nz^n = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \xi}{1 - \xi z}\Bigr)^r,$$
from which the mean $\langle n\rangle = G'(1) = \frac{r\xi}{1 - \xi}$ and the variance $\frac{r\xi}{(1 - \xi)^2}$ follow. In a census only species that are present are counted, so the RSA is the NB **truncated** to $n \geq 1$:
$$P^*_n(r, \xi) = \frac{1}{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}\binom{n + r - 1}{n}\xi^n(1 - \xi)^r, \qquad n \geq 1 .$$

::: {.proposition name="The log-series as a critical limit"}
As $r \to 0$ the truncated NB tends to Fisher's **log-series**
$$P^*_n \to \frac{1}{-\ln(1 - \xi)}\,\frac{\xi^n}{n}.$$
:::

*Proof.* $\binom{n + r - 1}{n} = \frac{r(r + 1)\cdots(r + n - 1)}{n!} \simeq \frac{r\,(n - 1)!}{n!} = \frac rn$ and $1 - (1 - \xi)^r \simeq -r\ln(1 - \xi)$ as $r \to 0$. $\square$

## Neutral theory

### The neutrality hypothesis

Fitting species-specific $(r_s, \xi_s)$ to thousands of species would need thousands of parameters. Hubbell's **neutral theory** (2001) assumes instead that species on the same trophic level (e.g. all tree species of a forest) are *demographically equivalent*: they have the same per-capita birth, death, dispersal and speciation rates. Different species are then different realisations of the *same* stochastic process, and their abundances differ only by chance (**ecological drift**), not by fitness.

Under neutrality the $S$ species are $S$ independent draws from one distribution, so the expected RSA is simply $\phi_n = S\,P^*_n(r, \xi)$, with two parameters for the whole community. Fits of the NB to the RSA of large tropical forest plots (Barro Colorado Island, Pasoh, Korup, Lambir, Yasuní, Sinharaja) are excellent. Neutrality is surprising, since species obviously differ, and controversial. Its success says that, for the *abundance distribution*, differences between species average out in very diverse communities. It is a null model, and departures from it point to the biology that matters.

### The voter model with speciation

Neutral theory can also be formulated in a spatially explicit, zero-sum way. A landscape (say, a lattice) is saturated by $J$ individuals. At each step a random individual dies and is replaced either by the offspring of a neighbour (probability $1 - \nu$) or by an individual of a brand-new species (speciation, probability $\nu$). Without speciation this is the **voter model** of statistical physics: domains of the same "opinion" coarsen and one species eventually takes over. Speciation injects diversity and produces a statistically stationary community.

In the well-mixed (mean-field) version, the abundance of each species performs a birth–death walk with $b \lesssim d$, and new species enter at rate $\nu$. The resulting metacommunity RSA is the log-series
$$\langle\phi_n\rangle = \theta\,\frac{x^n}{n}, \qquad \theta \simeq \frac{2J\nu}{1 - \nu},$$
where $\theta$ is Hubbell's **fundamental biodiversity number** and $x = b/d \to 1$ for large communities. The mean number of species found among $J$ individuals is $\langle S\rangle = \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)$ (Ewens' sampling formula).

### A local community fed by a metacommunity

Real plots are open: they receive immigrants from a large surrounding **metacommunity**, the "continent" to the plot's "island". Consider an island of fixed size $J$ (zero-sum dynamics). At each event an individual dies, and the vacancy is filled with probability $1 - m$ by local reproduction and with probability $m$ by an immigrant drawn from the metacommunity, where species $k$ has relative abundance $\mu_k/J_M$. The rates for species $k$ with $n$ individuals on the island are
$$b_{n,k} = (1 - m)\Bigl(1 - \frac nJ\Bigr)\frac{n}{J - 1} + m\Bigl(1 - \frac nJ\Bigr)\frac{\mu_k}{J_M},$$
$$d_{n,k} = (1 - m)\frac nJ\,\frac{J - n}{J - 1} + m\,\frac nJ\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mu_k}{J_M}\Bigr).$$
In $b_{n,k}$ an individual of *another* species dies (probability $1 - n/J$) and is replaced by a local offspring of $k$ or by an immigrant of $k$. In $d_{n,k}$ an individual of $k$ dies and is replaced by another species. With
$$\lambda_k = \frac{m}{1 - m}(J - 1)\frac{\mu_k}{J_M}, \qquad \theta_k = J + \frac{m}{1 - m}(J - 1)\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mu_k}{J_M}\Bigr),$$
the rates become $b_{n,k} = \frac{1 - m}{J}\frac{J - n}{J - 1}(n + \lambda_k)$ and $d_{n,k} = \frac{1 - m}{J - 1}\frac nJ(\theta_k - n)$, and the stationary distribution is
$$P^*_{n,k} = P_{0,k}\,\frac{J!}{n!\,(J - n)!}\,\frac{\Gamma(n + \lambda_k)}{\Gamma(\lambda_k)}\,\frac{\Gamma(\theta_k - n)}{\Gamma(\theta_k)} .$$
On a log scale it resembles a log-normal, but it comes from a mechanism, which is why Volkov et al. (2003) prefer it to the phenomenological log-normal. The island RSA follows by averaging over the metacommunity abundances, $\phi_n = S_M\sum_kP^*_{n,k}P(\mu_k)$. Strong dispersal limitation (small $m$) depletes rare species locally and produces the hump of empirical Preston plots.

## Upscaling: from a sample to the whole forest

### The problem

The Amazon holds about $10^{11}$ trees; no census can count them. Typically, plots covering a fraction $p = a/A \ll 1$ of a forest of area $A$ are sampled. How many species does the whole forest contain? Rare species are mostly missed by small samples, so the RSA is **scale dependent**. At small $p$ it is a hollow curve dominated by singletons. At larger $p$, species accumulate individuals, mass moves out of the lowest abundance classes, and the curve develops an interior maximum. Linear extrapolation of $S$ fails.

### Binomial sampling and self-similarity

Assume individuals are spread over the forest independently of each other (**random placement**, or well-mixed). If a species has $n$ individuals in total, the number $k$ found in a region covering a fraction $p$ of the area is binomial:
$$P_p(k \mid n) = \binom nkp^k(1 - p)^{n - k}.$$
The abundance distribution at scale $p$ is then
$$P_p(k) = \sum_{n\geq k}\binom nkp^k(1 - p)^{n-k}\,P_n(r, \xi).$$

::: {.theorem name="Self-similarity of the negative binomial"}
If abundances at scale $1$ are $\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi)$, then abundances at scale $p$ are $\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi_p)$ with the same $r$ and
$$\xi_p = \frac{p\,\xi}{1 - \xi(1 - p)} .$$
:::

*Proof.* Binomial thinning acts on generating functions as $G_p(z) = G(1 - p + pz)$: each individual is kept with probability $p$. For the NB,
$$G_p(z) = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \xi}{1 - \xi + \xi p - \xi pz}\Bigr)^r = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \xi_p}{1 - \xi_pz}\Bigr)^r, \qquad 1 - \xi_p = \frac{1 - \xi}{1 - \xi(1 - p)},$$
again an NB, with $\xi_p$ as stated. $\square$

The NB is a fixed form under coarse-graining, like a renormalisation-group fixed point: only $\xi$ flows with scale, $r$ does not. Inverting,
$$\xi = \frac{\xi_p}{p + \xi_p(1 - p)} .$$

::: {.callout-caution}    
## Zero size probability of a species

At scale $p$ there is a non-zero probability of having a zero size population species, since 

$$
P_p(k)\equiv NB(r,\xi_p)
$$
Which normalization is:
$$
|\sum_{i=0}^{n} NB(r,\xi_p)=1 
$$
:::


### Estimating the number of species

A species is missed by the sample with probability $P_p(0)$. If $S^*$ species are observed at scale $p$, then $S^* = S/[1 - P_p(0)]$. For the NB truncated at scale $1$, $P_p(0) = \frac{(1 - \xi_p)^r - (1 - \xi)^r}{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}$, hence
$$S = S^*\,\frac{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}{1 - (1 - \xi_p)^r}.$$
The recipe: fit a truncated NB to the sample RSA to get $(r, \xi_p)$; compute $\xi$ from self-similarity; compute $S$. The same machinery predicts the RSA and the species–area relationship at any scale $p' \in (p, 1]$.

The method was validated on computer-generated forests, where the truth is known, and on fully censused plots sub-sampled at random. It outperforms classical estimators based on the log-series or on non-parametric corrections (Chao). Applied to tropical forests (Tovo et al., 2017), it predicts, for instance, about $366 \pm 15$ tree species at Barro Colorado from $301$ observed, and an Amazonian richness of order $10^4$ species. The large number of hyper-rare species implied (*hyper-rarity*), together with the dominance of a few hundred species (*hyper-dominance*), is the main ecological outcome.

### How much data is enough?

A bootstrap answers whether a sample is large enough. Upscale the sample from $p^*$ to $1$ (estimate $S$); generate synthetic samples at a scale $p_{\text{pred}}$ by binomial thinning; upscale them again and compare the new estimate $S'$ with $S$. The smallest $p_{\text{pred}}$ for which $S' \simeq S$ within the desired precision is the scale that *encodes sufficient information*. If $p_{\text{pred}}/p^* > 1$ more fieldwork is needed; if it is below $1$ the sample is more than sufficient.

## Tropical forests are near a critical point

The relative fluctuations of a species' abundance are measured by the coefficient of variation
$$\mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sqrt{\langle(n - \langle n\rangle)^2\rangle}}{\langle n\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r\,\xi}}\quad \text{(untruncated NB)},$$
and for the truncated NB it diverges in the limit $\xi \to 1$, $r \to 0$, where the distribution becomes the log-series with a cut-off $1/(1 - \xi) \to \infty$. In that limit the system has no characteristic abundance scale: births balance deaths ($b = d$), the hallmark of a critical point. Fits of $(r, \xi)$ to real forests consistently give $r \lesssim 1$ and $\xi$ extremely close to $1$ ($\ln(1 - \xi) \sim -10$ to $-20$): tropical forests sit very near criticality, with large fluctuations of species abundances across forests.

## A phenomenological renormalisation group

Random placement neglects spatial clustering: trees of the same species aggregate because seeds fall near their parents. Clustering is quantified by the **pair correlation function**
$$g(r) = \frac{\langle n_{\mathbf x}\,n_{\mathbf y}\rangle}{\langle n\rangle^2}, \qquad r = |\mathbf x - \mathbf y|,$$
where $n_{\mathbf x}$ is the abundance in a small cell at $\mathbf x$ and the average runs over positions and, by neutrality, over species. A spatially explicit neutral model with dispersal predicts
$$g(r) = 1 + \frac1T\Bigl(\frac\rho\lambda\Bigr)^2K_0\Bigl(\frac r\lambda\Bigr),$$
with $K_0$ the modified Bessel function of the second kind, $\lambda$ a correlation length set by dispersal and speciation, and $T$, $\rho$ model constants. Taking the continuum (Gamma) version of the NB as the RSA in a circle of radius $R$,
$$P(n \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{\beta\,\Gamma(\alpha)}\Bigl(\frac n\beta\Bigr)^{\alpha - 1}e^{-n/\beta},$$
the parameters at each scale are fixed by matching the first two moments,
$$\alpha(R) = \frac{\langle N(R)\rangle^2}{\sigma^2(R)}, \qquad \beta(R) = \frac{\sigma^2(R)}{\langle N(R)\rangle},$$
with $\langle N(R)\rangle = \pi R^2\langle n\rangle$ and $\sigma^2(R)$ obtained by integrating $g$ over pairs of points in the circle. The flow of $(\alpha, \beta)$ with $R$ is an empirical renormalisation group: it predicts the RSA, and from it the SAR, at every scale, now including clustering (Azaele et al., 2015).

## Biodiversity from presence–absence data

Abundances are expensive to measure; presence/absence on a grid of cells is cheaper. Two summaries replace the RSA:

- the **relative species occupancy** (RSO): the number of species occupying $c$ cells;
- the **species accumulation curve** (SAC): the mean number of species $\langle S_a\rangle$ found in randomly placed sub-areas of size $a < a^*$, with $a^*$ the surveyed area.

Under the NB model the probability that a species is absent from a cell of relative area $p$ is $P_p(0)$, computed above. The expected SAC is therefore
$$\langle S_a\rangle = S\bigl[1 - P_{a/A}(0)\bigr],$$
a closed form in $(r, \xi, S)$. Fitting it to the empirical SAC inside the surveyed area, and extrapolating to $a = A$, estimates total biodiversity from presence–absence data alone (Tovo et al., 2019). The fitted parameters also predict the RSO, which provides an independent check.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(Negative binomial from the master equation)** For $b_n = bn + r_s$, $d_n = dn$, show that the stationary distribution is $\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi)$ with $r = r_s/b$, $\xi = b/d$. Compute its mean and variance from the generating function.
:::

::: {.solution}
$P^*_n = P^*_0\prod_{j=1}^n\frac{b(j - 1) + r_s}{dj} = P^*_0\frac{\xi^n}{n!}\prod_{j=1}^n(j - 1 + r) = P^*_0\frac{\Gamma(n + r)}{\Gamma(r)\,n!}\xi^n$. Normalisation gives $P^*_0 = (1 - \xi)^r$ by the binomial series $\sum_n\binom{n + r - 1}n\xi^n = (1 - \xi)^{-r}$. With $G(z) = ((1 - \xi)/(1 - \xi z))^r$: $G'(1) = \frac{r\xi}{1 - \xi}$ and $G''(1) = \frac{r(r + 1)\xi^2}{(1 - \xi)^2}$, so the variance is $G'' + G' - G'^2 = \frac{r\xi}{(1 - \xi)^2}$. The variance-to-mean ratio $1/(1 - \xi) > 1$ makes the distribution over-dispersed compared to a Poisson.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Preston octaves of a log-series)** For $\phi_n = \theta x^n/n$ with $x = 0.99$ and $\theta = 50$, compute the number of species in octaves $\{1\}, \{2, 3\}, \{4, \dots, 7\}, \dots$. Explain the shape.
:::

::: {.solution}
For $x \to 1$ each octave $k \geq 1$ contains $\theta\sum_{n=2^k}^{2^{k+1}-1}1/n \to \theta\ln2 \simeq 34.7$ species. The first octave has $\theta x = 49.5$, the second $\theta(x^2/2 + x^3/3) \simeq 40.7$. With $x = 0.99$ the cut-off $1/(1 - x) = 100$ makes octaves beyond $n \sim 100$ ($k \geq 7$) decay exponentially. The Preston plot is a plateau followed by a fall, not a hump: a monotone $\phi_n$ looks flat when binned in octaves.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Self-similarity)** Verify by direct summation, for $r = 1$, that binomial sampling maps $\mathrm{NB}(1, \xi)$ (a geometric distribution) into $\mathrm{NB}(1, \xi_p)$. Then show that $\xi_p$ decreases monotonically as $p$ decreases, and interpret.
:::

::: {.solution}
$P_n = (1 - \xi)\xi^n$. $P_p(k) = \sum_{n\geq k}\binom nkp^k(1 - p)^{n-k}(1 - \xi)\xi^n = (1 - \xi)(p\xi)^k\sum_{m\geq0}\binom{m + k}k[\xi(1 - p)]^m = \frac{(1 - \xi)(p\xi)^k}{[1 - \xi(1 - p)]^{k+1}}$, which is $(1 - \xi_p)\xi_p^k$ with $\xi_p = \frac{p\xi}{1 - \xi(1 - p)}$. Since $\frac{d\xi_p}{dp} = \frac{\xi(1 - \xi)}{[1 - \xi(1 - p)]^2} > 0$, smaller samples have smaller $\xi_p$: the distribution is pushed toward low abundances, giving the hollow curves of small plots.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Upscaling a forest)** The file `RSA_sampled_1percent.xlsx` contains the abundances of the $S^* = 4283$ species observed in a $1\%$ random sample of a forest (total $39\,779$ individuals). Fit a truncated NB by maximum likelihood, upscale it to $p = 1$, and estimate the total number of species. Compare with the estimate from Fisher's log-series, $S(J) = \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)$, extrapolated to $100$ times more individuals.
:::

::: {.solution}
Maximising $\sum_i\ln P^*_{n_i}(r, \xi_p)$ gives $r = 0.82$ and $\xi_p = 0.906$. Inverting self-similarity with $p = 0.01$: $\xi = \xi_p/(p + \xi_p(1 - p)) = 0.99897$. Then $S = S^*\frac{1 - (1 - \xi)^r}{1 - (1 - \xi_p)^r} \simeq 4976$. The forest is a computer-generated one with $4974$ species (Lecture 2), so the NB estimate is essentially exact. The log-series fit to the sample gives $\theta = 1218$ and, extrapolated to $100J$ individuals, $S \simeq 9856$, a twofold overestimate. Fixing $r = 0$ forces too many rare species.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Criticality)** For the untruncated $\mathrm{NB}(r, \xi)$ show that $\mathrm{CV}^2 = 1/(r\xi)$. For the truncated NB with $r \to 0$ (log-series with parameter $\xi$), compute $\langle n\rangle$ and $\langle n^2\rangle$ and show that the CV diverges as $\xi \to 1$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Mean $\frac{r\xi}{1-\xi}$, variance $\frac{r\xi}{(1-\xi)^2}$, so $\mathrm{CV}^2 = \frac{1}{r\xi}$. For the log-series $P_n = \frac{\xi^n}{n\,L}$ with $L = -\ln(1 - \xi)$: $\langle n\rangle = \frac{\xi}{(1 - \xi)L}$ and $\langle n^2\rangle = \frac{\xi}{(1 - \xi)^2L}$. Then $\mathrm{CV}^2 = \frac{\langle n^2\rangle}{\langle n\rangle^2} - 1 = \frac{L}{\xi} - 1 \simeq -\ln(1 - \xi) \to \infty$. Fluctuations grow without bound as births and deaths balance: the critical point of the neutral model.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Neutral community simulation)** Simulate the mean-field neutral model: a community of $J = 2000$ individuals; at each step one random individual dies and is replaced with probability $1 - \nu$ by the offspring of a random individual, and with probability $\nu$ by a new species. Run to stationarity for $\nu = 0.005$ and compare the RSA with the log-series $\theta x^n/n$, $\theta = 2J\nu/(1 - \nu)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
After a transient of order $J/\nu$ deaths, the number of species fluctuates around Ewens' $\langle S\rangle = \sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\frac{\theta}{\theta + i} \simeq \theta\ln(1 + J/\theta)$. With $\theta \simeq 20.1$ this is about $93$ species. The time-averaged RSA follows $\theta x^n/n$, with $x$ fixed by $\sum_nn\phi_n = J$. Singletons are the most common abundance class, and a few species hold hundreds of individuals. Many rare species and few dominant ones arise from pure drift, without fitness differences.
:::
