11  Plasticity, learning and associative memory

Long-term memories are thought to be stored in the strengths of synapses, which change with experience: synaptic plasticity. This chapter formalises Hebb’s idea that “neurons that fire together wire together”, shows why the naive rule is unstable, and develops stabilised rules (covariance, normalisation, BCM, Oja), which turn a neuron into a principal-component analyser. Hebbian learning in recurrent networks then produces associative memories: activity patterns stored as attractors of the dynamics. The Hopfield network makes this precise with an energy function, a capacity, and spurious states. A final section on reservoir computing shows a complementary, supervised use of recurrent dynamics.

11.1 Hebbian plasticity

11.1.1 Hebb’s rule and its problems

Consider a single postsynaptic neuron with linear dynamics \(\tau_r\dot v = -v + \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u\) driven by inputs \(\mathbf u\). Plasticity is much slower than neural dynamics, so \(v = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u\) at all times. Hebb’s (1949) conjecture becomes \[\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = \langle v\,\mathbf u\rangle = Q\,\mathbf w, \qquad Q = \langle\mathbf u\mathbf u^\top\rangle,\] averaged over the input patterns. The input correlation matrix \(Q\) is positive semi-definite, so \(\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf w|^2 = \frac2{\tau_w}\langle v^2\rangle \geq 0\). Three problems follow:

  1. Unbounded growth: weights grow exponentially along the leading eigenvector of \(Q\), requiring saturation by hand (\(0 \leq w \leq w_{\max}\)).
  2. No depression: with positive rates, weights can only increase, yet long-term depression (LTD) is observed alongside long-term potentiation (LTP).
  3. No competition: all synapses grow together, while learning requires some synapses to win at the expense of others.

11.1.2 Covariance rules

Thresholds introduce depression. Plasticity is potentiating if the postsynaptic rate exceeds \(\theta_v\) and depressing otherwise: \[\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = \langle(v - \theta_v)\mathbf u\rangle \qquad\text{or}\qquad \tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = \langle v(\mathbf u - \boldsymbol\theta_u)\rangle .\] Choosing \(\theta_v = \langle v\rangle\) or \(\boldsymbol\theta_u = \langle\mathbf u\rangle\) gives \(\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w\), with \(C = \langle(\mathbf u - \langle\mathbf u\rangle)(\mathbf u - \langle\mathbf u\rangle)^\top\rangle\) the input covariance matrix: these are the covariance rules. They allow LTD but still grow without bound.

11.1.3 Normalisation and competition

Subtractive normalisation keeps the total weight fixed: \[\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = v\mathbf u - \frac{v(\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf u)}{N}\mathbf 1, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf w) = \frac{v\,\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf u}{\tau_w}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf 1}{N}\Bigr) = 0 .\] It is highly competitive: weights are reduced by the same amount, so small weights shrink proportionally more and are driven to zero.

The BCM rule (Bienenstock, Cooper, Munro, 1982) uses a sliding threshold, \(\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = v\mathbf u(v - \theta_v)\), with \(\tau_\theta\dot\theta_v = v^2 - \theta_v\). If a synapse gets strong, the threshold rises and makes further potentiation harder: stability through homeostasis.

Oja’s rule (1982) adds a multiplicative decay: \[\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = v\,\mathbf u - \alpha v^2\,\mathbf w, \qquad \alpha > 0 .\]

For zero-mean inputs with covariance \(C\), the averaged Oja dynamics \(\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w - \alpha(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w\) converges to \(\mathbf w^* = \pm\mathbf e_1/\sqrt\alpha\), where \(\mathbf e_1\) is the eigenvector of \(C\) with the largest eigenvalue.

Proof. (i) Norm: \(\tau_w\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf w|^2 = 2(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)(1 - \alpha|\mathbf w|^2)\), so \(|\mathbf w|^2 \to 1/\alpha\). (ii) Fixed points satisfy \(C\mathbf w = \lambda\mathbf w\) with \(\lambda = \alpha\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w\): eigenvectors of \(C\), normalised to \(1/\sqrt\alpha\). (iii) Linearising around \(\mathbf e_\mu/\sqrt\alpha\), a perturbation along \(\mathbf e_\nu\) grows at rate \(\lambda_\nu - \lambda_\mu\). Only \(\mu = 1\), the largest eigenvalue, is stable. \(\square\)

The neuron’s output \(v = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u\) becomes the projection of the input onto its direction of largest variance. A single Hebbian neuron performs principal component analysis, an unsupervised compression of its input. Networks of such neurons with lateral inhibition (Sanger’s rule) extract several components.

11.2 Associative memory

Recurrent networks can hold short-term memories (the integrator of Chapter 10). Long-term associative memory stores patterns so that a partial or noisy cue recalls the complete pattern, as Pavlov’s dog associates the bell with food. The idea is to make the stored patterns fixed points of the network dynamics, each with a basin of attraction: all initial conditions sufficiently similar to a memory flow toward it. Learning is unsupervised. Given patterns \(\mathbf v_m\), \(m = 1, \dots, N_{\text{mem}}\), choose weights such that \(\mathbf v_m = \psi(M\mathbf v_m)\).

For rate networks with sparse binary patterns, \(v_m(i) \in \{0, c\}\) with a fraction \(\alpha\) of active units, a weight matrix that works is the Hebbian covariance construction \[M = \frac{\lambda}{c^2\alpha N(1 - \alpha)}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{mem}}}(\mathbf v_n - \alpha c\mathbf 1)(\mathbf v_n - \alpha c\mathbf 1)^\top - \frac{\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top}{\alpha N},\] whose first term makes each pattern an approximate eigenvector, using that uncorrelated patterns overlap only by \(\mathbf v_n\cdot\mathbf v_m \simeq N\alpha^2c^2\). The second term is a global inhibition that keeps inactive units off. With a threshold-like \(\psi\) satisfying \(\psi(-c(1 + \alpha\lambda)) = 0\) and \(\psi(c(\lambda - 1 - \alpha\lambda)) = c\), each pattern is a fixed point. The construction is symmetric, which guarantees convergence to fixed points (a Lyapunov function exists). As more patterns are stored, the crosstalk between them grows and the memories degrade: capacity is finite.

11.3 The Hopfield network

11.3.1 Definition and energy

Hopfield (1982) considered \(N\) binary units \(\sigma_i = \pm1\) with symmetric couplings \(J_{ij} = J_{ji}\), \(J_{ii} = 0\), and thresholds \(h_i\). Asynchronous dynamics picks a unit at random and aligns it with its local field: \[\sigma_i \leftarrow \operatorname{sign}\Bigl(\sum_jJ_{ij}\sigma_j - h_i\Bigr).\] The dynamics never increases the energy \[\mathcal E(\boldsymbol\sigma) = -\frac12\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j + \sum_ih_i\sigma_i .\] Flipping \(\sigma_i\) changes the energy by \(\Delta\mathcal E = 2\sigma_i\bigl(\sum_jJ_{ij}\sigma_j - h_i\bigr)\), and the update flips \(\sigma_i\) only when this is negative. Since \(\mathcal E\) is bounded below and decreases at each effective flip, the dynamics reaches a local minimum in finite time: a Metropolis algorithm at zero temperature. Symmetry of \(J\) is essential. With asymmetric couplings no energy exists and the dynamics can cycle.

11.3.2 The Hebb (Hopfield) rule

To store \(p\) patterns \(\boldsymbol\xi^\mu \in \{\pm1\}^N\), set (\(h_i = 0\)) \[J_{ij} = \frac1N\sum_{\mu=1}^p\xi^\mu_i\xi^\mu_j, \qquad i \neq j .\] Units that agree in a pattern get an excitatory coupling, units that disagree an inhibitory one. The energy becomes \[\mathcal E = -\frac N2\sum_\mu m_\mu^2 + \frac p2, \qquad m_\mu = \frac1N\sum_i\xi^\mu_i\sigma_i,\] where \(m_\mu\) is the overlap with pattern \(\mu\). Each stored pattern (\(m_\mu = 1\)) is therefore a low-energy configuration. The local field on unit \(i\) in state \(\boldsymbol\xi^\nu\) is \[h_i = \sum_jJ_{ij}\xi^\nu_j = \xi^\nu_i + \frac1N\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}\sum_{j\neq i}\xi^\mu_i\xi^\mu_j\xi^\nu_j .\] The first term is the signal, which stabilises the pattern. The second is crosstalk from the other patterns: a sum of about \(Np\) random \(\pm1\) terms divided by \(N\), Gaussian with variance \(p/N\). A unit is unstable when the crosstalk exceeds \(1\) with the wrong sign, with probability \(P_{\text{err}} \simeq \frac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{N/2p}\bigr)\). For small \(p/N\) errors are rare. As \(p/N\) grows, errors cascade.

11.3.3 Capacity and spurious states

The statistical mechanics of the model (Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky, 1985) treats it as a spin glass at temperature \(T\). Random patterns can be retrieved, with overlaps close to \(1\), as long as the load satisfies \[\alpha = \frac pN < \alpha_c \simeq 0.138 .\] At \(\alpha_c\) the retrieval states disappear discontinuously and the network falls into a spin-glass phase with no relation to the stored memories (catastrophic forgetting). Besides the stored patterns and their inverses \(-\boldsymbol\xi^\mu\) (the energy is even), there are spurious minima. The best known are odd mixtures \(\operatorname{sign}(\pm\boldsymbol\xi^{\mu_1} \pm \boldsymbol\xi^{\mu_2} \pm \boldsymbol\xi^{\mu_3})\), which have overlap \(1/2\) with each of three patterns. At large loads there are also spin-glass states. A finite temperature (stochastic updates, Chapter 12) destabilises the shallow spurious minima before the stored ones, which helps retrieval.

11.3.4 Variants

  • Sparse patterns with activity \(a \ll 1\) and covariance couplings \(J_{ij} \propto \sum_\mu(\xi^\mu_i - a)(\xi^\mu_j - a)\) have a much larger capacity, \(\alpha_c \sim 1/(a|\ln a|)\).
  • Diluted networks, where each unit connects to only a fraction of others (like cortex), keep a capacity proportional to the number of connections per unit.
  • Robustness: deleting a random fraction of the couplings degrades retrieval gracefully, a property shared by biological memory.

The Hopfield model also appears in biology beyond neuroscience. Gene-expression patterns of cell types behave like stored attractors of regulatory networks (Chapters 16–17).

11.4 Supervised learning: reservoir computing

A different use of recurrent networks keeps the recurrent weights random and fixed and trains only a linear readout. The reservoir has state \(\mathbf x\) obeying \[\tau_x\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = -\mathbf x + W_{\text{in}}I(t) + W_{\text{rec}}\,\psi(\mathbf x), \qquad y(t) = \mathbf W_{\text{out}}\cdot\psi(\mathbf x(t)).\] With \(W_{\text{rec}}\) random and scaled near the edge of chaos (spectral radius \(\lesssim 1\)), the reservoir maps the input history into a rich, high-dimensional nonlinear representation. Training reduces to linear regression. With \(\mathcal U\) the matrix whose columns are the reservoir states \(\mathbf u_t = \psi(\mathbf x_t)\) and \(\mathbf v\) the target outputs, minimising the mean squared error gives \[\mathbf W_{\text{out}} = (\mathcal U\mathcal U^\top + \beta\mathbb 1)^{-1}\mathcal U\mathbf v,\] with a ridge parameter \(\beta\) against overfitting. Online alternatives are stochastic gradient descent and the FORCE algorithm, which also trains feedback weights (Sussillo and Abbott, 2009). Echo-state networks predict chaotic time series and generate complex motor patterns (Jaeger and Haas, 2004).

11.5 Exercises

(Oja’s rule, class exercise Q1) Train a single linear neuron with Oja’s rule (\(\eta = 0.001\), \(\alpha = 0.1\), \(w(0)\) of norm \(5\) in a random direction) on \(p = 10^4\) points drawn from \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbb 1)\) in two dimensions. Repeat from several initial conditions. What do the weights converge to, and why?

Solution. The norm always converges to \(1/\sqrt\alpha = 3.16\) (simulations: \(3.1630\) in three runs), but the direction is different in each run (\((-2.61, 1.78)\), \((-0.88, 3.04)\), \((1.78, -2.61)\)). For isotropic data \(C = \mathbb 1\), every direction is an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue: the first principal component is not defined. The rule fixes the norm, while the direction diffuses neutrally, driven only by sampling noise.

(Oja’s rule, Q2) Repeat with \(p = 1000\) points of covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(10, 1)\), mean \((10, 10)\), rotated by \(30°\). Do the weights still find the first principal component?

Solution. No. Without centring the data the rule converges to the leading eigenvector of the second-moment matrix \(\langle\mathbf u\mathbf u^\top\rangle = C + \boldsymbol\mu\boldsymbol\mu^\top\), which is dominated by the mean. The simulated weight direction is \((0.27, 0.96)\), essentially the mean direction \((0.26, 0.97)\), while the true first principal component is \((0.88, 0.48)\). Oja’s rule performs PCA only on zero-mean inputs. Biologically, inputs must be centred first, e.g. by adaptation, which subtracts the mean rate.

(Oja’s rule, Q3) Compare Oja’s rule with \(\Delta\mathbf w = \eta\bigl[C\mathbf w - \alpha\mathbf w(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w)\bigr]\) on centred data.

Solution. At a fixed point of the new rule, \(C\mathbf w = \alpha|\mathbf w|^2\mathbf w\), so \(\mathbf w\) is again an eigenvector, and the same stability argument selects \(\mathbf e_1\). The norm is different: \(|\mathbf w|^2 = \lambda_1/\alpha\) instead of \(1/\alpha\). With covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(10, 1)\) rotated by \(30°\) (\(\lambda_1 \simeq 10.35\) in the sample), Oja’s rule gives \(|\mathbf w| = 3.16\) while the new rule gives \(|\mathbf w| \simeq \sqrt{\lambda_1/\alpha} = 10.2\) (about \(9.6\) after five epochs of online learning, still converging). Both point along \((0.87, 0.50)\). The new rule encodes the variance of the leading component in the weight norm, whereas Oja’s rule normalises it away.

(Instability of Hebb’s rule) For \(\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = Q\mathbf w\) with \(Q = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\), solve for \(\mathbf w(t)\) from \(\mathbf w(0) = (1, 0)\). Show that the direction converges while the norm diverges, and add subtractive normalisation to see what changes.

Solution. Eigenvalues \(3\) (along \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\)) and \(1\) (along \((1, -1)/\sqrt2\)). \(\mathbf w(t) = \frac12e^{3t/\tau_w}(1, 1) + \frac12e^{t/\tau_w}(1, -1)\): the direction tends to \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\) but \(|\mathbf w|\) grows as \(e^{3t/\tau_w}\). With subtractive normalisation the projection on \(\mathbf 1\) is frozen at \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\), and only the component along \((1, -1)\) evolves, at rate \(\lambda_2 = 1\): \(\mathbf w(t) = \frac12(1, 1) + \frac12e^{t/\tau_w}(1, -1)\). This grows until the bounds are hit, ending at \((1, 0)\) or \((0, 1)\): one synapse wins and the other is eliminated. Normalisation turns Hebbian growth into competition.

(Hopfield capacity, class exercise 1) Store \(p\) random patterns in a network of \(N = 400\) units and test retrieval from a \(5\%\)-corrupted version of each pattern using asynchronous zero-temperature updates. Plot the fraction of successful retrievals (final overlap \(> 0.9\)) against \(\alpha = p/N\).

Solution. Simulation (up to \(20\) patterns tested per load): \(\alpha = 0.05\): \(100\%\); \(0.10\): \(100\%\); \(0.12\): \(90\%\); \(0.14\): \(95\%\); \(0.16\): \(65\%\); \(0.20\): \(30\%\). Retrieval is essentially perfect below \(\alpha \approx 0.12\) and collapses between \(0.14\) and \(0.2\), consistent with the theoretical \(\alpha_c \simeq 0.138\); finite-size effects smooth the transition. With the signal-to-noise estimate, the single-step error probability at \(\alpha = 0.14\) is \(\frac12\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{1/0.28}) \approx 0.4\%\). Errors then cascade, which is why the actual threshold is lower than a naive estimate would suggest.

(HW 5) Playing with the Hopfield code. Use the course code hopfield.py: load images, convert them to \(\pm1\) patterns, store them with the Hebb rule, and retrieve them from corrupted cues. Summarise your observations in half a page.

Solution. A typical report:

  • Storage and retrieval. With a few (\(2\)–\(4\)) images of \(N \sim 10^3\)–\(10^4\) pixels, corrupting \(20\)–\(30\%\) of a pattern and running the dynamics recovers it exactly within a few sweeps, while the energy decreases monotonically.
  • Inverse states. Very corrupted cues (\(> 50\%\) flipped) converge to the inverted image, the other minimum of the even energy.
  • Correlated patterns. Natural images are correlated (large uniform regions, similar backgrounds), so their crosstalk is much larger than for random patterns. Storing several similar images produces mixtures: the capacity for correlated patterns is far below \(0.138N\). Centring the patterns (subtracting the mean activity), as in the covariance rule, improves it.
  • Mixtures. Starting from a superposition of three patterns often ends in the spurious mixture state.
  • Temperature. Running at small \(T\) (stochastic updates) helps escape spurious states. Too much noise destroys the memories.
  • Damage. Setting a fraction of weights to zero leaves retrieval nearly intact until a large fraction is removed.

(Spurious mixture states) For three patterns, show that \(\boldsymbol\sigma = \operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol\xi^1 + \boldsymbol\xi^2 + \boldsymbol\xi^3)\) has overlap \(1/2\) with each, and compute its energy relative to a pure pattern for large \(N\) and \(p = 3\).

Solution. At each site, the sign of the sum agrees with \(\xi^1_i\) unless both \(\xi^2_i\) and \(\xi^3_i\) disagree with it, which has probability \(1/4\). So \(m_1 = \frac34 - \frac14 = \frac12\), and likewise for \(m_2\), \(m_3\). The energy is \(\mathcal E = -\frac N2\sum_\mu m_\mu^2 = -\frac N2\cdot\frac34 = -0.375N\), against \(-\frac N2(1 + O(1/N))\) for a pure pattern (whose overlaps with the other two are \(O(N^{-1/2})\)). The mixture is a local minimum, but shallower than the stored memories, so a small temperature removes it first.

This chapter formalises Hebb's idea that "neurons that fire together wire together", shows why the naive rule is unstable, and develops stabilised rules (covariance, normalisation, BCM, Oja), which turn a neuron into a principal-component analyser. ## Hebbian plasticity

### Hebb's rule and its problems

Consider a single postsynaptic neuron with linear dynamics $\tau_r\dot v = -v + \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u$ driven by inputs $\mathbf u$. Plasticity is much slower than neural dynamics, so $v = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u$ at all times. Hebb's (1949) conjecture becomes
$$\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = \langle v\,\mathbf u\rangle = Q\,\mathbf w, \qquad Q = \langle\mathbf u\mathbf u^\top\rangle,$$
averaged over the input patterns. The input **correlation matrix** $Q$ is positive semi-definite, so $\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf w|^2 = \frac2{\tau_w}\langle v^2\rangle \geq 0$. Three problems follow:

1. **Unbounded growth**: weights grow exponentially along the leading eigenvector of $Q$, requiring saturation by hand ($0 \leq w \leq w_{\max}$).
2. **No depression**: with positive rates, weights can only increase, yet long-term depression (LTD) is observed alongside long-term potentiation (LTP).
3. **No competition**: all synapses grow together, while learning requires some synapses to win at the expense of others.

### Covariance rules

Thresholds introduce depression. Plasticity is potentiating if the postsynaptic rate exceeds $\theta_v$ and depressing otherwise:
$$\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = \langle(v - \theta_v)\mathbf u\rangle \qquad\text{or}\qquad \tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = \langle v(\mathbf u - \boldsymbol\theta_u)\rangle .$$
Choosing $\theta_v = \langle v\rangle$ or $\boldsymbol\theta_u = \langle\mathbf u\rangle$ gives $\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w$, with $C = \langle(\mathbf u - \langle\mathbf u\rangle)(\mathbf u - \langle\mathbf u\rangle)^\top\rangle$ the input **covariance** matrix: these are the **covariance rules**. They allow LTD but still grow without bound.

### Normalisation and competition

**Subtractive normalisation** keeps the total weight fixed:
$$\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = v\mathbf u - \frac{v(\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf u)}{N}\mathbf 1, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf w) = \frac{v\,\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf u}{\tau_w}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\mathbf 1\cdot\mathbf 1}{N}\Bigr) = 0 .$$
It is highly competitive: weights are reduced by the same amount, so small weights shrink proportionally more and are driven to zero.

The **BCM rule** (Bienenstock, Cooper, Munro, 1982) uses a sliding threshold, $\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = v\mathbf u(v - \theta_v)$, with $\tau_\theta\dot\theta_v = v^2 - \theta_v$. If a synapse gets strong, the threshold rises and makes further potentiation harder: stability through homeostasis.

**Oja's rule** (1982) adds a multiplicative decay:
$$\tau_w\frac{d\mathbf w}{dt} = v\,\mathbf u - \alpha v^2\,\mathbf w, \qquad \alpha > 0 .$$

::: {.theorem name="Oja's rule extracts the first principal component"}
For zero-mean inputs with covariance $C$, the averaged Oja dynamics $\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w - \alpha(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w$ converges to $\mathbf w^* = \pm\mathbf e_1/\sqrt\alpha$, where $\mathbf e_1$ is the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue.
:::

*Proof.* (i) Norm: $\tau_w\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf w|^2 = 2(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)(1 - \alpha|\mathbf w|^2)$, so $|\mathbf w|^2 \to 1/\alpha$. (ii) Fixed points satisfy $C\mathbf w = \lambda\mathbf w$ with $\lambda = \alpha\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w$: eigenvectors of $C$, normalised to $1/\sqrt\alpha$. (iii) Linearising around $\mathbf e_\mu/\sqrt\alpha$, a perturbation along $\mathbf e_\nu$ grows at rate $\lambda_\nu - \lambda_\mu$. Only $\mu = 1$, the largest eigenvalue, is stable. $\square$

The neuron's output $v = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u$ becomes the projection of the input onto its direction of largest variance. A single Hebbian neuron performs **principal component analysis**, an unsupervised compression of its input. Networks of such neurons with lateral inhibition (Sanger's rule) extract several components.

## Associative memory

Recurrent networks can hold short-term memories (the integrator of Chapter 10). Long-term **associative memory** stores patterns so that a partial or noisy cue recalls the complete pattern, as Pavlov's dog associates the bell with food. The idea is to make the stored patterns **fixed points** of the network dynamics, each with a **basin of attraction**: all initial conditions sufficiently similar to a memory flow toward it. Learning is unsupervised. Given patterns $\mathbf v_m$, $m = 1, \dots, N_{\text{mem}}$, choose weights such that $\mathbf v_m = \psi(M\mathbf v_m)$.

For rate networks with sparse binary patterns, $v_m(i) \in \{0, c\}$ with a fraction $\alpha$ of active units, a weight matrix that works is the Hebbian **covariance** construction
$$M = \frac{\lambda}{c^2\alpha N(1 - \alpha)}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{mem}}}(\mathbf v_n - \alpha c\mathbf 1)(\mathbf v_n - \alpha c\mathbf 1)^\top - \frac{\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top}{\alpha N},$$
whose first term makes each pattern an approximate eigenvector, using that uncorrelated patterns overlap only by $\mathbf v_n\cdot\mathbf v_m \simeq N\alpha^2c^2$. The second term is a global inhibition that keeps inactive units off. With a threshold-like $\psi$ satisfying $\psi(-c(1 + \alpha\lambda)) = 0$ and $\psi(c(\lambda - 1 - \alpha\lambda)) = c$, each pattern is a fixed point. The construction is symmetric, which guarantees convergence to fixed points (a Lyapunov function exists). As more patterns are stored, the crosstalk between them grows and the memories degrade: capacity is finite.

## The Hopfield network

### Definition and energy

Hopfield (1982) considered $N$ binary units $\sigma_i = \pm1$ with symmetric couplings $J_{ij} = J_{ji}$, $J_{ii} = 0$, and thresholds $h_i$. Asynchronous dynamics picks a unit at random and aligns it with its local field:
$$\sigma_i \leftarrow \operatorname{sign}\Bigl(\sum_jJ_{ij}\sigma_j - h_i\Bigr).$$
The dynamics never increases the **energy**
$$\mathcal E(\boldsymbol\sigma) = -\frac12\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j + \sum_ih_i\sigma_i .$$
Flipping $\sigma_i$ changes the energy by $\Delta\mathcal E = 2\sigma_i\bigl(\sum_jJ_{ij}\sigma_j - h_i\bigr)$, and the update flips $\sigma_i$ only when this is negative. Since $\mathcal E$ is bounded below and decreases at each effective flip, the dynamics reaches a local minimum in finite time: a Metropolis algorithm at zero temperature. Symmetry of $J$ is essential. With asymmetric couplings no energy exists and the dynamics can cycle.

### The Hebb (Hopfield) rule

To store $p$ patterns $\boldsymbol\xi^\mu \in \{\pm1\}^N$, set ($h_i = 0$)
$$J_{ij} = \frac1N\sum_{\mu=1}^p\xi^\mu_i\xi^\mu_j, \qquad i \neq j .$$
Units that agree in a pattern get an excitatory coupling, units that disagree an inhibitory one. The energy becomes
$$\mathcal E = -\frac N2\sum_\mu m_\mu^2 + \frac p2, \qquad m_\mu = \frac1N\sum_i\xi^\mu_i\sigma_i,$$
where $m_\mu$ is the **overlap** with pattern $\mu$. Each stored pattern ($m_\mu = 1$) is therefore a low-energy configuration. The local field on unit $i$ in state $\boldsymbol\xi^\nu$ is
$$h_i = \sum_jJ_{ij}\xi^\nu_j = \xi^\nu_i + \frac1N\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}\sum_{j\neq i}\xi^\mu_i\xi^\mu_j\xi^\nu_j .$$
The first term is the **signal**, which stabilises the pattern. The second is **crosstalk** from the other patterns: a sum of about $Np$ random $\pm1$ terms divided by $N$, Gaussian with variance $p/N$. A unit is unstable when the crosstalk exceeds $1$ with the wrong sign, with probability $P_{\text{err}} \simeq \frac12\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{N/2p}\bigr)$. For small $p/N$ errors are rare. As $p/N$ grows, errors cascade.

### Capacity and spurious states

The statistical mechanics of the model (Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky, 1985) treats it as a spin glass at temperature $T$. Random patterns can be retrieved, with overlaps close to $1$, as long as the **load** satisfies
$$\alpha = \frac pN < \alpha_c \simeq 0.138 .$$
At $\alpha_c$ the retrieval states disappear discontinuously and the network falls into a spin-glass phase with no relation to the stored memories (catastrophic forgetting). Besides the stored patterns and their inverses $-\boldsymbol\xi^\mu$ (the energy is even), there are **spurious** minima. The best known are odd mixtures $\operatorname{sign}(\pm\boldsymbol\xi^{\mu_1} \pm \boldsymbol\xi^{\mu_2} \pm \boldsymbol\xi^{\mu_3})$, which have overlap $1/2$ with each of three patterns. At large loads there are also spin-glass states. A finite temperature (stochastic updates, Chapter 12) destabilises the shallow spurious minima before the stored ones, which helps retrieval.

### Variants

- **Sparse patterns** with activity $a \ll 1$ and covariance couplings $J_{ij} \propto \sum_\mu(\xi^\mu_i - a)(\xi^\mu_j - a)$ have a much larger capacity, $\alpha_c \sim 1/(a|\ln a|)$.
- **Diluted** networks, where each unit connects to only a fraction of others (like cortex), keep a capacity proportional to the number of connections per unit.
- **Robustness**: deleting a random fraction of the couplings degrades retrieval gracefully, a property shared by biological memory.

The Hopfield model also appears in biology beyond neuroscience. Gene-expression patterns of cell types behave like stored attractors of regulatory networks (Chapters 16–17).

## Supervised learning: reservoir computing

A different use of recurrent networks keeps the recurrent weights random and fixed and trains only a linear readout. The **reservoir** has state $\mathbf x$ obeying
$$\tau_x\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = -\mathbf x + W_{\text{in}}I(t) + W_{\text{rec}}\,\psi(\mathbf x), \qquad y(t) = \mathbf W_{\text{out}}\cdot\psi(\mathbf x(t)).$$
With $W_{\text{rec}}$ random and scaled near the edge of chaos (spectral radius $\lesssim 1$), the reservoir maps the input history into a rich, high-dimensional nonlinear representation. Training reduces to linear regression. With $\mathcal U$ the matrix whose columns are the reservoir states $\mathbf u_t = \psi(\mathbf x_t)$ and $\mathbf v$ the target outputs, minimising the mean squared error gives
$$\mathbf W_{\text{out}} = (\mathcal U\mathcal U^\top + \beta\mathbb 1)^{-1}\mathcal U\mathbf v,$$
with a ridge parameter $\beta$ against overfitting. Online alternatives are stochastic gradient descent and the FORCE algorithm, which also trains feedback weights (Sussillo and Abbott, 2009). Echo-state networks predict chaotic time series and generate complex motor patterns (Jaeger and Haas, 2004).

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(Oja's rule, class exercise Q1)** Train a single linear neuron with Oja's rule ($\eta = 0.001$, $\alpha = 0.1$, $w(0)$ of norm $5$ in a random direction) on $p = 10^4$ points drawn from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbb 1)$ in two dimensions. Repeat from several initial conditions. What do the weights converge to, and why?
:::

::: {.solution}
The norm always converges to $1/\sqrt\alpha = 3.16$ (simulations: $3.1630$ in three runs), but the direction is different in each run ($(-2.61, 1.78)$, $(-0.88, 3.04)$, $(1.78, -2.61)$). For isotropic data $C = \mathbb 1$, every direction is an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue: the first principal component is not defined. The rule fixes the norm, while the direction diffuses neutrally, driven only by sampling noise.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Oja's rule, Q2)** Repeat with $p = 1000$ points of covariance $\operatorname{diag}(10, 1)$, mean $(10, 10)$, rotated by $30°$. Do the weights still find the first principal component?
:::

::: {.solution}
No. Without centring the data the rule converges to the leading eigenvector of the *second-moment* matrix $\langle\mathbf u\mathbf u^\top\rangle = C + \boldsymbol\mu\boldsymbol\mu^\top$, which is dominated by the mean. The simulated weight direction is $(0.27, 0.96)$, essentially the mean direction $(0.26, 0.97)$, while the true first principal component is $(0.88, 0.48)$. Oja's rule performs PCA only on zero-mean inputs. Biologically, inputs must be centred first, e.g. by adaptation, which subtracts the mean rate.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Oja's rule, Q3)** Compare Oja's rule with $\Delta\mathbf w = \eta\bigl[C\mathbf w - \alpha\mathbf w(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w)\bigr]$ on centred data.
:::

::: {.solution}
At a fixed point of the new rule, $C\mathbf w = \alpha|\mathbf w|^2\mathbf w$, so $\mathbf w$ is again an eigenvector, and the same stability argument selects $\mathbf e_1$. The norm is different: $|\mathbf w|^2 = \lambda_1/\alpha$ instead of $1/\alpha$. With covariance $\operatorname{diag}(10, 1)$ rotated by $30°$ ($\lambda_1 \simeq 10.35$ in the sample), Oja's rule gives $|\mathbf w| = 3.16$ while the new rule gives $|\mathbf w| \simeq \sqrt{\lambda_1/\alpha} = 10.2$ (about $9.6$ after five epochs of online learning, still converging). Both point along $(0.87, 0.50)$. The new rule encodes the *variance* of the leading component in the weight norm, whereas Oja's rule normalises it away.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Instability of Hebb's rule)** For $\tau_w\dot{\mathbf w} = Q\mathbf w$ with $Q = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$, solve for $\mathbf w(t)$ from $\mathbf w(0) = (1, 0)$. Show that the direction converges while the norm diverges, and add subtractive normalisation to see what changes.
:::

::: {.solution}
Eigenvalues $3$ (along $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$) and $1$ (along $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$). $\mathbf w(t) = \frac12e^{3t/\tau_w}(1, 1) + \frac12e^{t/\tau_w}(1, -1)$: the direction tends to $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ but $|\mathbf w|$ grows as $e^{3t/\tau_w}$. With subtractive normalisation the projection on $\mathbf 1$ is frozen at $w_1 + w_2 = 1$, and only the component along $(1, -1)$ evolves, at rate $\lambda_2 = 1$: $\mathbf w(t) = \frac12(1, 1) + \frac12e^{t/\tau_w}(1, -1)$. This grows until the bounds are hit, ending at $(1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$: one synapse wins and the other is eliminated. Normalisation turns Hebbian growth into competition.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Hopfield capacity, class exercise 1)** Store $p$ random patterns in a network of $N = 400$ units and test retrieval from a $5\%$-corrupted version of each pattern using asynchronous zero-temperature updates. Plot the fraction of successful retrievals (final overlap $> 0.9$) against $\alpha = p/N$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Simulation (up to $20$ patterns tested per load): $\alpha = 0.05$: $100\%$; $0.10$: $100\%$; $0.12$: $90\%$; $0.14$: $95\%$; $0.16$: $65\%$; $0.20$: $30\%$. Retrieval is essentially perfect below $\alpha \approx 0.12$ and collapses between $0.14$ and $0.2$, consistent with the theoretical $\alpha_c \simeq 0.138$; finite-size effects smooth the transition. With the signal-to-noise estimate, the single-step error probability at $\alpha = 0.14$ is $\frac12\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{1/0.28}) \approx 0.4\%$. Errors then cascade, which is why the actual threshold is lower than a naive estimate would suggest.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(HW 5) Playing with the Hopfield code.** Use the course code `hopfield.py`: load images, convert them to $\pm1$ patterns, store them with the Hebb rule, and retrieve them from corrupted cues. Summarise your observations in half a page.
:::

::: {.solution}
A typical report:

- **Storage and retrieval.** With a few ($2$–$4$) images of $N \sim 10^3$–$10^4$ pixels, corrupting $20$–$30\%$ of a pattern and running the dynamics recovers it exactly within a few sweeps, while the energy decreases monotonically.
- **Inverse states.** Very corrupted cues ($> 50\%$ flipped) converge to the *inverted* image, the other minimum of the even energy.
- **Correlated patterns.** Natural images are correlated (large uniform regions, similar backgrounds), so their crosstalk is much larger than for random patterns. Storing several similar images produces mixtures: the capacity for correlated patterns is far below $0.138N$. Centring the patterns (subtracting the mean activity), as in the covariance rule, improves it.
- **Mixtures.** Starting from a superposition of three patterns often ends in the spurious mixture state.
- **Temperature.** Running at small $T$ (stochastic updates) helps escape spurious states. Too much noise destroys the memories.
- **Damage.** Setting a fraction of weights to zero leaves retrieval nearly intact until a large fraction is removed.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Spurious mixture states)** For three patterns, show that $\boldsymbol\sigma = \operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol\xi^1 + \boldsymbol\xi^2 + \boldsymbol\xi^3)$ has overlap $1/2$ with each, and compute its energy relative to a pure pattern for large $N$ and $p = 3$.
:::

::: {.solution}
At each site, the sign of the sum agrees with $\xi^1_i$ unless both $\xi^2_i$ and $\xi^3_i$ disagree with it, which has probability $1/4$. So $m_1 = \frac34 - \frac14 = \frac12$, and likewise for $m_2$, $m_3$. The energy is $\mathcal E = -\frac N2\sum_\mu m_\mu^2 = -\frac N2\cdot\frac34 = -0.375N$, against $-\frac N2(1 + O(1/N))$ for a pure pattern (whose overlaps with the other two are $O(N^{-1/2})$). The mixture is a local minimum, but shallower than the stored memories, so a small temperature removes it first.
:::
