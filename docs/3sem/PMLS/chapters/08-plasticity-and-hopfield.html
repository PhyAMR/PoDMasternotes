9  Plasticity, associative memory and Hopfield networks

The synapses \(W_{ij}\) of Chapter 7 were fixed. In real brains they change — that is plasticity — and the pattern of changes lets the network store and retrieve information. This chapter mirrors Prof. Suweis’s Chapter 8: Hebbian plasticity and its subtractively-normalised variant, associative-memory networks, and the Hopfield model with its statistical-mechanical analysis. The extension to stochastic recurrent networks (Boltzmann machines) is treated separately in Chapter 9.

9.1 Hebbian plasticity

The essence of Hebb’s rule is captured in the slogan “neurons that fire together, wire together”. Formally:

Let \(\phi_i, \phi_j\) be the firing rates of two neurons and \(W_{ij}\) the weight of the synapse from \(j\) to \(i\). The Hebbian rule is \[\tau_W\,\dot W_{ij} = \eta\,\phi_i\,\phi_j ,\] with learning rate \(\eta > 0\). Under repeated correlated activity of pre- and post-synaptic neurons, \(W_{ij}\) grows without bound.

Remark. The unbounded growth is unphysical: real synaptic weights are bounded. Two standard fixes are (i) subtractive normalisation — subtract the mean activity from each factor; (ii) multiplicative normalisation (Oja’s rule) — subtract a term proportional to \(\phi_i^2 W_{ij}\) that stabilises the norm.

Under the subtractively normalised Hebbian rule with a linear rate unit driven by inputs \(\mathbf x(t)\), the synaptic weight vector converges to the leading eigenvector of the input covariance \(\Sigma = \langle \mathbf x \mathbf x^\top\rangle\). Hebbian plasticity therefore performs principal component analysis.

9.2 Associative memory networks

Suppose we want the network to remember a set of patterns \(\{\boldsymbol\xi^\mu\}_{\mu=1}^{P}\), each a vector of \(N\) binary units \(\xi_i^\mu \in \{-1, +1\}\). The Hebbian prescription is:

Given patterns \(\boldsymbol\xi^\mu\), the Hebbian synaptic matrix is \[W_{ij} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\xi_i^\mu\,\xi_j^\mu, \qquad W_{ii} = 0 .\] The network dynamics is \(s_i(t+1) = \text{sgn}\bigl(\sum_j W_{ij}\,s_j(t)\bigr)\). A pattern \(\boldsymbol\xi^\mu\) is stored if it is a fixed point of these dynamics.

This construction is the Hopfield network. Its analysis, unlike the recurrent nets of Chapter 5, is statistical-mechanical: the dynamics has a Lyapunov function, so a temperature can be introduced by hand and the equilibrium ensemble studied.

9.3 Hopfield energy landscape

For the Hopfield model with symmetric weights \(W_{ij} = W_{ji}\) and zero diagonal, the quantity \[H[\mathbf s] = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij} W_{ij}\,s_i\,s_j\] is monotonically non-increasing under synchronous or asynchronous sign updates. Fixed points are local minima of \(H\).

The stored patterns \(\boldsymbol\xi^\mu\) are, by construction, minima of \(H\). But the Hebbian rule also creates spurious minima — combinations like \(\text{sgn}(\xi^{\mu_1} + \xi^{\mu_2} + \xi^{\mu_3})\) that are also local minima of \(H\), but do not correspond to any single stored pattern.

The maximum number of patterns that can be stored and retrieved reliably in a Hopfield network of \(N\) binary units is \[P_{\max} \approx 0.138\,N .\] Beyond this critical loading \(\alpha_c = P/N \approx 0.138\), retrieval fails: a spin-glass phase takes over in which memory patterns cease to be attractors.

The threshold \(\alpha_c\) is not a hand-wavy back-of-the-envelope result; it comes from the replica-symmetric solution of the Hopfield model treated as a spin glass.

9.4 Statistical mechanics of the Hopfield model

At temperature \(T = 1/\beta\) the Hopfield model is a disordered Ising system with couplings \(W_{ij}\) built from random patterns. The partition function is \[Z(\beta, \{\boldsymbol\xi\}) = \sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta H[\mathbf s]}.\] Averaging \(\ln Z\) over the quenched randomness of the patterns (as in Chapter 4’s replica trick) gives the free energy in terms of two order parameters: the overlaps \(m^\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \xi_i^\mu s_i\) with each stored pattern, and the Edwards–Anderson order parameter \(q = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \langle s_i\rangle^2\) measuring frozen local magnetisation.

The \((T, \alpha)\) phase diagram of the Hopfield model has three phases:

  1. Retrieval phase at low \(T\) and \(\alpha < \alpha_c(T)\): one \(m^\mu\) is \(O(1)\), memories are attractors.
  2. Spin-glass phase at low \(T\) and \(\alpha > \alpha_c(T)\): all \(m^\mu = 0\) but \(q > 0\); memories are lost.
  3. Paramagnetic phase at high \(T\): \(q = 0\); no correlations survive.

At \(T = 0\) the critical loading is \(\alpha_c = 0.138\).

Fixing \(\alpha < 0.138\) and cooling from high \(T\), the Hopfield network undergoes a second-order transition from paramagnet to retrieval phase at \(T_c(\alpha)\). Below \(T_c\) the network settles to a state with macroscopic overlap with a randomly chosen stored pattern. This is the physical mechanism of associative memory.

9.5 Supervised learning with recurrent networks (reservoir computing)

An alternative to storing static patterns is to use the transient dynamics of a randomly connected recurrent network to represent inputs. This is the idea of reservoir computing (also known as an echo-state network or liquid-state machine):

Fix a large, random, sub-critical recurrent network with weights \(W\) (with \(\sigma\sqrt{N} < 1\) so activity decays). Drive it with a time-varying input \(\mathbf u(t)\). Train only a linear readout \(\mathbf y = C\,\boldsymbol\phi(t)\) to match a target signal \(\mathbf y^*(t)\).

Because only the readout is trained, the optimisation is convex (least squares) and does not suffer from vanishing gradients. Reservoirs poised near the edge of chaos (\(\sigma\sqrt N \approx 1\)) have the highest computational capacity — an application of the criticality theme from Chapter 4.

9.6 Toward stochastic recurrent networks

The Hopfield model is deterministic: given the initial state, the trajectory is uniquely determined by the sign updates. Making it stochastic — the update is drawn from a Boltzmann distribution over the two options — gives the Boltzmann machine and connects the associative-memory picture to modern energy-based generative models. This extension is the subject of Chapter 9.

9.7 Exercises

Verify that Oja’s rule \(\dot W = \eta(\phi \mathbf x - \phi^2 W)\) (with \(\phi = W^\top \mathbf x\)) drives \(W\) to unit norm and to the principal eigenvector of the input covariance.

Solution. Compute \(\dot{|W|^2} = 2 W^\top \dot W = 2\eta(\phi W^\top \mathbf x - \phi^2 |W|^2) = 2\eta\phi^2(1 - |W|^2)\): the norm relaxes to one. On the sphere, the fixed points satisfy \(\Sigma W = \lambda W\) with \(\lambda = W^\top \Sigma W\). Stability analysis shows that only the top eigenvector is stable.

Show that the Hopfield capacity \(\alpha_c \approx 0.138\) can be derived heuristically by demanding that the noise contribution to the local field on a pattern be smaller than the signal. Give the order-of-magnitude argument.

Solution. The local field on pattern \(\mu\) at site \(i\) is \(h_i^\mu = \xi_i^\mu + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}\sum_j \xi_i^\nu \xi_j^\nu \xi_j^\mu\). The signal is \(\xi_i^\mu = \pm 1\) (variance 1); the noise is a sum of \(P - 1\) terms each of variance \(1/N\), so its variance is \((P-1)/N \sim \alpha\). Retrieval requires signal-to-noise \(> 1\), giving \(\alpha \lesssim 1\). The precise number \(0.138\) comes from a more careful replica calculation that accounts for correlations among the noise terms.

9.8 Callouts

Hebbian plasticity is one of very few learning rules that is both biologically plausible and mathematically well-behaved. Most modern deep-learning algorithms (backprop) are neither.

When implementing a Hopfield network from scratch, always plot the energy \(H[\mathbf s(t)]\) during dynamics: monotone decrease is a necessary correctness check.

The Hopfield capacity \(\alpha_c \approx 0.138\) is the number of uncorrelated patterns that can be stored. Correlated patterns (e.g. real images) have a much lower effective capacity.

Real synapses are sign-constrained (Dale’s law) and typically bounded above by a saturation weight. The mathematical Hopfield model ignores both. Introducing these constraints reduces capacity and can change the phase diagram — see Brunel & Nadal (1998).

9.9 Book supplements

The statistical-mechanical analysis of Hopfield networks is treated in canonical detail in Hertz, Krogh & Palmer, Introduction to the Theory of Neural Computation, Westview (1991), Chapters 2 and 10. For a modern perspective on associative memory and its capacity, Krotov & Hopfield, Dense associative memory is robust to adversarial inputs, Neural Comput. 30, 3151 (2018).
