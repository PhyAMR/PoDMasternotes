5  Species interactions and random matrices

Neutral theory (Chapter 3) tells us how much of an ecological pattern can be explained by demographic noise alone. To move beyond it we now add the biological content that neutral theory deliberately ignores: species interact. This chapter builds up from the classical Lotka–Volterra system, generalises it to arbitrarily many species, and shows how the celebrated May stability paradox leads directly to random matrix theory.

5.1 The Lotka–Volterra equations

For two species — predator \(y\) and prey \(x\) — the deterministic Lotka–Volterra system is \[\dot x = \alpha x - \beta x y, \qquad \dot y = \delta x y - \gamma y ,\] with all four rate constants positive. Trajectories are closed orbits around the neutral fixed point \((x^*, y^*) = (\gamma/\delta, \alpha/\beta)\).

For \(S\) species with abundances \(\mathbf x = (x_1, \ldots, x_S)\) the generalised Lotka–Volterra equations are \[\dot x_i = x_i\left(r_i + \sum_{j=1}^{S} \alpha_{ij}\,x_j\right), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, S,\] where \(r_i\) is the intrinsic growth rate of species \(i\) and \(\alpha_{ij}\) is the per-capita effect of species \(j\) on species \(i\). Diagonal entries \(\alpha_{ii} < 0\) encode intra-specific competition.

The interior fixed point \(\mathbf x^*\) of GLV satisfies \(\mathbf r + A\mathbf x^* = 0\), i.e. \(\mathbf x^* = -A^{-1}\mathbf r\). Linearising around \(\mathbf x^*\) gives the Jacobian \(J = \text{diag}(\mathbf x^*)\,A\), so stability of \(\mathbf x^*\) is governed by the eigenvalues of a weighted version of the interaction matrix.

Remark. GLV is the workhorse model of community ecology because it reproduces the three qualitatively distinct dynamical regimes (fixed point, limit cycle, chaos) with a single interaction matrix. It also emerges as the quasi-steady-state limit of many consumer–resource models.

5.2 Stochastic Volterra model

Adding demographic noise to Lotka–Volterra gives the stochastic model \[dx_i = x_i\Bigl(r_i + \sum_j \alpha_{ij} x_j\Bigr) dt + \sqrt{2 D_i x_i}\,dW_i(t),\] whose power spectrum shows stochastic oscillations even in parameter regimes where the deterministic model has a stable fixed point.

Around a stable interior fixed point \(\mathbf x^*\), linearised fluctuations \(\delta \mathbf x(t) = \mathbf x(t) - \mathbf x^*\) satisfy \[d\delta\mathbf x = J\,\delta\mathbf x\,dt + \Sigma^{1/2}\,d\mathbf W(t) ,\] with \(J = \text{diag}(\mathbf x^*)A\). The Fourier power spectrum of \(\delta x_i\) is \[S_{ii}(\omega) = \sum_{jk}\bigl[(J - i\omega I)^{-1}\bigr]_{ij}\bigl[(J + i\omega I)^{-1}\bigr]_{ik} \Sigma_{jk} ,\] which typically has a peak at a non-zero \(\omega\) — the stochastic amplification of a mode that would decay deterministically.

5.3 Random interactions and the May paradox

If \(S \gg 1\) the biologist is confronted with \(S^2\) interaction coefficients, which cannot possibly be estimated one by one. Robert May’s insight (1972) was to sidestep the details and ask: what does a “typical” interaction matrix imply for stability?

Consider a community of \(S\) species with random pairwise interactions: \[A_{ij} = -\delta_{ij} + \sigma\,c\,g_{ij}, \qquad g_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\text{ i.i.d.\ off-diagonal},\] where \(c \in [0, 1]\) is the connectance (fraction of non-zero entries) and \(\sigma\) the interaction strength. The diagonal encodes self-regulation.

The community defined above is stable with high probability in the limit \(S \to \infty\) if and only if \[\sigma\sqrt{cS} < 1 .\] Equivalently, if the “complexity” \(\sigma\sqrt{cS}\) exceeds one, the interior fixed point loses stability and the community can undergo cycles or extinctions.

The theorem is proved by observing that the eigenvalues of \(A\) concentrate on a disc in the complex plane of radius \(\sigma\sqrt{cS}\) centred at \(-1\) (the circular law), so the rightmost eigenvalue crosses zero exactly at the threshold above.

Among the three parameters \((S, c, \sigma)\), stability is preserved by decreasing interaction strength or connectance, but not by adding more species. Real ecosystems appear to escape this trade-off by having structured, non-random interaction networks.

5.4 Proof idea: the circular law

The key tool behind May’s result is the circular law of random matrix theory:

Let \(M\) be an \(S \times S\) matrix with entries i.i.d. with zero mean and variance \(1/S\). In the limit \(S \to \infty\), the empirical spectral distribution of \(M\) converges (weakly, almost surely) to the uniform distribution on the unit disc in \(\mathbb C\).

Applied to the shifted matrix \(A + I\), the circular law gives eigenvalues uniformly on the disc of radius \(\sigma\sqrt{cS}\), whose real part is bounded above by that radius.

For a Gaussian \(M\) the spectral density can be derived explicitly using a Coulomb-gas / replica calculation. The joint density of eigenvalues in the complex plane is \[P(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_S) \propto \prod_{i<j} |\lambda_i - \lambda_j|^2\,\prod_k e^{-S|\lambda_k|^2} .\] Interpreted as a 2D Coulomb gas at temperature \(1/S\), the distribution flattens into the uniform disc as \(S \to \infty\).

The replica trick evaluates \(\langle \ln \det(zI - M)\rangle\) by writing \(\ln x = \lim_{n\to 0}(x^n - 1)/n\), promoting the average of the \(n\)-th power of the determinant to a Gaussian integral, and solving the saddle-point at large \(S\). The trick was invented for spin glasses (Sherrington–Kirkpatrick) but reappears everywhere random matrices show up — including in the neural chapters below.

5.5 Structured interactions

Real ecosystems are neither i.i.d. nor mean-field. Two forms of structure matter most:

Let the off-diagonal entries of \(A\) be jointly Gaussian with variance \(\sigma^2\) and correlation \(\rho = \text{corr}(A_{ij}, A_{ji})\). Then the spectrum lies on an ellipse of semi-axes \(\sigma\sqrt{cS}(1 + \rho)\) and \(\sigma\sqrt{cS}(1 - \rho)\). Predator–prey communities (\(\rho < 0\)) tend to be more stable than mutualistic ones (\(\rho > 0\)) for the same complexity.

Empirical food webs show strongly negative \(\rho\): whenever species \(j\) affects \(i\), the reverse effect \(A_{ji}\) has the opposite sign. This is one of the main reasons real ecosystems can be much larger than May’s threshold suggests.

5.6 Mutualism, competition, and food webs

Beyond pairwise correlations, network structure — who interacts with whom — is decisive.

Empirical plant–pollinator networks are highly nested: specialists interact with a subset of the partners of generalists. Nested networks concentrate spectral mass and give a lower principal eigenvalue than random networks of the same \(S\) and \(c\), so they can be more stable than uncorrelated matrices. Bastolla et al. (2009) argue this explains why mutualistic communities persist at complexities where May’s threshold would predict collapse.

Remark (Adaptive stability). Species evolve. If the interaction matrix \(A\) is itself a slow variable driven by selection for stability, the community can self-organise into a stable configuration even if a random \(A\) of the same statistics would not be. This is the adaptive or evolutionary form of the complexity–stability question.

5.7 Competitive exclusion

Finally, the classic result of Gause: two species competing for the same limiting resource cannot coexist at equilibrium. In GLV form, if two species \(i, j\) have identical growth requirements (\(r_i / \alpha_{ii} = r_j/\alpha_{jj}\) and identical impact on the resource), the equilibrium is a one-parameter family, and any small perturbation drives one to extinction. The generalisation to \(S\) species and \(R\) resources gives:

In a purely resource-mediated community, the number of coexisting species at equilibrium cannot exceed the number of independent limiting resources: \(S_{coex} \le R\).

Nature routinely exceeds this bound (the plankton paradox); the tools of the previous section — noise, non-equilibrium dynamics, network structure — are how the paradox is resolved.

5.8 Exercises

Show that the two-species Lotka–Volterra system is Hamiltonian: find conserved quantities \(H(x, y)\) such that \(\dot H = 0\). Sketch the phase portrait.

Solution. Multiply \(\dot x/x = \alpha - \beta y\) by \(\delta\) and \(\dot y/y = -\gamma + \delta x\) by \(\beta\) and add: \[\delta \dot x /x + \beta \dot y /y = \delta \alpha - \gamma \beta,\] so \(H(x, y) = \delta x - \gamma \ln x + \beta y - \alpha \ln y\) is conserved along trajectories. Level sets are closed curves around the fixed point \((x^*, y^*) = (\gamma/\delta, \alpha/\beta)\).

Verify May’s threshold numerically: for \(S = 100\), \(c = 0.5\), sweep \(\sigma\) from \(0.05\) to \(0.3\) and estimate the fraction of \(10^3\) random matrices whose principal eigenvalue crosses zero. Compare with the analytical threshold.

Solution. The theoretical threshold is \(\sigma^* = 1/\sqrt{cS} \approx 0.141\). A numerical experiment sampling \(\sigma\,g_{ij}\) with \(g_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) shows a sharp transition of the “stable” fraction from \(\approx 1\) to \(\approx 0\) within a window \(\sigma^* \pm 0.02\), in agreement with the finite-\(S\) correction predicted by the Tracy–Widom distribution.

5.9 Callouts

May’s paradox is not a proof that ecosystems collapse; it is a proof that random ecosystems collapse. The next generation of ecological modelling is about which network structures escape it.

When simulating GLV, keep the diagonal of \(A\) strictly negative and of order \(-1\): this fixes the noise scale so that different \((S, c, \sigma)\) triples are directly comparable.

“Complexity” in ecology has three technical meanings that are often conflated: species richness \(S\), connectance \(c\), and interaction strength \(\sigma\). May’s result is about their product \(\sigma\sqrt{cS}\), not about any of them alone.

The circular law relies on i.i.d. entries. Any structured pattern (block-diagonal, nested, phylogenetically constrained) breaks the assumption and changes the effective threshold. Don’t apply May’s formula to real food-web data without accounting for structure.
