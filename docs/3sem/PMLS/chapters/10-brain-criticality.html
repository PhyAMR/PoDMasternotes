11  Structure–function and brain criticality

The final chapter of the neural half of the course puts together three threads: the structural connectome (which neurons connect to which), the functional dynamics on top of it (Chapter 5), and the hypothesis — increasingly supported by data — that healthy brain activity operates near a critical point. This is where the ecological criticality of Chapter 3 and the neural criticality of Chapter 5 meet.

11.1 The brain criticality hypothesis

The observation is qualitative but robust: neural avalanches in vitro, LFP power spectra in vivo, fMRI resting-state fluctuations, and behavioural reaction-time distributions all show approximate power-law statistics, consistent with a system tuned close to a phase transition.

A neural avalanche is a cluster of firing events separated from other clusters by silent intervals longer than a chosen threshold. Its size \(S\) is the number of events in the cluster; its duration \(T\) is the interval spanned.

In cortical slice cultures (rat somatosensory cortex, Beggs & Plenz, 2003) the size and duration distributions of neural avalanches follow \[P(S)\sim S^{-3/2}, \qquad P(T)\sim T^{-2}, \qquad \langle S\rangle(T) \sim T^{2},\] with exponents that are consistent with the mean-field directed percolation universality class. Corresponding measurements in vivo (monkey and human cortex) reproduce the same exponents to within a factor of two.

Remark. The mean-field DP class is what one expects from a critical branching process: each firing event triggers on average one descendant. Sub-critical values give fewer descendants and shorter avalanches; super-critical values give runaway activity (a seizure). The healthy brain sits, on this hypothesis, exactly at the critical point.

11.2 The Haldeman–Beggs (HTC) model

To connect avalanche statistics to structural connectomes, Haldeman & Beggs proposed a minimal susceptible–active–refractory three-state model on a lattice (or graph). Each node \(i\) is at any time in state \(s_i \in \{S, A, R\}\); the update rules are:

Given the structural graph with adjacency \(W_{ij}\) and threshold \(\theta\), on each discrete time step:

  • If \(s_i = S\) and \(\sum_j W_{ij}\,\mathbf 1[s_j = A] \geq \theta\), then \(s_i \to A\) (activation).
  • If \(s_i = S\) with sub-threshold input, then \(s_i \to A\) with probability \(r_1\) (spontaneous activation).
  • If \(s_i = A\), then \(s_i \to R\) (deterministic firing).
  • If \(s_i = R\), then \(s_i \to S\) with probability \(r_2\) (recovery).

The parameters \((\theta, r_1, r_2)\) set the operating point. The order parameter is the fraction of active nodes, and phase transitions in the model can be located analytically in the continuous-time mean-field limit.

The HTC model has three phases: sub-critical (activity decays), critical (power-law avalanches), and super-critical (spontaneous bulk activity). The critical line is \(\theta = \theta_c(r_1, r_2)\), and at the transition the avalanche exponents match the empirical Beggs–Plenz values.

11.3 Connectomes from diffusion MRI and function from fMRI

Feeding the HTC model real structural connectomes rather than lattices is what makes the theory testable against neuroimaging data.

Using diffusion-tensor imaging (DTI) to reconstruct a \(\sim 100\)-region structural connectome and resting-state fMRI to record spontaneous activity time series, one can (i) simulate HTC dynamics on the DTI graph and (ii) compare the model functional connectivity \(C_{ij}^{model}\) (correlations of simulated node activities) with the empirical fMRI functional connectivity. The best fit occurs when the model operates at its critical point \(\theta = \theta_c\), with model–data correlations of \(r \approx 0.7\). Away from criticality the fit degrades sharply.

11.4 Continuous-time approximation

For analytical progress the HTC model is coarse-grained to a continuous-time density field \(\rho(\mathbf x, t)\) satisfying a reaction–diffusion equation: \[\partial_t \rho = D\nabla^2\rho + a\rho - b\rho^2 - c\rho^3 + \sqrt{2\rho}\,\eta(\mathbf x, t),\] where \(\eta\) is space-time white noise. This is the directed-percolation Langevin equation.

The mean-field HTC dynamics reduces to the Langevin equation \[d\rho = (a\rho - b\rho^2)\,dt + \sqrt{2\rho/N}\,dW ,\] whose Fokker–Planck equation admits an exact stationary solution \[P_{ss}(\rho) \propto \rho^{-1}\exp\bigl(-N\int^\rho \tfrac{a - b\rho'}{\rho'}d\rho'\bigr).\] Above criticality (\(a > 0\)) the mode of \(P_{ss}\) is at \(\rho^* = a/b\); below criticality the distribution is concentrated near zero. At exactly \(a = 0\) the drift is purely quadratic and the stationary distribution has a power-law form.

11.5 Thermodynamic limit

For an infinite-size HTC model the fluctuations are washed out and one has a clean second-order phase transition with mean-field exponents.

In the limit \(N \to \infty\) the HTC order parameter \(\rho^*\) satisfies:

  • Sub-critical (\(a < 0\)): \(\rho^* = 0\).
  • Critical (\(a = 0\)): \(\rho^* = 0\) but with anomalously slow relaxation and long correlations.
  • Super-critical (\(a > 0\)): \(\rho^* = a/b > 0\).

Linear stability of the equilibria is set by the sign of \(a - 2b\rho^*\); the sub-critical fixed point is stable for \(a < 0\), the super-critical for \(a > 0\).

At criticality the relaxation time of the order parameter diverges as \(\tau \sim (a - a_c)^{-1}\). The corresponding power spectrum of order-parameter fluctuations is a Lorentzian at frequency \(1/\tau\); superposing many degrees of freedom with a distribution of relaxation times gives the \(1/f\) noise observed in EEG.

11.6 Stability analysis of equilibria

Small perturbations \(\delta \rho\) around the mean-field equilibrium \(\rho^*\) satisfy \[\partial_t \delta\rho = D\nabla^2 \delta\rho + (a - 2b\rho^*)\delta\rho + \sqrt{2\rho^*/N}\,\eta ,\] which is a stochastic diffusion equation with mass \(|a - 2b\rho^*|\). The correlation length \(\xi = \sqrt{D/|a - 2b\rho^*|}\) diverges at \(\rho^* = a/(2b)\) — the critical point.

The power spectrum of \(\delta\rho\) at wavevector \(k = 0\) is \[S(\omega) = \frac{2\rho^*/N}{\omega^2 + (a - 2b\rho^*)^2},\] a Lorentzian whose width shrinks to zero at the critical point. Empirical EEG spectra show precisely this critical slowing on approach to seizures.

11.7 Why would evolution build a critical brain?

Two arguments are often given, both quantitative:

  • Dynamic range. A network responds to a range of input intensities that is maximised at criticality. Sub-critical networks saturate quickly; super-critical networks are unresponsive. Kinouchi & Copelli (2006) proved this analytically for a simple neural model.
  • Information transmission. The mutual information between input and output patterns of a random neural network is maximal at the edge of chaos. This is a rigorous statement in reservoir computing (Bertschinger & Natschläger, 2004) and matches the empirical observation that cortical activity operates near \(\sigma\sqrt N \approx 1\).

Remark (Cross-domain parallels). The mathematical statements — power-law avalanches with mean-field exponents, divergent correlation lengths, \(1/f\) spectra — are literally the same as those we met in the neutral theory of biodiversity and in the SCS random rate network. Universality in the technical sense of statistical mechanics is what allows insights to transfer from ecology to the brain.

11.8 Exercises

Derive the mean-field HTC equilibrium \(\rho^* = a/b\) from the coarse-grained Langevin equation. Show that the corresponding correlation length diverges as \(|a - a_c|^{-1/2}\).

Solution. Set the deterministic drift to zero: \(a\rho^* - b(\rho^*)^2 = 0\) gives \(\rho^* = 0\) or \(\rho^* = a/b\). Linearising around \(\rho^* = a/b\) and looking for solutions \(\delta\rho \sim e^{ikx - \omega t}\) gives dispersion \(\omega = Dk^2 + (a - 2b\rho^*) = Dk^2 - a\). The correlation length is \(\xi = \sqrt{D/a}\), which diverges as \(a \to 0^+\) with exponent \(\nu = 1/2\).

Show that the mean-field DP exponents \(\tau_S = 3/2\), \(\tau_T = 2\), \(\gamma = 2\) satisfy the scaling relation \(\tau_S = 1 + (\tau_T - 1)/\gamma\). Comment on the physical meaning of the relation.

Solution. Substitute: \(\tau_S = 1 + (2 - 1)/\gamma = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2\). The relation is equivalent to \(\langle S\rangle(T)\sim T^\gamma\), which for a critical branching process means that avalanches with duration \(T\) have a typical size that scales as a power. Consistency of the exponents is the operational meaning of “the system is at a critical point”; any single exponent could be reproduced by accident, but the relation between them requires criticality.

11.9 Callouts

Brain criticality is a hypothesis. It is well-supported for cortical avalanche statistics but not universally accepted for whole-brain fMRI. The reader should treat the numerical matches as suggestive rather than definitive.

When you fit a power law to neural data, always check that the range spans at least two decades and that the maximum-likelihood estimate is compared against an exponential null hypothesis using the Clauset–Shalizi–Newman test. A one-decade “power law” is not a power law.

The distinction between criticality and quasi-criticality (a system that is not fine-tuned but whose slow modes look critical) matters for the causal argument. Only genuinely critical systems have divergent susceptibilities.

Interpreting \(1/f\) EEG noise as evidence of brain criticality is contested. Many non-critical mechanisms (superposed Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes, multiplicative noise) also produce \(1/f\) spectra. A single spectral shape is not proof of criticality.
