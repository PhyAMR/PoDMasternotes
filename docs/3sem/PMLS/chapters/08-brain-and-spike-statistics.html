8  The brain as a complex system: measuring and describing neural activity

The second module applies the toolbox of Part I to the nervous system. The analogy is direct: individuals become neurons, abundance becomes activity, predator–prey pairs become excitatory–inhibitory pairs, and generalised Lotka–Volterra communities become recurrent neural networks. This chapter introduces the brain as a complex system and the techniques that measure it. It then describes neural activity statistically: firing rates, tuning curves, spike-train statistics and the Poisson null models against which collective phenomena such as neuronal avalanches must be tested.

8.1 The brain as a complex system

The human brain contains about \(10^{11}\) neurons, each receiving about \(10^4\) synapses, for a total of about \(10^{14}\)–\(10^{15}\) connections. It is organised on several scales: molecular (ion channels), cellular (neurons), circuit (microcircuits and cortical columns), mesoscopic (brain areas), and the whole brain. Its functions emerge from the interactions between these levels. No single neuron “understands” a face or remembers a phone number.

The modelling programme parallels ecology:

ecology neuroscience
one species, \(\dot x = rx(1 - x)\) one neuron, activity \(x(t)\)
predator–prey excitatory–inhibitory neurons
\(S\) species, GLV, \(\dot x_i = r_ix_i(1 - \sum_jM_{ij}x_j)\) \(N\) neurons, recurrent networks, \(\dot x_i = -\tau x_i + \sum_jM_{ij}\tanh(x_j)\)

Two emergent patterns of collective activity frame the module.

  • Neuronal avalanches (Beggs and Plenz, 2003). In cortical slices recorded with multi-electrode arrays, activity propagates in bursts separated by silence. Bin time in windows \(\Delta t\); an avalanche is a run of consecutive non-empty bins, and its size \(s\) is the total number of events. The size distribution follows a power law, \(P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}\), with a cut-off set by the number of electrodes, and durations follow \(P(T) \sim T^{-2}\). These are the exponents of a critical branching process, which suggests that the cortex operates near a critical point (Chapter 13).
  • Brain waves. Population activity oscillates in characteristic bands: delta (\(0.5\)–\(4\) Hz, deep sleep), theta (\(4\)–\(8\) Hz), alpha (\(8\)–\(13\) Hz, relaxed wakefulness), beta (\(13\)–\(32\) Hz, active thinking) and gamma (\(32\)–\(100\) Hz, attention and binding). Oscillations require the synchronisation of large populations and arise from excitatory–inhibitory loops (Chapter 10).

How complex should a model be? The answer depends on the question and on the quality of the data. Models range along a scale of resolution: linear models, mean-field rate models, interacting particles (binary neurons), neural-mass models, networks of neural masses, neural fields, and detailed simulations (e.g. the Blue Brain project). A recurring question is whether structure determines function: whether the anatomical network predicts the correlations of spontaneous (“resting-state”) activity.

8.2 Measuring brain activity

Techniques differ in what they measure and at which spatial and temporal resolution.

Non-invasive, whole brain:

  • Electroencephalography (EEG): electrodes on the scalp record the electric potential generated by synchronous activity of large populations. It has millisecond resolution but poor spatial resolution, and reconstructing sources from surface signals is an ill-posed inverse problem. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) is the magnetic analogue.
  • Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI): MRI measurements of the anisotropic diffusion of water reveal the white-matter fibre tracts. Tractography reconstructs the structural connectome: a weighted network between \(10^2\)–\(10^3\) brain regions (parcels).
  • Functional MRI (fMRI): the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal measures changes in blood flow and oxygenation that follow neural activity, with a resolution of a few millimetres and a few seconds. Pearson correlations between regional BOLD time series define functional connectivity networks, with large individual variability.

Invasive, few neurons:

  • Electrode probes record the extracellular potential near neurons (spikes and local field potentials) or the membrane potential from inside a cell. Modern silicon probes (Neuropixels) record from hundreds of neurons along a shank simultaneously.
  • Neuronal cultures grown on multi-electrode arrays: homogeneous or clustered networks whose spontaneous activity can be followed for weeks.
  • Calcium imaging: genetically encoded fluorescent indicators (GCaMP) light up when calcium enters the cell during spiking. Optical (two-photon) imaging then follows thousands of neurons in behaving animals.
  • Optogenetics: neurons engineered to express light-sensitive ion channels can be switched on and off with light, giving causal control of activity.

8.3 From stimulus to response

8.3.1 Firing rates

A neuron’s output is a sequence of action potentials (“spikes”) at times \(t_1, \dots, t_n\). Since their shape is stereotyped, a spike train is represented by the neural response function \[\rho(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n\delta(t - t_i), \qquad \sum_ih(t - t_i) = \int d\tau\,h(\tau)\rho(t - \tau).\] Spike trains vary from trial to trial even with identical stimuli. Useful rates are:

  • the spike-count rate \(r = n/T = \frac1T\int_0^T\rho(\tau)d\tau\);
  • the time-dependent firing rate \(r(t) = \frac1{\Delta t}\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\langle\rho(\tau)\rangle d\tau\), with \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) an average over trials. For small \(\Delta t\), \(r(t)\Delta t\) is the probability of a spike in \([t, t + \Delta t]\), and for any smooth \(h\), \(\int h(\tau)\langle\rho(t - \tau)\rangle d\tau = \int h(\tau)r(t - \tau)d\tau\).

From a single trial, \(r(t)\) is estimated by filtering the spike train with a window \(w\): \(r(t) \simeq \int w(\tau)\rho(t - \tau)d\tau\). Options are a rectangular window of width \(\Delta t\), a Gaussian \(w(\tau) \propto e^{-\tau^2/2\sigma_w^2}\), or the causal kernel \(w(\tau) = [\alpha^2\tau e^{-\alpha\tau}]_+\), which uses only past spikes. The choice of window sets a trade-off between temporal resolution and noise.

8.3.2 Tuning curves

The tuning curve \(f(s) = \langle r\rangle\) gives the mean firing rate as a function of a stimulus parameter \(s\). Examples are a Gaussian tuning to the orientation of a bar in primary visual cortex, \(f(s) = r_{\max}e^{-(s - s_{\max})^2/2\sigma^2}\); cosine tuning to arm-movement direction in motor cortex, \(f(s) = r_0 + (r_{\max} - r_0)\cos(s - s_{\max})\); and sigmoidal tuning to binocular disparity. Populations of differently tuned neurons encode stimuli collectively.

8.3.3 Psychophysical laws

Sensory systems respond over enormous ranges: the retina detects single photons and works in daylight with \(10^6\) times more. This is possible through adaptation: neurons signal changes relative to a background.

  • Weber’s law: the just-noticeable difference \(\Delta s\) is proportional to the stimulus, \(\Delta s/s = k\).
  • Fechner’s law: integrating Weber’s law, perceived intensity is logarithmic in the stimulus, \(P = k\ln(s/s_0)\).

Both follow from coding efficiently a stimulus whose fluctuations scale with its mean (a project topic; see Sims, 2018).

8.3.4 Spike-triggered average

Because of adaptation one studies fluctuations around a zero-mean stimulus. Assuming stationarity and ergodicity (trial averages equal time averages), the spike-triggered average stimulus is \[C(\tau) = \Bigl\langle\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ns(t_i - \tau)\Bigr\rangle \simeq \frac{1}{\langle n\rangle}\int_0^Tr(t)s(t - \tau)dt = \frac{Q_{rs}(-\tau)}{\langle r\rangle},\] where \(Q_{rs}(\tau) = \frac1T\int_0^Tr(t)s(t + \tau)dt\) is the rate–stimulus correlation. \(C(\tau)\) shows what, on average, the stimulus did just before a spike: it is the first step in building a linear model of the neuron’s response (reverse correlation).

8.4 Spike-train statistics

8.4.1 The homogeneous Poisson process

The simplest null model assumes spikes are independent events at a constant rate \(r\). Divide \([0, T]\) into \(M\) bins of width \(\Delta t = T/M\), small enough to hold at most one spike. The probability of \(n\) spikes is \[P_T(n) = \lim_{\Delta t\to0}\binom Mn(r\Delta t)^n(1 - r\Delta t)^{M - n} = \frac{(rT)^n}{n!}e^{-rT},\] using \((1 - r\Delta t)^{M} \to e^{-rT}\) and \(\frac{M!}{(M - n)!} \simeq M^n\). Mean and variance are both \(rT\). The probability density of a specific ordered sequence of spike times is \(p(t_1, \dots, t_n) = r^ne^{-rT}\), and the interspike interval (ISI) distribution is exponential, \(p(\tau) = re^{-r\tau}\), with mean \(1/r\) and coefficient of variation \(C_V = \sigma_\tau/\langle\tau\rangle = 1\).

8.4.2 The inhomogeneous Poisson process

If the rate varies in time, the probability of no spike between \(t_i\) and \(t_{i+1}\) is \(\prod_m(1 - r(t_i + m\Delta t)\Delta t) \to \exp\bigl(-\int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}}r(t)dt\bigr)\). Multiplying over intervals, \[p(t_1, \dots, t_n) = \prod_{i=1}^nr(t_i)\,\exp\Bigl(-\int_0^Tr(t)\,dt\Bigr).\]

To sample an inhomogeneous Poisson process with \(r(t) \leq r_{\max}\): generate a homogeneous process at rate \(r_{\max}\) (exponential gaps of mean \(1/r_{\max}\)), then keep each candidate spike at \(t_i\) with probability \(r(t_i)/r_{\max}\) (draw \(u \sim \mathcal U(0, 1)\), keep if \(u < r(t_i)/r_{\max}\)). Equivalently, on a grid of width \(dt\), fire with probability \(r(t)\,dt\) in each bin.

8.4.3 Comparison with data

Real spike trains are close to, but not exactly, Poisson. The variance of the spike count across trials grows roughly linearly with the mean, with a Fano factor \(F = \sigma_n^2/\langle n\rangle\) near \(1\) (fits \(\sigma^2_n = A\langle n\rangle^B\) give \(A, B \approx 1\), varying with the counting window). ISI histograms, however, are not exponential at short intervals: after a spike a neuron cannot fire again for a few milliseconds (refractoriness). A Poisson process with a dead time (or a gamma-distributed ISI) fits much better.

8.5 Avalanches from Poisson null models

Power-law avalanches are often taken as evidence of criticality. A good null model shows how easily independent neurons, driven by fluctuating inputs, produce avalanche statistics.

Consider \(N\) neurons, time bins of width \(dt\), and spiking probability \(\lambda_jdt\) in bin \(j\) for every neuron. The probability that bin \(j\) is not empty is \(1 - (1 - \lambda_jdt)^N\), so the probability that an avalanche lasts longer than \(n\) bins, given the rates, is \[P_>(n \mid \boldsymbol\lambda) = \prod_{j=1}^n\bigl[1 - (1 - \lambda_jdt)^N\bigr].\]

Rates redrawn at every bin. If \(\lambda_j\) are independent draws from \(Q(\lambda)\), \(P_>(n) = q^n\) with \(q = 1 - \int Q(\lambda)(1 - \lambda dt)^Nd\lambda\): durations are geometric (exponential), with no power law. For an exponential \(Q(\lambda) = \gamma e^{-\gamma\lambda}\) and \((1 - \lambda dt)^N \simeq e^{-N\lambda dt}\), the silence probability is \(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma + N\,dt}\).

Rate fixed during each avalanche. If \(\lambda\) is drawn once and kept for the whole avalanche (a slowly varying common input), then with \((1 - \lambda dt)^N \simeq e^{-\delta\lambda}\), \(\delta = N\,dt\), \[P_>(n) = \gamma\int_0^\infty d\lambda\,e^{-\gamma\lambda}\bigl(1 - e^{-\delta\lambda}\bigr)^n = \frac\gamma\delta B\Bigl(\frac\gamma\delta, n + 1\Bigr) \simeq \Gamma\Bigl(1 + \frac\gamma\delta\Bigr)n^{-\gamma/\delta}.\] For large \(n\) the integrand is a sharp step at \(\lambda^* = \ln n/\delta\) (saddle point), so \(P_>(n) \simeq e^{-\gamma\lambda^*} = n^{-\gamma/\delta}\): a power law, with an exponent set by the parameters, not by any critical point. Slow, shared fluctuations of the input suffice to produce scale-free avalanches (Touboul and Destexhe, 2017). Criticality claims require more than a power law: exponent relations, data collapse and the dependence on the bin width (Mariani et al., 2022).

8.6 Exercises

(Spike-count statistics) Show that for a homogeneous Poisson process the Fano factor is \(1\) for any window, the ISI is exponential, and \(C_V = 1\). How do refractoriness and bursting change \(C_V\)?

Solution. \(\langle n\rangle = \sigma_n^2 = rT\) gives \(F = 1\). The probability of no spike in \([t_i, t_i + \tau]\) and one in \([t_i + \tau, t_i + \tau + d\tau]\) is \(e^{-r\tau}r\,d\tau\), so \(p(\tau) = re^{-r\tau}\), with \(\langle\tau\rangle = 1/r\), \(\sigma_\tau = 1/r\), \(C_V = 1\). A dead time \(\tau_{\text{ref}}\) shifts the distribution: \(p(\tau) = re^{-r(\tau - \tau_{\text{ref}})}\) for \(\tau > \tau_{\text{ref}}\), so \(C_V = \frac{1/r}{\tau_{\text{ref}} + 1/r} < 1\) (more regular). Bursting produces many short and some long intervals, so \(C_V > 1\).

(Thinning) Simulate an inhomogeneous Poisson process with \(r(t) = 20[1 + \sin(2\pi t)]\) Hz on \([0, 10]\) s by thinning from \(r_{\max} = 40\) Hz. Check that the expected count is \(200\) and that the peri-stimulus time histogram reproduces \(r(t)\).

Solution. Generate exponential gaps of mean \(1/40\) s, giving about \(400\) candidates, and accept each with probability \(r(t_i)/40\). The expected number of accepted spikes is \(\int_0^{10}r(t)dt = 200\), since the sine integrates to zero over whole periods. Histogramming spike times modulo \(1\) s over many trials reproduces the sinusoidal profile, with Poisson errors \(\propto\sqrt{\text{count}}\). The method is exact: the accepted points form a Poisson process with intensity \(r_{\max}\cdot r(t)/r_{\max} = r(t)\).

(HW 4) Poisson null models of avalanches. Generate spike trains for \(N = 100\) neurons with \(dt = 0.01\), firing probability \(\lambda_t\,dt\) per bin, for at least \(10^4\) bins, in three cases: (1) independent rates for each neuron, drawn every bin from \(p(\lambda) = re^{-r\lambda}\) with \(r = 0.1\); (2) a common rate for all neurons drawn every bin from the same exponential; (3) a common rate drawn every bin from a gamma distribution with mean \(0.2\) and variance \(10\). Compare the avalanches. Optional: compute the avalanche durations analytically.

Solution. The exponential has mean \(1/r = 10\) Hz, so \(\langle\lambda dt\rangle = 0.1\). The gamma has shape \(k = 0.2^2/10 = 0.004\) and scale \(\theta = 50\). With \(10^5\) bins:

case fraction of silent bins number of avalanches mean duration mean size
(1) independent \(\simeq 10^{-4}\) a handful \(\sim 10^4\) bins \(\sim 10^5\)
(2) shared exponential \(0.090\) \(8\,179\) \(11.1\) bins \(123\)
(3) shared gamma \(0.984\) \(1\,573\) \(1.02\) bins \(12.8\)

  1. With \(N = 100\) independent neurons the probability of a silent bin is \((1 - 0.1)^{100} \simeq 3\times10^{-5}\): activity never stops, so avalanches are not even defined.

  2. Silence probability \(\int Q(\lambda)e^{-N\lambda dt}d\lambda = \frac{1}{1 + N\,dt\,\langle\lambda\rangle} = \frac1{11} = 0.091\). Durations are geometric with continuation probability \(10/11\), mean \(11\) bins, exactly as observed. Sizes have an exponential tail.

  3. The gamma rate is zero most of the time and occasionally huge. Silence probability \((1 + N\,dt\,\theta)^{-k} = 51^{-0.004} = 0.984\); avalanches almost always last one bin, but their sizes, \(\mathrm{Binomial}(N, \lambda dt)\) with \(\lambda\) heavy-tailed, are broadly distributed up to \(N\), looking like a power law over a decade.

Neither case is critical. Correlations induced by a common input change the statistics completely. Analytically, with rates redrawn every bin the duration is always geometric, \(P(T > n) = q^n\). Only rates that persist over an avalanche give power-law durations (next exercise).

(Power-law durations from a quenched rate) Compute \(P_>(n) = \gamma\int_0^\infty e^{-\gamma\lambda}(1 - e^{-\delta\lambda})^n d\lambda\) exactly and find its large-\(n\) behaviour. Check numerically for \(\gamma = 1\), \(\delta = 0.5\).

Solution. With \(u = e^{-\delta\lambda}\), \(P_>(n) = \frac\gamma\delta\int_0^1u^{\gamma/\delta - 1}(1 - u)^ndu = \frac\gamma\delta B(\frac\gamma\delta, n + 1) = \frac{\Gamma(1 + \gamma/\delta)\,\Gamma(n + 1)}{\Gamma(n + 1 + \gamma/\delta)} \simeq \Gamma(1 + \gamma/\delta)\,n^{-\gamma/\delta}\). For \(\gamma/\delta = 2\): the ratio \(P_>(n)/n^{-2}\) is \(1.52\), \(1.94\), \(1.994\), \(1.9994\) for \(n = 10\), \(10^2\), \(10^3\), \(10^4\), converging to \(\Gamma(3) = 2\). The duration density decays as \(n^{-3}\) here; with \(\gamma = \delta\) it would decay as \(n^{-2}\), the “critical” exponent, obtained without any criticality.

(Spike-triggered average of a linear neuron) Suppose \(r(t) = r_0 + \int_0^\infty D(\tau)s(t - \tau)d\tau\) with white-noise stimulus, \(\langle s(t)s(t')\rangle = \sigma^2\delta(t - t')\). Show that \(C(\tau) = \frac{\sigma^2}{r_0}D(\tau)\).

Solution. \(Q_{rs}(-\tau) = \frac1T\int r(t)s(t - \tau)dt = \int D(\tau')\langle s(t - \tau')s(t - \tau)\rangle d\tau' = \sigma^2D(\tau)\), since the \(r_0\) term averages to zero against a zero-mean stimulus. Dividing by \(\langle r\rangle = r_0\) gives \(C(\tau) = \sigma^2D(\tau)/r_0\). For white-noise stimuli the spike-triggered average is the linear filter of the neuron, up to a constant: this is why reverse correlation identifies receptive fields.

## From stimulus to response

### Firing rates

A neuron's output is a sequence of action potentials ("spikes") at times $t_1, \dots, t_n$. Since their shape is stereotyped, a spike train is represented by the **neural response function**
$$\rho(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n\delta(t - t_i), \qquad \sum_ih(t - t_i) = \int d\tau\,h(\tau)\rho(t - \tau).$$
Spike trains vary from trial to trial even with identical stimuli. Useful rates are:

- the **spike-count rate** $r = n/T = \frac1T\int_0^T\rho(\tau)d\tau$;

For small $\Delta t$, $r(t)\Delta t$ is the probability of a spike in $[t, t + \Delta t]$, and for any smooth $h$, $\int h(\tau)\langle\rho(t - \tau)\rangle d\tau = \int h(\tau)r(t - \tau)d\tau$.

From a single trial, $r(t)$ is estimated by filtering the spike train with a window $w$: $r(t) \simeq \int w(\tau)\rho(t - \tau)d\tau$. Options are a rectangular window of width $\Delta t$, a Gaussian $w(\tau) \propto e^{-\tau^2/2\sigma_w^2}$, or the causal kernel $w(\tau) = [\alpha^2\tau e^{-\alpha\tau}]_+$, which uses only past spikes. The choice of window sets a trade-off between temporal resolution and noise.

The **tuning curve** $f(s) = \langle r\rangle$ gives the mean firing rate as a function of a stimulus parameter $s$. Examples are a Gaussian tuning to the orientation of a bar in primary visual cortex, $f(s) = r_{\max}e^{-(s - s_{\max})^2/2\sigma^2}$; cosine tuning to arm-movement direction in motor cortex, $f(s) = r_0 + (r_{\max} - r_0)\cos(s - s_{\max})$; and sigmoidal tuning to binocular disparity. - **Weber's law**: the just-noticeable difference $\Delta s$ is proportional to the stimulus, $\Delta s/s = k$.
- **Fechner's law**: integrating Weber's law, perceived intensity is logarithmic in the stimulus, $P = k\ln(s/s_0)$.

### Spike-triggered average

$$C(\tau) = \Bigl\langle\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ns(t_i - \tau)\Bigr\rangle \simeq \frac{1}{\langle n\rangle}\int_0^Tr(t)s(t - \tau)dt = \frac{Q_{rs}(-\tau)}{\langle r\rangle},$$

## Spike-train statistics

### The homogeneous Poisson process

The simplest null model assumes spikes are independent events at a constant rate $r$. Divide $[0, T]$ into $M$ bins of width $\Delta t = T/M$, small enough to hold at most one spike. The probability of $n$ spikes is
$$P_T(n) = \lim_{\Delta t\to0}\binom Mn(r\Delta t)^n(1 - r\Delta t)^{M - n} = \frac{(rT)^n}{n!}e^{-rT},$$
using $(1 - r\Delta t)^{M} \to e^{-rT}$ and $\frac{M!}{(M - n)!} \simeq M^n$. Mean and variance are both $rT$. The probability density of a specific ordered sequence of spike times is $p(t_1, \dots, t_n) = r^ne^{-rT}$, and the **interspike interval** (ISI) distribution is exponential, $p(\tau) = re^{-r\tau}$, with mean $1/r$ and coefficient of variation $C_V = \sigma_\tau/\langle\tau\rangle = 1$.

### The inhomogeneous Poisson process

If the rate varies in time, the probability of no spike between $t_i$ and $t_{i+1}$ is $\prod_m(1 - r(t_i + m\Delta t)\Delta t) \to \exp\bigl(-\int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}}r(t)dt\bigr)$. Multiplying over intervals,
$$p(t_1, \dots, t_n) = \prod_{i=1}^nr(t_i)\,\exp\Bigl(-\int_0^Tr(t)\,dt\Bigr).$$

::: {.definition name="Simulating spike trains by thinning"}
To sample an inhomogeneous Poisson process with $r(t) \leq r_{\max}$: generate a homogeneous process at rate $r_{\max}$ (exponential gaps of mean $1/r_{\max}$), then keep each candidate spike at $t_i$ with probability $r(t_i)/r_{\max}$ (draw $u \sim \mathcal U(0, 1)$, keep if $u < r(t_i)/r_{\max}$). Equivalently, on a grid of width $dt$, fire with probability $r(t)\,dt$ in each bin.
:::

### Comparison with data

Real spike trains are close to, but not exactly, Poisson. The variance of the spike count across trials grows roughly linearly with the mean, with a **Fano factor** $F = \sigma_n^2/\langle n\rangle$ near $1$ (fits $\sigma^2_n = A\langle n\rangle^B$ give $A, B \approx 1$, varying with the counting window). ISI histograms, however, are not exponential at short intervals: after a spike a neuron cannot fire again for a few milliseconds (**refractoriness**). A Poisson process with a dead time (or a gamma-distributed ISI) fits much better.

## Avalanches from Poisson null models

Power-law avalanches are often taken as evidence of criticality. A good null model shows how easily independent neurons, driven by fluctuating inputs, produce avalanche statistics.

Consider $N$ neurons, time bins of width $dt$, and spiking probability $\lambda_jdt$ in bin $j$ for every neuron. The probability that bin $j$ is not empty is $1 - (1 - \lambda_jdt)^N$, so the probability that an avalanche lasts longer than $n$ bins, given the rates, is
$$P_>(n \mid \boldsymbol\lambda) = \prod_{j=1}^n\bigl[1 - (1 - \lambda_jdt)^N\bigr].$$

**Rates redrawn at every bin.** If $\lambda_j$ are independent draws from $Q(\lambda)$, $P_>(n) = q^n$ with $q = 1 - \int Q(\lambda)(1 - \lambda dt)^Nd\lambda$: durations are **geometric** (exponential), with no power law. For an exponential $Q(\lambda) = \gamma e^{-\gamma\lambda}$ and $(1 - \lambda dt)^N \simeq e^{-N\lambda dt}$, the silence probability is $\frac{\gamma}{\gamma + N\,dt}$.

**Rate fixed during each avalanche.** If $\lambda$ is drawn once and kept for the whole avalanche (a slowly varying common input), then with $(1 - \lambda dt)^N \simeq e^{-\delta\lambda}$, $\delta = N\,dt$,
$$P_>(n) = \gamma\int_0^\infty d\lambda\,e^{-\gamma\lambda}\bigl(1 - e^{-\delta\lambda}\bigr)^n = \frac\gamma\delta B\Bigl(\frac\gamma\delta, n + 1\Bigr) \simeq \Gamma\Bigl(1 + \frac\gamma\delta\Bigr)n^{-\gamma/\delta}.$$
For large $n$ the integrand is a sharp step at $\lambda^* = \ln n/\delta$ (saddle point), so $P_>(n) \simeq e^{-\gamma\lambda^*} = n^{-\gamma/\delta}$: a **power law**, with an exponent set by the parameters, not by any critical point. Slow, shared fluctuations of the input suffice to produce scale-free avalanches (Touboul and Destexhe, 2017). Criticality claims require more than a power law: exponent relations, data collapse and the dependence on the bin width (Mariani et al., 2022).

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(Spike-count statistics)** Show that for a homogeneous Poisson process the Fano factor is $1$ for any window, the ISI is exponential, and $C_V = 1$. How do refractoriness and bursting change $C_V$?
:::

::: {.solution}
$\langle n\rangle = \sigma_n^2 = rT$ gives $F = 1$. The probability of no spike in $[t_i, t_i + \tau]$ and one in $[t_i + \tau, t_i + \tau + d\tau]$ is $e^{-r\tau}r\,d\tau$, so $p(\tau) = re^{-r\tau}$, with $\langle\tau\rangle = 1/r$, $\sigma_\tau = 1/r$, $C_V = 1$. A dead time $\tau_{\text{ref}}$ shifts the distribution: $p(\tau) = re^{-r(\tau - \tau_{\text{ref}})}$ for $\tau > \tau_{\text{ref}}$, so $C_V = \frac{1/r}{\tau_{\text{ref}} + 1/r} < 1$ (more regular). Bursting produces many short and some long intervals, so $C_V > 1$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Thinning)** Simulate an inhomogeneous Poisson process with $r(t) = 20[1 + \sin(2\pi t)]$ Hz on $[0, 10]$ s by thinning from $r_{\max} = 40$ Hz. Check that the expected count is $200$ and that the peri-stimulus time histogram reproduces $r(t)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Generate exponential gaps of mean $1/40$ s, giving about $400$ candidates, and accept each with probability $r(t_i)/40$. The expected number of accepted spikes is $\int_0^{10}r(t)dt = 200$, since the sine integrates to zero over whole periods. Histogramming spike times modulo $1$ s over many trials reproduces the sinusoidal profile, with Poisson errors $\propto\sqrt{\text{count}}$. The method is exact: the accepted points form a Poisson process with intensity $r_{\max}\cdot r(t)/r_{\max} = r(t)$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(HW 4) Poisson null models of avalanches.** Generate spike trains for $N = 100$ neurons with $dt = 0.01$, firing probability $\lambda_t\,dt$ per bin, for at least $10^4$ bins, in three cases: (1) independent rates for each neuron, drawn every bin from $p(\lambda) = re^{-r\lambda}$ with $r = 0.1$; (2) a common rate for all neurons drawn every bin from the same exponential; (3) a common rate drawn every bin from a gamma distribution with mean $0.2$ and variance $10$. Compare the avalanches. Optional: compute the avalanche durations analytically.
:::

::: {.solution}
The exponential has mean $1/r = 10$ Hz, so $\langle\lambda dt\rangle = 0.1$. The gamma has shape $k = 0.2^2/10 = 0.004$ and scale $\theta = 50$. With $10^5$ bins:

| case | fraction of silent bins | number of avalanches | mean duration | mean size |
|:--|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| (1) independent | $\simeq 10^{-4}$ | a handful | $\sim 10^4$ bins | $\sim 10^5$ |
| (2) shared exponential | $0.090$ | $8\,179$ | $11.1$ bins | $123$ |
| (3) shared gamma | $0.984$ | $1\,573$ | $1.02$ bins | $12.8$ |

(1) With $N = 100$ independent neurons the probability of a silent bin is $(1 - 0.1)^{100} \simeq 3\times10^{-5}$: activity never stops, so avalanches are not even defined.

(2) Silence probability $\int Q(\lambda)e^{-N\lambda dt}d\lambda = \frac{1}{1 + N\,dt\,\langle\lambda\rangle} = \frac1{11} = 0.091$. Durations are geometric with continuation probability $10/11$, mean $11$ bins, exactly as observed. Sizes have an exponential tail.

(3) The gamma rate is zero most of the time and occasionally huge. Silence probability $(1 + N\,dt\,\theta)^{-k} = 51^{-0.004} = 0.984$; avalanches almost always last one bin, but their sizes, $\mathrm{Binomial}(N, \lambda dt)$ with $\lambda$ heavy-tailed, are broadly distributed up to $N$, looking like a power law over a decade.

Neither case is critical. Correlations induced by a common input change the statistics completely. Analytically, with rates redrawn every bin the duration is always geometric, $P(T > n) = q^n$. Only rates that persist over an avalanche give power-law durations (next exercise).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Power-law durations from a quenched rate)** Compute $P_>(n) = \gamma\int_0^\infty e^{-\gamma\lambda}(1 - e^{-\delta\lambda})^n d\lambda$ exactly and find its large-$n$ behaviour. Check numerically for $\gamma = 1$, $\delta = 0.5$.
:::

::: {.solution}
With $u = e^{-\delta\lambda}$, $P_>(n) = \frac\gamma\delta\int_0^1u^{\gamma/\delta - 1}(1 - u)^ndu = \frac\gamma\delta B(\frac\gamma\delta, n + 1) = \frac{\Gamma(1 + \gamma/\delta)\,\Gamma(n + 1)}{\Gamma(n + 1 + \gamma/\delta)} \simeq \Gamma(1 + \gamma/\delta)\,n^{-\gamma/\delta}$. For $\gamma/\delta = 2$: the ratio $P_>(n)/n^{-2}$ is $1.52$, $1.94$, $1.994$, $1.9994$ for $n = 10$, $10^2$, $10^3$, $10^4$, converging to $\Gamma(3) = 2$. The duration density decays as $n^{-3}$ here; with $\gamma = \delta$ it would decay as $n^{-2}$, the "critical" exponent, obtained without any criticality.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Spike-triggered average of a linear neuron)** Suppose $r(t) = r_0 + \int_0^\infty D(\tau)s(t - \tau)d\tau$ with white-noise stimulus, $\langle s(t)s(t')\rangle = \sigma^2\delta(t - t')$. Show that $C(\tau) = \frac{\sigma^2}{r_0}D(\tau)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$Q_{rs}(-\tau) = \frac1T\int r(t)s(t - \tau)dt = \int D(\tau')\langle s(t - \tau')s(t - \tau)\rangle d\tau' = \sigma^2D(\tau)$, since the $r_0$ term averages to zero against a zero-mean stimulus. Dividing by $\langle r\rangle = r_0$ gives $C(\tau) = \sigma^2D(\tau)/r_0$. For white-noise stimuli the spike-triggered average *is* the linear filter of the neuron, up to a constant: this is why reverse correlation identifies receptive fields.
:::
