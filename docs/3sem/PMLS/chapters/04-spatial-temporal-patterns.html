4  Spatial and temporal patterns of biodiversity

Chapter 3 explained the distribution of abundances in a community. Ecologists measure several other macroecological patterns: how many species are found in areas of increasing size, how many are endemic to an area, how long species persist before disappearing locally. This chapter derives them from the same null model of independent, randomly placed species. It then shows how space changes the picture, gives a “grand-canonical” formulation in which the number of species itself fluctuates, and closes with metapopulations: species living on a network of habitat patches, whose persistence becomes a spectral problem.

4.1 Species–area and endemic–area relationships

4.1.1 Three regimes of the SAR

Over many orders of magnitude of area, the SAR has three phases. At small scales \(S\) grows quickly, roughly linearly, because every new individual tends to be a new species. At intermediate scales it grows sublinearly, close to a power law \(S \propto A^z\) with \(z \approx 0.2\)–\(0.3\). At continental scales it steepens again, because new areas contain new, independent biotas. We model the intermediate regime.

4.1.2 The random-placement null model

Following Coleman (1981), divide a region of area \(A\) containing \(S\) species into cells and consider a sub-area \(a\), a fraction \(\alpha = a/A\) of the total. Individuals are placed independently and uniformly (random placement). A species with \(n_i\) individuals is absent from \(a\) with probability \(q_i = (1 - \alpha)^{n_i}\) and present with probability \(p_i = 1 - q_i\). The number of species found in \(a\) is a sum of independent Bernoulli variables, with generating function \[G(z) = \prod_{i=1}^S\bigl(q_i + p_iz\bigr).\] Its mean and variance, given the abundances \(\mathbf n = (n_1, \dots, n_S)\), are \[\langle s(a \mid \mathbf n)\rangle = \sum_ip_i = S - \sum_{i=1}^S(1 - \alpha)^{n_i}, \qquad \sigma^2(a \mid \mathbf n) = \sum_ip_iq_i = \sum_i\bigl[(1 - \alpha)^{n_i} - (1 - \alpha)^{2n_i}\bigr].\] If the abundances themselves are independent draws from the SAD \(P(n)\), averaging over \(\mathbf n\) gives \[\mathrm{SAR}(a) = S\Bigl[1 - \sum_{n\geq1}(1 - \alpha)^nP(n)\Bigr] = S\bigl[1 - \mathcal G(1 - \alpha)\bigr],\] where \(\mathcal G(z) = \sum_nP(n)z^n\) is the generating function of the SAD. The SAR is the SAD’s generating function evaluated at \(1 - \alpha\).

4.1.3 The endemic–area relationship

A species is endemic to \(a\) if all its individuals fall inside \(a\), which happens with probability \(\alpha^{n_i}\). Hence \[\mathrm{EAR}(a \mid \mathbf n) = \sum_i\alpha^{n_i}, \qquad \mathrm{EAR}(a) = S\sum_{n\geq1}\alpha^nP(n) = S\,\mathcal G(\alpha).\]

Under random placement, for any SAD, \[\mathrm{EAR}(a) = S - \mathrm{SAR}(A - a).\]

Proof. \(S - \mathrm{SAR}(A - a) = S\,\mathcal G(1 - (1 - \alpha)) = S\,\mathcal G(\alpha)\). A species is endemic to \(a\) exactly when it is absent from the complement \(A - a\). \(\square\)

4.1.4 The log-series case

For the log-series \(P(n) = \frac{x^n}{n\,|\ln(1 - x)|}\) (with \(x = 1 - \nu\) close to \(1\)), \(\mathcal G(z) = \frac{\ln(1 - xz)}{\ln(1 - x)}\), so \[\frac{\mathrm{SAR}(\alpha)}{S} = 1 - \frac{\ln\bigl(1 - x(1 - \alpha)\bigr)}{\ln(1 - x)} = 1 + \frac{\ln\bigl(\nu(1 - \alpha) + \alpha\bigr)}{|\ln\nu|}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{EAR}(\alpha)}{S} = \frac{\ln(1 - x\alpha)}{\ln(1 - x)}.\] At small \(\alpha\) the SAR grows linearly (\(\simeq \alpha x/[(1 - x)|\ln\nu|]\)); at intermediate \(\alpha\) it grows logarithmically, which over a couple of decades is hard to distinguish from a power law. The EAR stays tiny until \(\alpha\) approaches \(1\) and then shoots up.

4.1.5 Habitat loss and extinctions

Destroying a fraction of a region eliminates the species endemic to the destroyed part. The correct estimate of extinctions is therefore \(\mathrm{EAR}(a_{\text{lost}})\), which by the complementarity above equals \(S - \mathrm{SAR}(A - a_{\text{lost}})\). The common practice of fitting a power-law SAR, \(S \propto A^z\), and predicting a loss \(1 - (1 - f)^z\) for a lost fraction \(f\) uses an approximate SAR and typically overestimates extinctions. He and Hubbell (2011) showed that the EAR rises much more slowly than this backward extrapolation of the SAR suggests.

4.2 Persistence times

4.2.1 The species persistence-time distribution

Monitoring a region over time, a species appears (by speciation, immigration or recolonisation), persists for a while, and disappears. The species persistence time (SPT) \(\tau\) is the length of such an interval. Breeding-bird surveys in North America, grassland plots in Kansas, fish and forest censuses all give SPT distributions with a power-law body and a cut-off. Long-term data provide many intervals \(\tau_1, \tau_2, \dots\), treated as realisations of the same process, from which the distribution can be estimated.

4.2.2 Mean-field solution by generating functions

Take a species with linear rates \(b_n = bn\), \(d_n = dn\) (no immigration, \(b \lesssim d\)), starting from \(m\) individuals. The generating function \(G(z, t) = \sum_nP_n(t)z^n\) turns the master equation into a first-order PDE. Multiplying by \(z^n\) and summing, \[\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} = \bigl(d + bz^2 - (b + d)z\bigr)\frac{\partial G}{\partial z} = (1 - z)(d - bz)\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial z},\] with \(G(z, 0) = z^m\). It is solved by the method of characteristics. Along curves with \(\frac{dz}{dt} = -(1 - z)(d - bz)\), \(G\) is constant. Integrating by partial fractions with \(\delta = d - b > 0\), \[\frac{(z - 1)\bigl(bz_0 - d\bigr)}{(z_0 - 1)(bz - d)} = e^{-\delta t},\] which gives the initial point \(z_0\) of the characteristic through \((z, t)\): \[z_0 = \frac{d - g}{b - g}, \qquad g(z, t) = e^{-\delta t}\,\frac{bz - d}{z - 1}.\] Hence \(G(z, t) = \bigl[\frac{d - g(z, t)}{b - g(z, t)}\bigr]^m\). The probability of being extinct at time \(t\) is \(P_0(t) = G(0, t)\): \[P_0(t) = \Bigl[\frac{d\,(1 - e^{-\delta t})}{d - b\,e^{-\delta t}}\Bigr]^m.\] For \(m = 1\) the persistence probability \(\mathcal P(t) = 1 - P_0(t)\) is \[\mathcal P(t) = \frac{\delta\,e^{-\delta t}}{d - b\,e^{-\delta t}} \;\simeq\; \frac1{dt}\,\mathcal F(\delta t), \qquad \mathcal F(x) = \frac{x}{e^x - 1},\] in the scaling regime \(\delta \to 0\), \(t \to \infty\) with \(\delta t\) fixed. \(\mathcal F\) is a finite-size scaling function: \(\mathcal F(x) \to 1\) for \(x \to 0\) and decays exponentially for \(x \gg 1\). The SPT density is \[p(\tau) = -\frac{d\mathcal P}{d\tau} \propto \tau^{-2}\,f(\delta\tau),\] a power law with exponent \(2\) and an exponential cut-off at \(\tau_c = 1/\delta = 1/(d - b)\). At criticality (\(b = d\)) the cut-off disappears. The exponent differs from that of the first return of an unbiased random walk (\(3/2\)) because the step rates are proportional to \(n\): large populations fluctuate faster.

If instead \(b > d\), a finite fraction of species never goes extinct; if \(b < d\) with a large gap, lifetimes are exponential with mean \(1/(d - b)\).

4.2.3 Space changes the exponent

The mean-field model ignores space. In the spatial voter model with speciation (Chapter 3), individuals sit on a lattice or network, die, and are replaced by a neighbour’s offspring or, with probability \(\nu\), by a new species. Simulations give power-law SPT distributions whose exponent depends on the geometry. In terms of the cumulative persistence \(\mathcal P(t) \sim t^{-\beta}\):

  • \(\beta \simeq 1/2\) on a one-dimensional chain (lifetimes \(\propto t^{-3/2}\), as for a random walk),
  • intermediate values on river-like networks and in two dimensions (where logarithmic corrections appear),
  • \(\beta \simeq 1\) in three dimensions and in the well-mixed (global) case, recovering the mean-field \(t^{-2}\) density.

Spatial constraints make species persist longer: a species confined to a region of a chain can only be eroded from its edges.

4.2.4 Sampling effects

Observation windows are finite. A species already present at the start, or still present at the end, has a truncated measured lifetime. Censoring piles up probability at the longest observable time and produces a spurious bump at the end of the empirical distribution. Suweis et al. (2012) model the effective lifetime \(L_e\) in terms of the true one \(L\), the observation window \([x_0, x_f]\) and the appearance time, and derive the distribution of \(L_e\). This model fits breeding-bird, grassland, forest and fish data, including the final bump, with the same underlying power-law SPT.

4.3 The grand-canonical formulation of neutral theory

So far the number of individuals was fixed. Let it fluctuate, and describe the community by the occupation numbers \(\boldsymbol\phi = (\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots)\): \(\phi_k\) is the number of species with abundance \(k\). Species are created (speciation or immigration) at rate \(b_0\) with one individual. A species with \(k\) individuals gains one at rate \(b_k\) and loses one at rate \(d_k\), moving from class \(k\) to \(k \pm 1\). Writing \(\hat e_k\) for the unit vector of class \(k\), \[\dot P(\boldsymbol\phi) = b_0P(\boldsymbol\phi - \hat e_1) + d_1(\phi_1 + 1)P(\boldsymbol\phi + \hat e_1) - d_1\phi_1P(\boldsymbol\phi) + \sum_{k\geq2}\bigl[b_{k-1}(\phi_{k-1} + 1)P(\boldsymbol\phi + \hat e_{k-1} - \hat e_k) - d_k\phi_kP(\boldsymbol\phi)\bigr]\] \[\qquad + \sum_{k\geq1}\bigl[d_{k+1}(\phi_{k+1} + 1)P(\boldsymbol\phi + \hat e_{k+1} - \hat e_k) - b_k\phi_kP(\boldsymbol\phi)\bigr] - b_0P(\boldsymbol\phi).\] Detailed balance for each move, together with the ansatz of independent occupation numbers, \(P_{\text{st}}(\boldsymbol\phi) = \prod_kP_k(\phi_k)/Z\), gives \[P_k(\phi_k) \propto \frac{1}{\phi_k!}\Bigl(\frac{b_0}{d_1}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\frac{b_{i-1}}{d_i}\Bigr)^{\phi_k}.\] For linear rates \(b_i = bi\), \(d_i = di\), with \(x = b/d\) and \(\gamma = b_0/d\), \[P_k(\phi_k) = \frac{1}{\phi_k!}\Bigl(\gamma\frac{x^k}k\Bigr)^{\phi_k}e^{-\gamma x^k/k}:\] each \(\phi_k\) is an independent Poisson variable with mean \(\langle\phi_k\rangle = \gamma x^k/k\), which is again the log-series. The normalisation is \(Z = \exp\bigl(\gamma\sum_kx^k/k\bigr) = (1 - x)^{-\gamma}\).

The total number of species \(S = \sum_k\phi_k\) is a sum of independent Poisson variables, hence Poisson: \[P(S) = \frac{\langle S\rangle^S}{S!}e^{-\langle S\rangle}, \qquad \langle S\rangle = -\gamma\ln(1 - x).\]

4.3.1 Relations between patterns

The grand-canonical picture suggests a coarse-grained view of a community. Species appear at random times, at a rate \(\lambda\) (speciation or immigration), and each persists for a time \(\tau\) drawn from the SPT distribution, independently of the others: an “\(M/G/\infty\) queue”. Then:

  • the number of species present is Poisson, with mean given by Little’s law: \(\langle S\rangle = \lambda\langle\tau\rangle\);
  • the SPT and the species richness are linked. With \(p(\tau) \propto \tau^{-2}\) cut off at \(\tau_c = 1/\delta\), \(\langle\tau\rangle \propto \ln\tau_c\), so \(\langle S\rangle \propto \lambda\ln(1/\delta)\). Compare \(-\gamma\ln(1 - x)\) above, with \(1 - x = \delta/d\);
  • making appearances also random in space, each species occupying a region that grows with its age, turns the SPT into a range-size distribution and hence into a SAR (Suweis, Rinaldo, Maritan, 2012). Temporal and spatial patterns are two faces of the same birth–death–speciation process.

4.4 Metapopulations

Many species live on patchy habitats: ponds, meadows, islands, forest fragments. Levins (1969) introduced the metapopulation, a “population of populations” in which each local population has a substantial risk of extinction and the species persists only regionally, through recolonisation. Hanski applied and extended the idea (e.g. to the Glanville fritillary butterfly in the Åland islands).

4.4.1 The Hanski model

Let \(P_x(t) \in [0, 1]\) be the probability that patch \(x\) is occupied. Occupied patches go extinct at rate \(v_x\); an empty patch is colonised from occupied patches \(y\) at rate \(K_{xy}\): \[\dot P_x = -v_xP_x + (1 - P_x)\sum_yK_{xy}P_y, \qquad K_{xy} = c\,e^{-|x - y|/\xi}\ (y \neq x),\] with \(\xi\) the dispersal distance and \(c\) a colonisation rate (which can include patch sizes). The dynamics keeps \(0 \leq P_x \leq 1\).

4.4.2 Persistence as an eigenvalue problem

The empty state \(P = 0\) is always an equilibrium. Linearising, \(\dot P_x \simeq \sum_y\bigl(K_{xy} - v_x\delta_{xy}\bigr)P_y\). With equal extinction rates \(v_x = v\), the empty state is unstable, and the species persists, if and only if \[\lambda_M > v,\] where \(\lambda_M\) is the largest eigenvalue of the colonisation matrix \(K\), the metapopulation capacity of the landscape. Since \(K\) has positive entries (and is irreducible if every patch can be reached), the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees that \(\lambda_M\) is real, simple and dominant, with an eigenvector of positive entries. The landscape’s ability to support the species is summarised in a single number. With heterogeneous \(v_x\), the criterion becomes that the leading eigenvalue of \(E^{-1}K\), with \(E = \operatorname{diag}(v_x)\), exceeds \(1\). Data on the fraction of occupied patches against \(\log\lambda_M\) across patch networks show the predicted threshold.

4.4.3 Translation-invariant landscapes

On a regular lattice with \(v_x = v\) and \(K_{xy} = \hat K_{x-y}\), Fourier modes \(\hat P(q) = \sum_xe^{iqx}P_x\) decouple at linear order: \[\dot{\hat P}(q) = \bigl(-v + \hat K(q)\bigr)\hat P(q), \qquad \hat K(q) = \sum_xe^{iqx}\hat K_x .\] Since \(|\hat K(q)| \leq \hat K(0) = \sum_x\hat K_x\), the most unstable mode is uniform. The species survives iff \(\hat K(0) > v\), and the homogeneous non-trivial equilibrium is \[P^* = 1 - \frac{v}{\hat K(0)} .\] This is a mean-field transcritical bifurcation, the same structure as the SIS epidemic threshold (Chapter 2) and the contact process.

4.4.4 Heterogeneous extinction rates and localisation

Let \(\hat K(0) = 1\) and let the \(v_x\) be drawn independently from a distribution \(Q(v)\). In mean field each patch feels the average occupancy \(m = \frac1N\sum_yP_y\): \[\dot P_x = -v_xP_x + (1 - P_x)m \;\Longrightarrow\; P^*_x = \frac{m^*}{v_x + m^*},\] with self-consistency \(m^* = \int dv\,Q(v)\,\frac{m^*}{v + m^*}\). Besides \(m^* = 0\), a positive solution exists iff \(\int dv\,Q(v)/v > 1\). As the transition is approached, \(m^* \to 0\) and patches with small \(v_x\) dominate the occupancy. How localised the occupancy becomes is measured by the participation ratio \[I = \frac{\langle P^2\rangle}{\langle P\rangle^2}, \qquad \langle P^\ell\rangle = \int dv\,Q(v)\Bigl(\frac{m^*}{v + m^*}\Bigr)^\ell .\] If \(Q\) is bounded away from zero (e.g. uniform on \([\bar v - \Delta, \bar v + \Delta]\)), \(I\) stays finite as \(m^* \to 0\): the species is spread over the landscape. If \(Q(v) \propto v^{\delta - 1}\) near \(v = 0\) (e.g. a Gamma distribution with shape \(\delta < 1\)), then \(\langle P\rangle \propto m^{*\delta}\) and \(\langle P^2\rangle \propto m^{*\delta}\), so \(I \propto m^{*-\delta} \to \infty\). Near extinction the species survives only in a vanishing fraction of exceptionally good patches: localisation. Conservation must then target these refugia.

4.5 Exercises

(HW 2a) SAR and EAR. Using the log-series SAD \(P(n) = c(1 - \nu)^n/n\), plot \(\mathrm{SAR}(\alpha)/S\) and \(\mathrm{EAR}(\alpha)/S\) against the relative area \(\alpha\). Compare the SAR with a power law \(k\alpha^z\), with \(z \in (0.2, 0.3)\) and \(k\) tuned to match the random-placement solution as closely as possible.

Solution. With \(x = 1 - \nu\): \(\mathrm{SAR}/S = 1 - \ln(1 - x(1 - \alpha))/\ln(1 - x)\) and \(\mathrm{EAR}/S = \ln(1 - x\alpha)/\ln(1 - x)\). The SAR rises linearly at very small \(\alpha\), bends over, and approaches \(1\) logarithmically. The EAR is concave-up and negligible until \(\alpha \gtrsim 0.5\). A straight-line fit of \(\ln\mathrm{SAR}\) against \(\ln\alpha\) over \(\alpha \in [10^{-3}, 1]\) gives \(z \simeq 0.31\) for \(x = 0.999\) and larger \(z\) for smaller \(x\) (\(0.55\) for \(x = 0.99\), \(0.75\) for \(x = 0.95\)). Exponents in the empirical range \(0.2\)–\(0.3\) correspond to \(x\) extremely close to \(1\), i.e. to the near-critical forests of Chapter 3. A single power law matches the curve only over an intermediate window; on log–log axes the random-placement SAR is visibly curved.

(HW 2b) Habitat loss. If one third of the area is removed, how many species go extinct? Estimate it with the EAR and with the SAR, and compare with the power-law estimate.

Solution. Extinct species are those endemic to the removed third: \(\mathrm{EAR}(1/3)/S = \ln(1 - x/3)/\ln(1 - x)\). Using the SAR, the survivors are the species present in the remaining two thirds, \(\mathrm{SAR}(2/3)\), so the loss is \(1 - \mathrm{SAR}(2/3)/S\), which is the same number by complementarity. For \(x = 0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 0.999\) the loss is \(15.5\%\), \(12.7\%\), \(8.7\%\), \(5.9\%\). A power-law SAR with \(z = 0.25\) gives \(1 - (2/3)^{0.25} = 9.6\%\), and with \(z\) fitted over the whole range (e.g. \(z = 0.55\) for \(x = 0.99\)) it gives \(20\%\), more than twice the exact \(8.7\%\). Approximating the SAR by a power law and extrapolating overestimates extinctions; the EAR is the right tool.

(Variance of the number of species) Under random placement with fixed abundances, show that the variance of the number of species in \(a\) is maximal for intermediate \(\alpha\), and compute it for \(S\) species all with the same abundance \(n\).

Solution. \(\sigma^2 = \sum_ip_iq_i\) with \(q_i = (1 - \alpha)^{n_i}\). Each term vanishes at \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\alpha = 1\) and is maximal when \(q_i = 1/2\). For equal abundances \(\sigma^2 = S\,q(1 - q)\) with \(q = (1 - \alpha)^n\), maximal (\(= S/4\)) at \(\alpha^* = 1 - 2^{-1/n} \simeq \ln2/n\). The species count fluctuates most at the scale where a typical species is equally likely to be found or missed.

(Extinction probability) For the linear birth–death process with \(b < d\) and \(m\) initial individuals, show from \(P_0(t)\) that eventual extinction is certain, and compute the mean time to extinction for \(m = 1\). What happens for \(b > d\)?

Solution. As \(t \to \infty\), \(P_0(t) \to [d/d]^m = 1\) for \(b < d\). For \(m = 1\) the mean lifetime is \(\int_0^\infty\mathcal P(t)dt = \int_0^\infty\frac{\delta e^{-\delta t}}{d - be^{-\delta t}}dt = \frac1b\ln\frac{d}{d - b} = \frac1b\ln\frac{1}{1 - b/d}\), which diverges logarithmically at criticality \(b \to d\): the \(t^{-2}\) tail with cut-off \(1/\delta\). For \(b > d\), \(e^{-\delta t}\) grows: \(P_0(\infty) = (d/b)^m < 1\), so a species survives forever with probability \(1 - (d/b)^m\).

(Grand-canonical community) In the grand-canonical model with \(x = 0.99\) and \(\gamma = 30\), compute \(\langle S\rangle\), \(\mathrm{Var}(S)\), the expected number of singletons, and the mean total abundance.

Solution. \(\langle S\rangle = -\gamma\ln(1 - x) = 30\ln100 = 138.2\) and, being Poisson, \(\mathrm{Var}(S) = 138.2\) (standard deviation \(11.8\)). Singletons: \(\langle\phi_1\rangle = \gamma x = 29.7\). Total abundance: \(\sum_kk\langle\phi_k\rangle = \gamma\sum_kx^k = \gamma\frac{x}{1 - x} = 2970\) individuals.

(Metapopulation capacity) Three patches on a line at positions \(0\), \(1\), \(3\), with \(K_{xy} = e^{-|x - y|}\) for \(x \neq y\) and \(K_{xx} = 0\). Compute \(\lambda_M\) and the critical extinction rate. Which patch matters most?

Solution. \(K = \begin{pmatrix}0 & e^{-1} & e^{-3}\\ e^{-1} & 0 & e^{-2}\\ e^{-3} & e^{-2} & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0.368 & 0.050\\0.368 & 0 & 0.135\\0.050 & 0.135 & 0\end{pmatrix}\). Its largest eigenvalue is \(\lambda_M \simeq 0.41\), so the species persists iff \(v < 0.41\). The Perron eigenvector \(\simeq (0.66, 0.69, 0.31)\) weights the middle patch most: it is close to both others. The isolated patch at \(x = 3\) contributes little, and removing it lowers \(\lambda_M\) only slightly, to \(e^{-1} = 0.37\).

## Species–area and endemic–area relationships

### Three regimes of the SAR

Over many orders of magnitude of area, the SAR has three phases. At small scales $S$ grows quickly, roughly linearly, because every new individual tends to be a new species. At intermediate scales it grows sublinearly, close to a power law $S \propto A^z$ with $z \approx 0.2$–$0.3$. At continental scales it steepens again, because new areas contain new, independent biotas. We model the intermediate regime.

### The random-placement null model

Following Coleman (1981), divide a region of area $A$ containing $S$ species into cells and consider a sub-area $a$, a fraction $\alpha = a/A$ of the total. Individuals are placed independently and uniformly (**random placement**). A species with $n_i$ individuals is absent from $a$ with probability $q_i = (1 - \alpha)^{n_i}$ and present with probability $p_i = 1 - q_i$. The number of species found in $a$ is a sum of independent Bernoulli variables, with generating function
$$G(z) = \prod_{i=1}^S\bigl(q_i + p_iz\bigr).$$
Its mean and variance, given the abundances $\mathbf n = (n_1, \dots, n_S)$, are
$$\langle s(a \mid \mathbf n)\rangle = \sum_ip_i = S - \sum_{i=1}^S(1 - \alpha)^{n_i}, \qquad \sigma^2(a \mid \mathbf n) = \sum_ip_iq_i = \sum_i\bigl[(1 - \alpha)^{n_i} - (1 - \alpha)^{2n_i}\bigr].$$
If the abundances themselves are independent draws from the SAD $P(n)$, averaging over $\mathbf n$ gives
$$\mathrm{SAR}(a) = S\Bigl[1 - \sum_{n\geq1}(1 - \alpha)^nP(n)\Bigr] = S\bigl[1 - \mathcal G(1 - \alpha)\bigr],$$
where $\mathcal G(z) = \sum_nP(n)z^n$ is the generating function of the SAD. The SAR is the SAD's generating function evaluated at $1 - \alpha$.

### The endemic–area relationship

A species is **endemic** to $a$ if *all* its individuals fall inside $a$, which happens with probability $\alpha^{n_i}$. Hence
$$\mathrm{EAR}(a \mid \mathbf n) = \sum_i\alpha^{n_i}, \qquad \mathrm{EAR}(a) = S\sum_{n\geq1}\alpha^nP(n) = S\,\mathcal G(\alpha).$$

::: {.proposition name="SAR and EAR are complementary"}
Under random placement, for any SAD,
$$\mathrm{EAR}(a) = S - \mathrm{SAR}(A - a).$$
:::

*Proof.* $S - \mathrm{SAR}(A - a) = S\,\mathcal G(1 - (1 - \alpha)) = S\,\mathcal G(\alpha)$. A species is endemic to $a$ exactly when it is absent from the complement $A - a$. $\square$

### The log-series case

For the log-series $P(n) = \frac{x^n}{n\,|\ln(1 - x)|}$ (with $x = 1 - \nu$ close to $1$), $\mathcal G(z) = \frac{\ln(1 - xz)}{\ln(1 - x)}$, so
$$\frac{\mathrm{SAR}(\alpha)}{S} = 1 - \frac{\ln\bigl(1 - x(1 - \alpha)\bigr)}{\ln(1 - x)} = 1 + \frac{\ln\bigl(\nu(1 - \alpha) + \alpha\bigr)}{|\ln\nu|}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{EAR}(\alpha)}{S} = \frac{\ln(1 - x\alpha)}{\ln(1 - x)}.$$
At small $\alpha$ the SAR grows linearly ($\simeq \alpha x/[(1 - x)|\ln\nu|]$); at intermediate $\alpha$ it grows logarithmically, which over a couple of decades is hard to distinguish from a power law. The EAR stays tiny until $\alpha$ approaches $1$ and then shoots up.

### Habitat loss and extinctions

Destroying a fraction of a region eliminates the species endemic to the destroyed part. The correct estimate of extinctions is therefore $\mathrm{EAR}(a_{\text{lost}})$, which by the complementarity above equals $S - \mathrm{SAR}(A - a_{\text{lost}})$. The common practice of fitting a power-law SAR, $S \propto A^z$, and predicting a loss $1 - (1 - f)^z$ for a lost fraction $f$ uses an *approximate* SAR and typically overestimates extinctions. He and Hubbell (2011) showed that the EAR rises much more slowly than this backward extrapolation of the SAR suggests.

## Persistence times

### The species persistence-time distribution

Monitoring a region over time, a species appears (by speciation, immigration or recolonisation), persists for a while, and disappears. The **species persistence time** (SPT) $\tau$ is the length of such an interval. Breeding-bird surveys in North America, grassland plots in Kansas, fish and forest censuses all give SPT distributions with a power-law body and a cut-off. Long-term data provide many intervals $\tau_1, \tau_2, \dots$, treated as realisations of the same process, from which the distribution can be estimated.

### Mean-field solution by generating functions

Take a species with linear rates $b_n = bn$, $d_n = dn$ (no immigration, $b \lesssim d$), starting from $m$ individuals. The generating function $G(z, t) = \sum_nP_n(t)z^n$ turns the master equation into a first-order PDE. Multiplying by $z^n$ and summing,
$$\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} = \bigl(d + bz^2 - (b + d)z\bigr)\frac{\partial G}{\partial z} = (1 - z)(d - bz)\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial z},$$
with $G(z, 0) = z^m$. It is solved by the **method of characteristics**. Along curves with $\frac{dz}{dt} = -(1 - z)(d - bz)$, $G$ is constant. Integrating by partial fractions with $\delta = d - b > 0$,
$$\frac{(z - 1)\bigl(bz_0 - d\bigr)}{(z_0 - 1)(bz - d)} = e^{-\delta t},$$
which gives the initial point $z_0$ of the characteristic through $(z, t)$:
$$z_0 = \frac{d - g}{b - g}, \qquad g(z, t) = e^{-\delta t}\,\frac{bz - d}{z - 1}.$$
Hence $G(z, t) = \bigl[\frac{d - g(z, t)}{b - g(z, t)}\bigr]^m$. The probability of being extinct at time $t$ is $P_0(t) = G(0, t)$:
$$P_0(t) = \Bigl[\frac{d\,(1 - e^{-\delta t})}{d - b\,e^{-\delta t}}\Bigr]^m.$$
For $m = 1$ the **persistence probability** $\mathcal P(t) = 1 - P_0(t)$ is
$$\mathcal P(t) = \frac{\delta\,e^{-\delta t}}{d - b\,e^{-\delta t}} \;\simeq\; \frac1{dt}\,\mathcal F(\delta t), \qquad \mathcal F(x) = \frac{x}{e^x - 1},$$
in the scaling regime $\delta \to 0$, $t \to \infty$ with $\delta t$ fixed. $\mathcal F$ is a **finite-size scaling function**: $\mathcal F(x) \to 1$ for $x \to 0$ and decays exponentially for $x \gg 1$. The SPT density is
$$p(\tau) = -\frac{d\mathcal P}{d\tau} \propto \tau^{-2}\,f(\delta\tau),$$
a power law with exponent $2$ and an exponential cut-off at $\tau_c = 1/\delta = 1/(d - b)$. At criticality ($b = d$) the cut-off disappears. The exponent differs from that of the first return of an unbiased random walk ($3/2$) because the step rates are proportional to $n$: large populations fluctuate faster.

If instead $b > d$, a finite fraction of species never goes extinct; if $b < d$ with a large gap, lifetimes are exponential with mean $1/(d - b)$.

### Space changes the exponent

The mean-field model ignores space. In the spatial voter model with speciation (Chapter 3), individuals sit on a lattice or network, die, and are replaced by a neighbour's offspring or, with probability $\nu$, by a new species. Simulations give power-law SPT distributions whose exponent depends on the geometry. In terms of the cumulative persistence $\mathcal P(t) \sim t^{-\beta}$:

- $\beta \simeq 1/2$ on a one-dimensional chain (lifetimes $\propto t^{-3/2}$, as for a random walk),
- intermediate values on river-like networks and in two dimensions (where logarithmic corrections appear),
- $\beta \simeq 1$ in three dimensions and in the well-mixed (global) case, recovering the mean-field $t^{-2}$ density.

Spatial constraints make species persist *longer*: a species confined to a region of a chain can only be eroded from its edges.

### Sampling effects

Observation windows are finite. A species already present at the start, or still present at the end, has a truncated measured lifetime. Censoring piles up probability at the longest observable time and produces a spurious bump at the end of the empirical distribution. Suweis et al. (2012) model the *effective* lifetime $L_e$ in terms of the true one $L$, the observation window $[x_0, x_f]$ and the appearance time, and derive the distribution of $L_e$. This model fits breeding-bird, grassland, forest and fish data, including the final bump, with the same underlying power-law SPT.

## The grand-canonical formulation of neutral theory

So far the number of individuals was fixed. Let it fluctuate, and describe the community by the occupation numbers $\boldsymbol\phi = (\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots)$: $\phi_k$ is the number of species with abundance $k$. Species are created (speciation or immigration) at rate $b_0$ with one individual. A species with $k$ individuals gains one at rate $b_k$ and loses one at rate $d_k$, moving from class $k$ to $k \pm 1$. Writing $\hat e_k$ for the unit vector of class $k$,
$$\dot P(\boldsymbol\phi) = b_0P(\boldsymbol\phi - \hat e_1) + d_1(\phi_1 + 1)P(\boldsymbol\phi + \hat e_1) - d_1\phi_1P(\boldsymbol\phi) + \sum_{k\geq2}\bigl[b_{k-1}(\phi_{k-1} + 1)P(\boldsymbol\phi + \hat e_{k-1} - \hat e_k) - d_k\phi_kP(\boldsymbol\phi)\bigr]$$
$$\qquad + \sum_{k\geq1}\bigl[d_{k+1}(\phi_{k+1} + 1)P(\boldsymbol\phi + \hat e_{k+1} - \hat e_k) - b_k\phi_kP(\boldsymbol\phi)\bigr] - b_0P(\boldsymbol\phi).$$
Detailed balance for each move, together with the ansatz of **independent occupation numbers**, $P_{\text{st}}(\boldsymbol\phi) = \prod_kP_k(\phi_k)/Z$, gives
$$P_k(\phi_k) \propto \frac{1}{\phi_k!}\Bigl(\frac{b_0}{d_1}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\frac{b_{i-1}}{d_i}\Bigr)^{\phi_k}.$$
For linear rates $b_i = bi$, $d_i = di$, with $x = b/d$ and $\gamma = b_0/d$,
$$P_k(\phi_k) = \frac{1}{\phi_k!}\Bigl(\gamma\frac{x^k}k\Bigr)^{\phi_k}e^{-\gamma x^k/k}:$$
each $\phi_k$ is an independent **Poisson** variable with mean $\langle\phi_k\rangle = \gamma x^k/k$, which is again the log-series. The normalisation is $Z = \exp\bigl(\gamma\sum_kx^k/k\bigr) = (1 - x)^{-\gamma}$.

The total number of species $S = \sum_k\phi_k$ is a sum of independent Poisson variables, hence Poisson:
$$P(S) = \frac{\langle S\rangle^S}{S!}e^{-\langle S\rangle}, \qquad \langle S\rangle = -\gamma\ln(1 - x).$$

### Relations between patterns

The grand-canonical picture suggests a coarse-grained view of a community. Species appear at random times, at a rate $\lambda$ (speciation or immigration), and each persists for a time $\tau$ drawn from the SPT distribution, independently of the others: an "$M/G/\infty$ queue". Then:

- the number of species present is Poisson, with mean given by **Little's law**: $\langle S\rangle = \lambda\langle\tau\rangle$;
- the SPT and the species richness are linked. With $p(\tau) \propto \tau^{-2}$ cut off at $\tau_c = 1/\delta$, $\langle\tau\rangle \propto \ln\tau_c$, so $\langle S\rangle \propto \lambda\ln(1/\delta)$. Compare $-\gamma\ln(1 - x)$ above, with $1 - x = \delta/d$;
- making appearances also random in space, each species occupying a region that grows with its age, turns the SPT into a range-size distribution and hence into a SAR (Suweis, Rinaldo, Maritan, 2012). Temporal and spatial patterns are two faces of the same birth–death–speciation process.

## Metapopulations

Many species live on **patchy** habitats: ponds, meadows, islands, forest fragments. Levins (1969) introduced the **metapopulation**, a "population of populations" in which each local population has a substantial risk of extinction and the species persists only regionally, through recolonisation. Hanski applied and extended the idea (e.g. to the Glanville fritillary butterfly in the Åland islands).

### The Hanski model

Let $P_x(t) \in [0, 1]$ be the probability that patch $x$ is occupied. Occupied patches go extinct at rate $v_x$; an empty patch is colonised from occupied patches $y$ at rate $K_{xy}$:
$$\dot P_x = -v_xP_x + (1 - P_x)\sum_yK_{xy}P_y, \qquad K_{xy} = c\,e^{-|x - y|/\xi}\ (y \neq x),$$
with $\xi$ the dispersal distance and $c$ a colonisation rate (which can include patch sizes). The dynamics keeps $0 \leq P_x \leq 1$.

### Persistence as an eigenvalue problem

The empty state $P = 0$ is always an equilibrium. Linearising, $\dot P_x \simeq \sum_y\bigl(K_{xy} - v_x\delta_{xy}\bigr)P_y$. With equal extinction rates $v_x = v$, the empty state is unstable, and the species persists, if and only if
$$\lambda_M > v,$$
where $\lambda_M$ is the largest eigenvalue of the colonisation matrix $K$, the **metapopulation capacity** of the landscape. Since $K$ has positive entries (and is irreducible if every patch can be reached), the **Perron–Frobenius theorem** guarantees that $\lambda_M$ is real, simple and dominant, with an eigenvector of positive entries. The landscape's ability to support the species is summarised in a single number. With heterogeneous $v_x$, the criterion becomes that the leading eigenvalue of $E^{-1}K$, with $E = \operatorname{diag}(v_x)$, exceeds $1$. Data on the fraction of occupied patches against $\log\lambda_M$ across patch networks show the predicted threshold.

### Translation-invariant landscapes

On a regular lattice with $v_x = v$ and $K_{xy} = \hat K_{x-y}$, Fourier modes $\hat P(q) = \sum_xe^{iqx}P_x$ decouple at linear order:
$$\dot{\hat P}(q) = \bigl(-v + \hat K(q)\bigr)\hat P(q), \qquad \hat K(q) = \sum_xe^{iqx}\hat K_x .$$
Since $|\hat K(q)| \leq \hat K(0) = \sum_x\hat K_x$, the most unstable mode is uniform. The species survives iff $\hat K(0) > v$, and the homogeneous non-trivial equilibrium is
$$P^* = 1 - \frac{v}{\hat K(0)} .$$
This is a mean-field transcritical bifurcation, the same structure as the SIS epidemic threshold (Chapter 2) and the contact process.

### Heterogeneous extinction rates and localisation

Let $\hat K(0) = 1$ and let the $v_x$ be drawn independently from a distribution $Q(v)$. In mean field each patch feels the average occupancy $m = \frac1N\sum_yP_y$:
$$\dot P_x = -v_xP_x + (1 - P_x)m \;\Longrightarrow\; P^*_x = \frac{m^*}{v_x + m^*},$$
with self-consistency $m^* = \int dv\,Q(v)\,\frac{m^*}{v + m^*}$. Besides $m^* = 0$, a positive solution exists iff $\int dv\,Q(v)/v > 1$. As the transition is approached, $m^* \to 0$ and patches with small $v_x$ dominate the occupancy. How localised the occupancy becomes is measured by the participation ratio
$$I = \frac{\langle P^2\rangle}{\langle P\rangle^2}, \qquad \langle P^\ell\rangle = \int dv\,Q(v)\Bigl(\frac{m^*}{v + m^*}\Bigr)^\ell .$$
If $Q$ is bounded away from zero (e.g. uniform on $[\bar v - \Delta, \bar v + \Delta]$), $I$ stays finite as $m^* \to 0$: the species is spread over the landscape. If $Q(v) \propto v^{\delta - 1}$ near $v = 0$ (e.g. a Gamma distribution with shape $\delta < 1$), then $\langle P\rangle \propto m^{*\delta}$ and $\langle P^2\rangle \propto m^{*\delta}$, so $I \propto m^{*-\delta} \to \infty$. Near extinction the species survives only in a vanishing fraction of exceptionally good patches: **localisation**. Conservation must then target these refugia.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(HW 2a) SAR and EAR.** Using the log-series SAD $P(n) = c(1 - \nu)^n/n$, plot $\mathrm{SAR}(\alpha)/S$ and $\mathrm{EAR}(\alpha)/S$ against the relative area $\alpha$. Compare the SAR with a power law $k\alpha^z$, with $z \in (0.2, 0.3)$ and $k$ tuned to match the random-placement solution as closely as possible.
:::

::: {.solution}
With $x = 1 - \nu$: $\mathrm{SAR}/S = 1 - \ln(1 - x(1 - \alpha))/\ln(1 - x)$ and $\mathrm{EAR}/S = \ln(1 - x\alpha)/\ln(1 - x)$. The SAR rises linearly at very small $\alpha$, bends over, and approaches $1$ logarithmically. The EAR is concave-up and negligible until $\alpha \gtrsim 0.5$. A straight-line fit of $\ln\mathrm{SAR}$ against $\ln\alpha$ over $\alpha \in [10^{-3}, 1]$ gives $z \simeq 0.31$ for $x = 0.999$ and larger $z$ for smaller $x$ ($0.55$ for $x = 0.99$, $0.75$ for $x = 0.95$). Exponents in the empirical range $0.2$–$0.3$ correspond to $x$ extremely close to $1$, i.e. to the near-critical forests of Chapter 3. A single power law matches the curve only over an intermediate window; on log–log axes the random-placement SAR is visibly curved.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(HW 2b) Habitat loss.** If one third of the area is removed, how many species go extinct? Estimate it with the EAR and with the SAR, and compare with the power-law estimate.
:::

::: {.solution}
Extinct species are those endemic to the removed third: $\mathrm{EAR}(1/3)/S = \ln(1 - x/3)/\ln(1 - x)$. Using the SAR, the survivors are the species present in the remaining two thirds, $\mathrm{SAR}(2/3)$, so the loss is $1 - \mathrm{SAR}(2/3)/S$, which is the *same* number by complementarity. For $x = 0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 0.999$ the loss is $15.5\%$, $12.7\%$, $8.7\%$, $5.9\%$. A power-law SAR with $z = 0.25$ gives $1 - (2/3)^{0.25} = 9.6\%$, and with $z$ fitted over the whole range (e.g. $z = 0.55$ for $x = 0.99$) it gives $20\%$, more than twice the exact $8.7\%$. Approximating the SAR by a power law and extrapolating overestimates extinctions; the EAR is the right tool.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Variance of the number of species)** Under random placement with fixed abundances, show that the variance of the number of species in $a$ is maximal for intermediate $\alpha$, and compute it for $S$ species all with the same abundance $n$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\sigma^2 = \sum_ip_iq_i$ with $q_i = (1 - \alpha)^{n_i}$. Each term vanishes at $\alpha = 0$ and $\alpha = 1$ and is maximal when $q_i = 1/2$. For equal abundances $\sigma^2 = S\,q(1 - q)$ with $q = (1 - \alpha)^n$, maximal ($= S/4$) at $\alpha^* = 1 - 2^{-1/n} \simeq \ln2/n$. The species count fluctuates most at the scale where a typical species is equally likely to be found or missed.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Extinction probability)** For the linear birth–death process with $b < d$ and $m$ initial individuals, show from $P_0(t)$ that eventual extinction is certain, and compute the mean time to extinction for $m = 1$. What happens for $b > d$?
:::

::: {.solution}
As $t \to \infty$, $P_0(t) \to [d/d]^m = 1$ for $b < d$. For $m = 1$ the mean lifetime is $\int_0^\infty\mathcal P(t)dt = \int_0^\infty\frac{\delta e^{-\delta t}}{d - be^{-\delta t}}dt = \frac1b\ln\frac{d}{d - b} = \frac1b\ln\frac{1}{1 - b/d}$, which diverges logarithmically at criticality $b \to d$: the $t^{-2}$ tail with cut-off $1/\delta$. For $b > d$, $e^{-\delta t}$ grows: $P_0(\infty) = (d/b)^m < 1$, so a species survives forever with probability $1 - (d/b)^m$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Grand-canonical community)** In the grand-canonical model with $x = 0.99$ and $\gamma = 30$, compute $\langle S\rangle$, $\mathrm{Var}(S)$, the expected number of singletons, and the mean total abundance.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\langle S\rangle = -\gamma\ln(1 - x) = 30\ln100 = 138.2$ and, being Poisson, $\mathrm{Var}(S) = 138.2$ (standard deviation $11.8$). Singletons: $\langle\phi_1\rangle = \gamma x = 29.7$. Total abundance: $\sum_kk\langle\phi_k\rangle = \gamma\sum_kx^k = \gamma\frac{x}{1 - x} = 2970$ individuals.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Metapopulation capacity)** Three patches on a line at positions $0$, $1$, $3$, with $K_{xy} = e^{-|x - y|}$ for $x \neq y$ and $K_{xx} = 0$. Compute $\lambda_M$ and the critical extinction rate. Which patch matters most?
:::

::: {.solution}
$K = \begin{pmatrix}0 & e^{-1} & e^{-3}\\ e^{-1} & 0 & e^{-2}\\ e^{-3} & e^{-2} & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0.368 & 0.050\\0.368 & 0 & 0.135\\0.050 & 0.135 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. Its largest eigenvalue is $\lambda_M \simeq 0.41$, so the species persists iff $v < 0.41$. The Perron eigenvector $\simeq (0.66, 0.69, 0.31)$ weights the middle patch most: it is close to both others. The isolated patch at $x = 3$ contributes little, and removing it lowers $\lambda_M$ only slightly, to $e^{-1} = 0.37$.
:::
