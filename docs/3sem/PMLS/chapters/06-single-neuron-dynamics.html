7  Dynamics of a single neuron

Before we build networks we need to understand what one node does. This chapter mirrors Prof. Suweis’s Chapter 6: cellular structure and membrane potential; firing rate as a response to a stimulus; the bio-electrophysiological (Hodgkin–Huxley) description; and the McCulloch–Pitts abstraction that will underlie all networks in Chapters 7–9.

7.1 6.1 The neuron

Neurons are cells specialised for propagating signals over long distances. Their morphology has three functional parts:

  • Dendrites — highly branched extensions that receive input signals from \(\sim 10^4\) other neurons through synapses.
  • Soma (cell body) — houses the nucleus; integrates the incoming currents.
  • Axon — a single, often very long (\(\gtrsim\) 1 m in some sensory neurons), extension that carries the output signal to downstream neurons.

The membrane potential \(V\) is the electrical potential inside the cell relative to the extracellular medium. Under resting conditions \(V_{\text{rest}} \approx -70\) mV. The cell is then said to be polarised.

The resting potential is maintained by ion pumps and channels in the cell membrane that create concentration gradients of Na\(^+\), K\(^+\), Ca\(^{2+}\) and Cl\(^-\). In particular, K\(^+\) is more concentrated inside the neuron and Na\(^+\) more concentrated outside.

  • Current in the form of positive ions flowing out of the cell (or negative ions flowing in) drives \(V\) more negative: hyperpolarisation.
  • Current into the cell drives \(V\) less negative or positive: depolarisation.

When depolarisation pushes \(V\) above a threshold \(V_{th} \approx -55\) mV, the neuron fires an action potential — a stereotyped \(\sim 100\) mV pulse of \(\sim 1\) ms duration that propagates along the axon.

Remark. The action potential’s shape is very nearly the same every time; the information the neuron transmits is coded in the timing of spikes (rate code, temporal code), not in their amplitude. This is the “all-or-none” principle established by Adrian in the 1920s and mathematised by Hodgkin & Huxley in the 1950s.

7.2 6.2 Neuron firing as response to a stimulus

Given an input stimulus \(s(t)\) we ask: what is the spike output? Model the spike train as a sum of Dirac deltas:

Given spike times \(\{t_i\}_{i=1}^n\) in an interval \([0, T]\), the neural response function is \[\rho(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(t - t_i) .\] For any test function \(h(\tau)\) the convolution \[\sum_{i=1}^{n} h(t - t_i) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} h(\tau)\,\rho(t - \tau)\,d\tau\] converts the spike train into a smooth signal.

The spike-count rate on a window \([0, T]\) is \[r = \frac{n}{T} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \rho(\tau)\,d\tau .\] The time-dependent firing rate \(\phi(t)\) is the running average of \(\rho\) over a moving window; formally \[\phi(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\,\mathbb E\!\left[\int_{t - \Delta t /2}^{t + \Delta t/2}\rho(\tau)\,d\tau\right] .\]

For a stationary point process \(\phi(t) = \lambda\) is the intensity.

For a sensory neuron responding to an oriented visual stimulus, the firing rate as a function of orientation \(\theta\) is a bell-shaped tuning curve: \[\phi(\theta) = \phi_{\max}\exp\!\bigl(-(\theta - \theta^*)^2/(2\sigma^2)\bigr),\] where \(\theta^*\) is the preferred orientation. Cataloguing tuning curves is a workhorse method of systems neuroscience.

7.3 6.3 Bio-electrophysiological modelling of the neuron

To go beyond phenomenology we need a physical model of the membrane. The membrane behaves as a capacitor with parallel-conductance ion channels:

For a passive patch of membrane, \[C_m\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -g_L(V - V_L) + I(t) ,\] where \(C_m\) is the membrane capacitance, \(g_L\) the leak conductance, \(V_L\) the leak reversal potential, and \(I(t)\) the input current. In the absence of active channels, the membrane behaves as a low-pass filter with time constant \(\tau_m = C_m/g_L \approx 10{-}30\) ms.

Adding voltage-gated Na\(^+\) and K\(^+\) conductances, whose gating variables \(m, h, n\) obey first-order kinetics, gives \[C_m\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -g_{Na}\,m^3 h\,(V - V_{Na}) - g_K\,n^4\,(V - V_K) - g_L(V - V_L) + I(t),\] with \(\dot m = \alpha_m(V)(1 - m) - \beta_m(V)m\), and analogous for \(h, n\). The four-dimensional system exhibits an excitable regime with an action potential and a stable refractory period.

The Hodgkin–Huxley model won its authors the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology and remains the biophysical gold standard.

For network-scale modelling the Hodgkin–Huxley description is impractical. The leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron replaces the spike-generating dynamics by a threshold: integrate the passive RC equation until \(V\) crosses \(V_{th}\), emit a spike, reset \(V \to V_{reset}\), and impose a refractory period \(\tau_{ref}\). LIF captures the essential firing behaviour with a single ODE and one non-linearity.

For a LIF neuron driven by a constant current \(I_0 > (V_{th} - V_L)g_L\), the interspike interval is \[\Delta t = \tau_m\,\ln\!\left(\frac{I_0/g_L - (V_{reset} - V_L)}{I_0/g_L - (V_{th} - V_L)}\right),\] so the firing rate is \(\phi(I_0) = 1/\Delta t\). This is the rate transfer function used in Wilson–Cowan (Chapter 7).

7.4 6.4 McCulloch–Pitts artificial neuron

For network-scale mathematical modelling we abstract even further. McCulloch & Pitts (1943) proposed the following:

A McCulloch–Pitts neuron with \(N\) inputs \(x_1, \ldots, x_N\) (each 0 or 1), synaptic weights \(w_1, \ldots, w_N\), and threshold \(\theta\) produces output \[y = \Theta\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\,x_i - \theta\right) \in \{0, 1\},\] where \(\Theta\) is the Heaviside step function.

The McCulloch–Pitts unit is the fundamental building block of every neural network in this course.

With appropriate weights and threshold, a single McCulloch–Pitts unit computes any two-input Boolean function except XOR. A two-layer network of such units can compute XOR — the classic Minsky–Papert observation that motivated multi-layer networks.

Remark. The step-function activation of the McCulloch–Pitts unit was replaced in modern practice by the sigmoid \(\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})\) (allowing gradient-based learning) and later by ReLU \(\max(0, x)\) (avoiding vanishing gradients in deep networks). Both are approximations of the biological non-linearity.

7.5 Exercises

Derive the LIF firing rate under constant current given above. State the parameter regime where the neuron fires periodically.

Solution. Solve \(C_m \dot V = -g_L(V - V_L) + I_0\) with initial condition \(V(0) = V_{reset}\) and first-passage condition \(V(\Delta t) = V_{th}\). The solution is \(V(t) = V_\infty + (V_{reset} - V_\infty)e^{-t/\tau_m}\) with \(V_\infty = V_L + I_0/g_L\). Setting \(V(\Delta t) = V_{th}\) and solving for \(\Delta t\) gives the formula. The neuron fires periodically iff \(V_\infty > V_{th}\), i.e. \(I_0 > (V_{th} - V_L)g_L\).

Show that no single McCulloch–Pitts unit implements the XOR function on two inputs.

Solution. Suppose weights \(w_1, w_2\) and threshold \(\theta\) give XOR. Then \(w_1 + w_2 \le \theta - 1\) (input \((1,1)\) outputs 0), but each of \(w_1, w_2 > \theta - 1\) (inputs \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) output 1). Summing: \(w_1 + w_2 > 2\theta - 2\). Combining with \(w_1 + w_2 \le \theta - 1\): \(\theta - 1 > 2\theta - 2\), i.e. \(\theta < 1\). But also \((0,0)\) must output 0, so \(\theta > 0\). Both bounds together give \(0 < \theta < 1\). Now check \((0, 1)\): need \(w_2 > \theta > 0\), and \((1, 1)\): \(w_1 + w_2 \le \theta - 1 < 0\). So \(w_1 < -w_2 < -\theta < 0\). Then \((1, 0)\): \(w_1 < 0 < \theta\), so output is 0, not 1. Contradiction.

Verify that the leak time constant \(\tau_m = C_m / g_L\) has units of time given typical values \(C_m \sim 10^{-6}\,\text{F/cm}^2\) and \(g_L \sim 10^{-4}\,\text{S/cm}^2\).

Solution. \(C/g = \text{F/S} = (\text{C/V})/(1/\text{Ω}) = \text{C}\cdot\text{Ω/V} = \text{s}\) (since \(\text{V} = \text{Ω A} = \text{Ω C/s}\)). Numerically \(\tau_m = 10^{-2}\) s = 10 ms, matching biology.

7.6 Callouts

The three levels of description — Hodgkin–Huxley, integrate-and-fire, McCulloch–Pitts — represent decreasing biological fidelity but increasing analytical tractability. Which one to use depends on the question you are asking.

For any rate model of a network, first sketch the transfer function \(\phi(I)\) of a single neuron. Its shape (linear-rectifier, sigmoidal, threshold-linear) will determine the qualitative regimes of the network.

The action potential is stereotyped: its amplitude carries no information. This is the reason firing-rate models exist — throwing away the details of each spike loses little.

The LIF neuron has a hard reset that removes any memory of the spike time; a soft-reset LIF (subtract \(V_{th} - V_{reset}\)) can produce very different collective dynamics near the firing threshold.
