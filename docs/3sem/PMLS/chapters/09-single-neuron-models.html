9  Single-neuron dynamics

Chapter 8 treated neurons as black boxes that emit spikes. This chapter opens the box. The membrane potential is set by the balance between ion concentration gradients and electric forces. Treating the membrane as an electric circuit gives the leaky integrate-and-fire model. Voltage-dependent conductances give the Hodgkin–Huxley model of the action potential, which reduces to the two-variable FitzHugh–Nagumo model. At the other extreme of abstraction, the McCulloch–Pitts neuron and the perceptron capture computation with a single threshold unit.

9.1 The neuron

A neuron receives inputs on its dendrites through about \(10^4\) synapses, integrates them in the soma, and, when the membrane potential at the axon hillock crosses a threshold, emits an action potential that travels along the axon (sped up by the myelin sheath) to the synapses of other neurons. At rest the inside of the cell is about \(70\) mV more negative than the outside: the membrane is polarised. Ion pumps keep \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\) concentrated outside and \(\text{K}^+\) inside. Current flowing out of the cell (positive ions leaving) makes the potential more negative (hyperpolarisation); current flowing in (depolarisation) makes it less negative. A spike is a rapid depolarisation of about \(100\) mV lasting about \(1\) ms, followed by a refractory period.

9.2 Electrophysiology

9.2.1 The Nernst potential

Ions cross the membrane through selective channels. Two forces act on them: diffusion down the concentration gradient and the electric field across the membrane. The sodium–potassium pump uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis to move \(3\,\text{Na}^+\) out and \(2\,\text{K}^+\) in, maintaining both gradients and a net charge imbalance.

Consider a membrane permeable only to \(\text{K}^+\), initially electrically neutral but with more \(\text{K}^+\) inside. Potassium diffuses out, leaving negative charge (impermeable organic anions) behind. The resulting field opposes further outflow, and equilibrium is reached when the two forces balance. Since only a tiny number of ions needs to move to build the field, the concentrations are essentially unchanged.

The equilibrium (reversal) potential of an ion species \(X\) of valence \(z_X\) is \[V_X = \frac{RT}{z_XF}\ln\frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i},\] with \(R\) the gas constant, \(T\) the temperature and \(F\) Faraday’s constant.

Derivation. At constant \(T\) and pressure the relevant potential is the Gibbs free energy, \(dG = -SdT + VdP + \mu dn\), and the chemical potential of an ideal solute at concentration \(x\) is \(\mu = \mu_0 + RT\ln x\). A charged ion in a region at potential \(V\) has the electrochemical potential \(\mu = \mu_0 + RT\ln x + z_XFV\). Equilibrium across the membrane requires equal electrochemical potentials inside and outside: \(RT\ln x_i + z_XFV_i = RT\ln x_o + z_XFV_o\). Solving for \(V_m = V_i - V_o\) gives the result. \(\square\)

At room temperature (\(RT/F \simeq 25\) mV), with \([\text{K}^+]_i = 140\) mM, \([\text{K}^+]_o = 5\) mM, \(V_{\text{K}} \simeq -84\) mV; with \([\text{Na}^+]_i = 12\) mM, \([\text{Na}^+]_o = 140\) mM, \(V_{\text{Na}} \simeq +62\) mV.

9.2.2 Several ions: the resting potential

With several permeable ions, each current follows Ohm’s law, \(I_X = g_X(V_m - V_X)\), with a conductance \(g_X\). At rest the total current vanishes, so the membrane potential is a conductance-weighted average: \[V_{\text{rest}} = \frac{\sum_Xg_XV_X}{\sum_Xg_X}.\] For \(g_{\text{Na}}/g_{\text{K}} \simeq 0.05\), \(V_{\text{rest}} \simeq -77\) mV: close to \(V_{\text{K}}\), since potassium dominates at rest. The rest state is a dynamic equilibrium: individual currents flow continuously and the pumps compensate them.

9.2.3 The membrane as an RC circuit

The lipid membrane is a thin insulator, i.e. a capacitor (\(C \simeq 1\,\mu\)F/cm²). Channels are (variable) resistors of conductance \(g_X\) in series with batteries \(V_X\). Current conservation gives \[C\frac{dV}{dt} = -\sum_Xg_X(V - V_X) + I = -g_{\text{inp}}(V - V_{\text{rest}}) + I,\] with \(g_{\text{inp}} = \sum_Xg_X\) the input conductance and \(I\) an injected current.

Voltage clamp. A feedback amplifier injects whatever current is needed to hold \(V\) at a command value \(V_s\); the injected current then measures the total ionic current at that voltage. With constant conductances the response to a current step is the charging of an RC circuit, \[V(t) = V_s + (V_c - V_s)e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g_{\text{inp}}},\] and the steady current gives the linear I–V relation, whose slope is \(g_{\text{inp}}\). Measured I–V curves are nonlinear because the conductances depend on \(V\). Selectively blocking channels with drugs (tetrodotoxin for \(\text{Na}^+\), tetraethylammonium for \(\text{K}^+\)) isolates each contribution.

For a time-dependent current, with \(V\) measured from rest, the solution of \(\tau\dot V = -V + RI(t)\) (\(R = 1/g_{\text{inp}}\)) is a convolution: \[V(t) = \frac R\tau\int_{-\infty}^te^{-(t - s)/\tau}I(s)\,ds,\] i.e. the membrane is a low-pass filter with time constant \(\tau \sim 10\) ms.

9.3 The integrate-and-fire model

Lapicque’s (1907) leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron keeps only the subthreshold RC dynamics and replaces the spike by a rule: \[\tau\frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - V_{\text{rest}}) + RI, \qquad V(t^-) = V_{\text{th}} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{spike, } V(t^+) = V_{\text{reset}}.\] For a constant current, starting from \(V_{\text{reset}} = V_{\text{rest}}\), \(V(t) = V_{\text{rest}} + RI(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\). The threshold is reached at \(t_{\text{sp}}\) such that \(RI(1 - e^{-t_{\text{sp}}/\tau}) = V_{\text{th}} - V_{\text{rest}} \equiv \Delta\). The firing rate is \[r = \frac{1}{t_{\text{sp}}} = \Bigl[\tau\ln\frac{RI}{RI - \Delta}\Bigr]^{-1}\Theta(RI - \Delta) \;\underset{RI \gg \Delta}{\simeq}\; \frac{RI - \Delta/2}{\tau\Delta}.\] There is a current threshold \(RI = \Delta\) (the rheobase) below which the neuron never fires. Above it the rate rises steeply, then linearly in \(I\) (the f–I curve). With \(\tau = 10\) ms and \(\Delta = 16\) mV, \(RI = 18, 20, 25, 40\) mV gives \(46\), \(62\), \(98\), \(196\) Hz. An absolute refractory period \(\tau_{\text{ref}}\) adds to \(t_{\text{sp}}\) and caps the rate at \(1/\tau_{\text{ref}}\).

Real neurons are not perfect integrators. The threshold is not sharp: the same current pulse sometimes produces a spike and sometimes not. And some neurons are resonators: they fire preferentially to inputs at a particular frequency, and can even fire after an inhibitory burst (post-inhibitory rebound). These behaviours require the dynamics of the ion channels.

9.3.1 Spike-rate adaptation

Many neurons fire fast at the onset of a constant current and then slow down. A potassium conductance \(g_{\text{sra}}\) that increases by \(\Delta g_{\text{sra}}\) at each spike and decays with a time constant \(\tau_{\text{sra}}\) of \(\sim 100\) ms reproduces this: \[\tau\frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - V_{\text{rest}}) - r_mg_{\text{sra}}(V - V_{\text{K}}) + RI, \qquad \tau_{\text{sra}}\frac{dg_{\text{sra}}}{dt} = -g_{\text{sra}} + \tau_{\text{sra}}\Delta g_{\text{sra}}\sum_i\delta(t - t_i).\] Each spike makes the next harder to trigger, so interspike intervals lengthen until a steady state is reached.

9.4 Voltage-dependent conductances: Hodgkin–Huxley

9.4.1 Gating variables

A channel contains \(q\) independent gates, each open with probability \(n\). The channel conducts only if all gates are open, with probability \(n^q\), so the conductance per unit area is \(g = \bar g\,n^q\), with \(\bar g\) the maximal conductance. Each gate opens at rate \(\alpha_n(V)\) and closes at rate \(\beta_n(V)\), a two-state Markov process whose mean obeys \[\frac{dn}{dt} = \alpha_n(V)(1 - n) - \beta_n(V)\,n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau_n(V)\frac{dn}{dt} = n_\infty(V) - n,\] with \(\tau_n = 1/(\alpha_n + \beta_n)\) and \(n_\infty = \alpha_n\tau_n\). The rates follow from the Boltzmann factors of the gate’s conformational change, \(\alpha, \beta \propto \exp(\pm qBV/k_BT)\), which makes \(n_\infty(V)\) a sigmoid. Persistent conductances (like the delayed-rectifier \(\text{K}^+\), \(q = 4\)) only activate. Transient conductances (like \(\text{Na}^+\)) have activation gates \(m\) that open with depolarisation and an inactivation gate \(h\) that closes with depolarisation: \(g_{\text{Na}} = \bar g_{\text{Na}}m^3h\).

9.4.2 The Hodgkin–Huxley model

From voltage-clamp experiments on the squid giant axon, Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) obtained \[C_m\frac{dV}{dt} = -\bar g_{\text{K}}n^4(V - V_{\text{K}}) - \bar g_{\text{Na}}m^3h(V - V_{\text{Na}}) - \bar g_L(V - V_L) + I,\] \[\tau_w(V)\frac{dw}{dt} = w_\infty(V) - w, \qquad w = n, m, h .\] The action potential unfolds as follows. A depolarisation opens the fast \(m\) gates. Sodium flows in and depolarises further (positive feedback, the upstroke toward \(V_{\text{Na}}\)). On a slower time scale \(h\) closes, inactivating sodium, and \(n\) opens, letting potassium out (repolarisation and an undershoot toward \(V_{\text{K}}\), the after-hyperpolarisation). While \(h\) recovers and \(n\) closes, the neuron is refractory. The model reproduces the shape and threshold of the spike, refractoriness and propagation along the axon. It earned the 1963 Nobel Prize, but four nonlinear equations per neuron are hard to analyse and expensive to simulate in large networks.

9.4.3 The FitzHugh–Nagumo reduction

Two observations simplify HH. \(m\) is much faster than the other variables, so \(m \simeq m_\infty(V)\). And \(h\) and \(n\) evolve on similar time scales and are roughly linearly related, so one variable can stand for both. The result is a fast excitation variable \(v\) (the membrane potential) and a slow recovery variable \(w\): \[\frac{dv}{dt} = v(v - a)(1 - v) - w + I, \qquad \frac{dw}{dt} = \varepsilon(v - \gamma w), \qquad \varepsilon \ll 1 .\] The cubic \(v\)-nullcline and the linear \(w\)-nullcline organise the phase plane. For small \(I\) they cross on the left branch of the cubic: a stable rest state, from which a small perturbation decays but a perturbation beyond the middle branch triggers a large excursion (a spike) before returning. The neuron is excitable. As \(I\) increases, the crossing moves to the middle branch, the rest state loses stability through a Hopf bifurcation, and the system settles on a relaxation limit cycle: periodic spiking. With \(a = 0.1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.01\), \(\gamma = 0.5\), the rest state is stable up to \(I \simeq 0.11\) and the model spikes periodically (with \(v\) swinging between about \(-0.28\) and \(1\)) beyond it.

Neurons are dynamical systems, and the bifurcation through which they start firing determines their computational class (Izhikevich). A saddle-node on an invariant circle gives integrators, which can fire at arbitrarily low rates (class 1). A Hopf bifurcation gives resonators with a non-zero minimum frequency (class 2), able to show post-inhibitory rebound.

9.5 Artificial neurons

9.5.1 McCulloch–Pitts neurons

McCulloch and Pitts (1943) abstracted the neuron into a threshold unit: inputs \(\sigma_j \in \{0, 1\}\) are weighted and summed, and the output is \[\sigma_{\text{out}} = \Theta\Bigl(\sum_jw_j\sigma_j - \theta\Bigr).\] With suitable weights and thresholds a unit implements logic gates: NOT (\(w = -1\), \(\theta = -1/2\)), AND (\(w_1 = w_2 = 1\), \(\theta = 3/2\)), OR (\(w_1 = w_2 = 1\), \(\theta = 1/2\)). Networks of such units can compute any Boolean function. Replacing \(\Theta\) by smooth or piecewise-linear activation functions (sigmoid \(1/(1 + e^{-x})\), \(\tanh\), ReLU \(\max(0, x)\), leaky ReLU, ELU, maxout) gives the units of modern artificial neural networks.

9.5.2 The perceptron

Rosenblatt’s perceptron (1958) is a threshold unit that learns its weights from labelled examples \((\boldsymbol\sigma^\mu, t^\mu)\): \[y = \Theta(\mathbf w\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma - \theta), \qquad \Delta\mathbf w = \eta\,(t^\mu - y^\mu)\,\boldsymbol\sigma^\mu, \quad \Delta\theta = -\eta(t^\mu - y^\mu).\] Weights change only when the unit errs, moving the decision hyperplane toward the misclassified example. The perceptron convergence theorem guarantees that the algorithm finds a separating hyperplane in a finite number of steps whenever one exists. The perceptron is a linear classifier, so it fails on problems that are not linearly separable, the simplest being XOR (Minsky and Papert, 1969). Solving XOR needs a hidden layer, and multi-layer networks trained by backpropagation descend from this observation.

9.6 Exercises

(Nernst and resting potentials) (a) Compute \(V_{\text{K}}\) and \(V_{\text{Na}}\) at \(37\,^\circ\)C for the concentrations in the text. (b) Find \(V_{\text{rest}}\) for \(g_{\text{Na}}/g_{\text{K}} = 0.05\). (c) What happens to \(V_{\text{rest}}\) if extracellular \(\text{K}^+\) rises to \(20\) mM?

Solution.

  1. \(RT/F = 26.7\) mV at \(310\) K: \(V_{\text{K}} = 26.7\ln(5/140) = -89.0\) mV, \(V_{\text{Na}} = 26.7\ln(140/12) = +65.6\) mV. (b) \(V_{\text{rest}} = (-89.0 + 0.05\times65.6)/1.05 = -81.6\) mV. (c) \(V_{\text{K}} = 26.7\ln(20/140) = -52.0\) mV and \(V_{\text{rest}} = (-52.0 + 3.3)/1.05 = -46.4\) mV. The cell depolarises by about \(35\) mV, enough to inactivate sodium channels and block firing. This is why the potassium concentration in the blood is tightly regulated (hyperkalaemia is dangerous for heart and nerves).

(f–I curve of the LIF neuron) Derive the firing rate of the LIF neuron for constant current, including an absolute refractory period \(\tau_{\text{ref}}\). Evaluate it for \(\tau = 10\) ms, \(\Delta = 16\) mV, \(RI = 20\) mV, and \(\tau_{\text{ref}} = 0\) or \(2\) ms. Derive the large-current approximation.

Solution. \(r = [\tau_{\text{ref}} + \tau\ln\frac{RI}{RI - \Delta}]^{-1}\) for \(RI > \Delta\), else \(0\). For \(RI = 20\) mV: \(\tau\ln5 = 16.1\) ms, so \(r = 62\) Hz without refractoriness and \(1/18.1\,\text{ms} = 55\) Hz with \(2\) ms. For \(RI \gg \Delta\), \(\ln\frac{1}{1 - x} = x + x^2/2 + \dots\) with \(x = \Delta/RI\) gives \(t_{\text{sp}} \simeq \frac{\tau\Delta}{RI}(1 + \frac{\Delta}{2RI})\), so \(r \simeq \frac{RI - \Delta/2}{\tau\Delta}\): linear in \(I\) with slope \(1/(\tau\Delta)\). At \(RI = 40\) mV this gives \(200\) Hz against the exact \(196\) Hz. Without the \(-\Delta/2\) correction the approximation would give \(150\) Hz.

(Gating kinetics) A gate with \(\alpha_n = 0.5\,\text{ms}^{-1}\) and \(\beta_n = 0.125\,\text{ms}^{-1}\) at a given voltage starts closed (\(n = 0\)). Find \(n(t)\) and the potassium conductance \(\bar g_{\text{K}}n^4\). Why does the conductance rise with a delay (sigmoidally) while \(n\) rises exponentially?

Solution. \(\tau_n = 1/(0.625) = 1.6\) ms and \(n_\infty = 0.5\times1.6 = 0.8\), so \(n(t) = 0.8(1 - e^{-t/1.6})\). The conductance \(\propto n^4 = 0.41(1 - e^{-t/1.6})^4\) starts as \(t^4\) for small \(t\): all four gates must open, so the conductance has an initial delay and an S-shaped rise. This is exactly the delayed rise Hodgkin and Huxley observed for potassium, which led them to the fourth power.

(FitzHugh–Nagumo) For \(\dot v = v(v - a)(1 - v) - w + I\), \(\dot w = \varepsilon(v - \gamma w)\) with \(a = 0.1\), \(\gamma = 0.5\), \(\varepsilon = 0.01\): (a) find the fixed point and its stability for \(I = 0\); (b) find numerically the current at which repetitive firing starts; (c) explain the shape of the limit cycle.

Solution.

  1. With \(w = v/\gamma = 2v\): \(v(v - 0.1)(1 - v) = 2v\), whose only real root is \(v = 0\), so the fixed point is \((0, 0)\). The Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix}-a & -1\\ \varepsilon & -\varepsilon\gamma\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-0.1 & -1\\0.01 & -0.005\end{pmatrix}\), with trace \(-0.105\) and determinant \(0.0105\): stable. (b) Simulations show a stable rest state up to \(I \simeq 0.11\) and large periodic oscillations (\(v\) amplitude \(\simeq 1.2\)) for \(I \gtrsim 0.12\). (c) Because \(\varepsilon \ll 1\), the orbit alternates fast horizontal jumps between the outer branches of the cubic nullcline with slow drifts along them: a relaxation oscillation. The upstroke and downstroke are fast; the plateau and recovery are slow, like a real spike followed by refractoriness.

(Logic gates and XOR) Build McCulloch–Pitts units for OR and NAND. Show that no single unit computes XOR, and build XOR with a hidden layer.

Solution. OR: \(w = (1, 1)\), \(\theta = 0.5\). NAND: \(w = (-1, -1)\), \(\theta = -1.5\). XOR would need \(0 < \theta\) (input \(00 \to 0\)), \(w_1 > \theta\) and \(w_2 > \theta\) (inputs \(10\), \(01 \to 1\)), and \(w_1 + w_2 < \theta\) (input \(11 \to 0\)). Adding the middle two gives \(w_1 + w_2 > 2\theta > \theta\), a contradiction: XOR is not linearly separable. With a hidden layer, \(\text{XOR}(x_1, x_2) = \text{AND}(\text{OR}(x_1, x_2), \text{NAND}(x_1, x_2))\): two hidden units (OR, NAND) and an output AND unit with \(w = (1, 1)\), \(\theta = 1.5\).

(Perceptron learning) Train a perceptron on AND with \(\eta = 1\), starting from \(\mathbf w = 0\), \(\theta = 0\), presenting \(00, 01, 10, 11\) cyclically, with the convention \(\Theta(0) = 0\). How many epochs does it take?

Solution. Epoch 1: \(00\): \(y = \Theta(0) = 0 = t\). \(01\): correct. \(10\): correct. \(11\): \(y = 0\), \(t = 1\), so \(\mathbf w = (1, 1)\), \(\theta = -1\). Epoch 2: \(00\): \(\Theta(0 + 1) = 1 \neq 0\), so \(\theta = 0\). \(01\): \(\Theta(1) = 1 \neq 0\), so \(\mathbf w = (1, 0)\), \(\theta = 1\). \(10\): \(\Theta(1 - 1) = 0\), correct. \(11\): \(\Theta(1 - 1) = 0 \neq 1\), so \(\mathbf w = (2, 1)\), \(\theta = 0\). Epoch 3: \(00\) correct; \(01\): \(\Theta(1) = 1\), error, so \(\mathbf w = (2, 0)\), \(\theta = 1\); \(10\): \(\Theta(1) = 1\), error, so \(\mathbf w = (1, 0)\), \(\theta = 2\); \(11\): \(\Theta(-1) = 0\), error, so \(\mathbf w = (2, 1)\), \(\theta = 1\). Epoch 4: \(00\): \(0\) ✓; \(01\): \(\Theta(0) = 0\) ✓; \(10\): \(\Theta(1) = 1\), error, so \(\mathbf w = (1, 1)\), \(\theta = 2\); \(11\): \(\Theta(0) = 0\), error, so \(\mathbf w = (2, 2)\), \(\theta = 1\). Epoch 5: \(01\): \(\Theta(1) = 1\), error, so \(\mathbf w = (2, 1)\), \(\theta = 2\); \(10\): \(\Theta(0) = 0\) ✓; \(11\): \(\Theta(1) = 1\) ✓. Epoch 6: all four are correct (\(00 \to 0\), \(01 \to \Theta(-1)\), \(10 \to \Theta(0)\), \(11 \to \Theta(1)\)). The weights stop changing after five epochs with errors (the sixth epoch is error-free), converging to \(\mathbf w = (2, 1)\), \(\theta = 2\), as guaranteed by the convergence theorem since AND is linearly separable.

with $g_{\text{inp}} = \sum_Xg_X$ the input conductance and $I$ an injected current.

**Voltage clamp.** A feedback amplifier injects whatever current is needed to hold $V$ at a command value $V_s$; the injected current then measures the total ionic current at that voltage. With constant conductances the response to a current step is the charging of an RC circuit,
$$V(t) = V_s + (V_c - V_s)e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g_{\text{inp}}},$$
and the steady current gives the linear **I–V relation**, whose slope is $g_{\text{inp}}$. Measured I–V curves are nonlinear because the conductances depend on $V$. Selectively blocking channels with drugs (tetrodotoxin for $\text{Na}^+$, tetraethylammonium for $\text{K}^+$) isolates each contribution.

For a time-dependent current, with $V$ measured from rest, the solution of $\tau\dot V = -V + RI(t)$ ($R = 1/g_{\text{inp}}$) is a convolution:
$$V(t) = \frac R\tau\int_{-\infty}^te^{-(t - s)/\tau}I(s)\,ds,$$
i.e. the membrane is a **low-pass filter** with time constant $\tau \sim 10$ ms.

## The integrate-and-fire model

Lapicque's (1907) **leaky integrate-and-fire** (LIF) neuron keeps only the subthreshold RC dynamics and replaces the spike by a rule:
$$\tau\frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - V_{\text{rest}}) + RI, \qquad V(t^-) = V_{\text{th}} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{spike, } V(t^+) = V_{\text{reset}}.$$
For a constant current, starting from $V_{\text{reset}} = V_{\text{rest}}$, $V(t) = V_{\text{rest}} + RI(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. The threshold is reached at $t_{\text{sp}}$ such that $RI(1 - e^{-t_{\text{sp}}/\tau}) = V_{\text{th}} - V_{\text{rest}} \equiv \Delta$. The firing rate is
$$r = \frac{1}{t_{\text{sp}}} = \Bigl[\tau\ln\frac{RI}{RI - \Delta}\Bigr]^{-1}\Theta(RI - \Delta) \;\underset{RI \gg \Delta}{\simeq}\; \frac{RI - \Delta/2}{\tau\Delta}.$$
There is a current threshold $RI = \Delta$ (the rheobase) below which the neuron never fires. Above it the rate rises steeply, then linearly in $I$ (the f–I curve). With $\tau = 10$ ms and $\Delta = 16$ mV, $RI = 18, 20, 25, 40$ mV gives $46$, $62$, $98$, $196$ Hz. An absolute refractory period $\tau_{\text{ref}}$ adds to $t_{\text{sp}}$ and caps the rate at $1/\tau_{\text{ref}}$.

Real neurons are not perfect integrators. The threshold is not sharp: the same current pulse sometimes produces a spike and sometimes not. And some neurons are **resonators**: they fire preferentially to inputs at a particular frequency, and can even fire after an *inhibitory* burst (post-inhibitory rebound). These behaviours require the dynamics of the ion channels.

### Spike-rate adaptation

Many neurons fire fast at the onset of a constant current and then slow down. A potassium conductance $g_{\text{sra}}$ that increases by $\Delta g_{\text{sra}}$ at each spike and decays with a time constant $\tau_{\text{sra}}$ of $\sim 100$ ms reproduces this:
$$\tau\frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - V_{\text{rest}}) - r_mg_{\text{sra}}(V - V_{\text{K}}) + RI, \qquad \tau_{\text{sra}}\frac{dg_{\text{sra}}}{dt} = -g_{\text{sra}} + \tau_{\text{sra}}\Delta g_{\text{sra}}\sum_i\delta(t - t_i).$$
Each spike makes the next harder to trigger, so interspike intervals lengthen until a steady state is reached.

## Voltage-dependent conductances: Hodgkin–Huxley

### Gating variables

A channel contains $q$ independent gates, each open with probability $n$. The channel conducts only if all gates are open, with probability $n^q$, so the conductance per unit area is $g = \bar g\,n^q$, with $\bar g$ the maximal conductance. Each gate opens at rate $\alpha_n(V)$ and closes at rate $\beta_n(V)$, a two-state Markov process whose mean obeys
$$\frac{dn}{dt} = \alpha_n(V)(1 - n) - \beta_n(V)\,n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau_n(V)\frac{dn}{dt} = n_\infty(V) - n,$$
with $\tau_n = 1/(\alpha_n + \beta_n)$ and $n_\infty = \alpha_n\tau_n$. The rates follow from the Boltzmann factors of the gate's conformational change, $\alpha, \beta \propto \exp(\pm qBV/k_BT)$, which makes $n_\infty(V)$ a sigmoid. **Persistent** conductances (like the delayed-rectifier $\text{K}^+$, $q = 4$) only activate. **Transient** conductances (like $\text{Na}^+$) have activation gates $m$ that open with depolarisation and an inactivation gate $h$ that closes with depolarisation: $g_{\text{Na}} = \bar g_{\text{Na}}m^3h$.

### The Hodgkin–Huxley model

From voltage-clamp experiments on the squid giant axon, Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) obtained
$$C_m\frac{dV}{dt} = -\bar g_{\text{K}}n^4(V - V_{\text{K}}) - \bar g_{\text{Na}}m^3h(V - V_{\text{Na}}) - \bar g_L(V - V_L) + I,$$
$$\tau_w(V)\frac{dw}{dt} = w_\infty(V) - w, \qquad w = n, m, h .$$
The action potential unfolds as follows. A depolarisation opens the fast $m$ gates. Sodium flows in and depolarises further (positive feedback, the upstroke toward $V_{\text{Na}}$). On a slower time scale $h$ closes, inactivating sodium, and $n$ opens, letting potassium out (repolarisation and an undershoot toward $V_{\text{K}}$, the after-hyperpolarisation). While $h$ recovers and $n$ closes, the neuron is refractory. The model reproduces the shape and threshold of the spike, refractoriness and propagation along the axon. It earned the 1963 Nobel Prize, but four nonlinear equations per neuron are hard to analyse and expensive to simulate in large networks.

### The FitzHugh–Nagumo reduction

Two observations simplify HH. $m$ is much faster than the other variables, so $m \simeq m_\infty(V)$. And $h$ and $n$ evolve on similar time scales and are roughly linearly related, so one variable can stand for both. The result is a fast **excitation** variable $v$ (the membrane potential) and a slow **recovery** variable $w$:
$$\frac{dv}{dt} = v(v - a)(1 - v) - w + I, \qquad \frac{dw}{dt} = \varepsilon(v - \gamma w), \qquad \varepsilon \ll 1 .$$
The cubic $v$-nullcline and the linear $w$-nullcline organise the phase plane. For small $I$ they cross on the left branch of the cubic: a stable rest state, from which a small perturbation decays but a perturbation beyond the middle branch triggers a large excursion (a spike) before returning. The neuron is **excitable**. As $I$ increases, the crossing moves to the middle branch, the rest state loses stability through a Hopf bifurcation, and the system settles on a **relaxation limit cycle**: periodic spiking. With $a = 0.1$, $\varepsilon = 0.01$, $\gamma = 0.5$, the rest state is stable up to $I \simeq 0.11$ and the model spikes periodically (with $v$ swinging between about $-0.28$ and $1$) beyond it.

Neurons are dynamical systems, and the bifurcation through which they start firing determines their computational class (Izhikevich). A saddle-node on an invariant circle gives integrators, which can fire at arbitrarily low rates (class 1). A Hopf bifurcation gives resonators with a non-zero minimum frequency (class 2), able to show post-inhibitory rebound.

## Artificial neurons

### McCulloch–Pitts neurons

McCulloch and Pitts (1943) abstracted the neuron into a threshold unit: inputs $\sigma_j \in \{0, 1\}$ are weighted and summed, and the output is
$$\sigma_{\text{out}} = \Theta\Bigl(\sum_jw_j\sigma_j - \theta\Bigr).$$
With suitable weights and thresholds a unit implements logic gates: NOT ($w = -1$, $\theta = -1/2$), AND ($w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $\theta = 3/2$), OR ($w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $\theta = 1/2$). Networks of such units can compute any Boolean function. Replacing $\Theta$ by smooth or piecewise-linear **activation functions** (sigmoid $1/(1 + e^{-x})$, $\tanh$, ReLU $\max(0, x)$, leaky ReLU, ELU, maxout) gives the units of modern artificial neural networks.

### The perceptron

Rosenblatt's **perceptron** (1958) is a threshold unit that *learns* its weights from labelled examples $(\boldsymbol\sigma^\mu, t^\mu)$:
$$y = \Theta(\mathbf w\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma - \theta), \qquad \Delta\mathbf w = \eta\,(t^\mu - y^\mu)\,\boldsymbol\sigma^\mu, \quad \Delta\theta = -\eta(t^\mu - y^\mu).$$
Weights change only when the unit errs, moving the decision hyperplane toward the misclassified example. The **perceptron convergence theorem** guarantees that the algorithm finds a separating hyperplane in a finite number of steps whenever one exists. The perceptron is a linear classifier, so it fails on problems that are not linearly separable, the simplest being XOR (Minsky and Papert, 1969). Solving XOR needs a hidden layer, and multi-layer networks trained by backpropagation descend from this observation.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
**(Nernst and resting potentials)** (a) Compute $V_{\text{K}}$ and $V_{\text{Na}}$ at $37\,^\circ$C for the concentrations in the text. (b) Find $V_{\text{rest}}$ for $g_{\text{Na}}/g_{\text{K}} = 0.05$. (c) What happens to $V_{\text{rest}}$ if extracellular $\text{K}^+$ rises to $20$ mM?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $RT/F = 26.7$ mV at $310$ K: $V_{\text{K}} = 26.7\ln(5/140) = -89.0$ mV, $V_{\text{Na}} = 26.7\ln(140/12) = +65.6$ mV. (b) $V_{\text{rest}} = (-89.0 + 0.05\times65.6)/1.05 = -81.6$ mV. (c) $V_{\text{K}} = 26.7\ln(20/140) = -52.0$ mV and $V_{\text{rest}} = (-52.0 + 3.3)/1.05 = -46.4$ mV. The cell depolarises by about $35$ mV, enough to inactivate sodium channels and block firing. This is why the potassium concentration in the blood is tightly regulated (hyperkalaemia is dangerous for heart and nerves).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(f–I curve of the LIF neuron)** Derive the firing rate of the LIF neuron for constant current, including an absolute refractory period $\tau_{\text{ref}}$. Evaluate it for $\tau = 10$ ms, $\Delta = 16$ mV, $RI = 20$ mV, and $\tau_{\text{ref}} = 0$ or $2$ ms. Derive the large-current approximation.
:::

::: {.solution}
$r = [\tau_{\text{ref}} + \tau\ln\frac{RI}{RI - \Delta}]^{-1}$ for $RI > \Delta$, else $0$. For $RI = 20$ mV: $\tau\ln5 = 16.1$ ms, so $r = 62$ Hz without refractoriness and $1/18.1\,\text{ms} = 55$ Hz with $2$ ms. For $RI \gg \Delta$, $\ln\frac{1}{1 - x} = x + x^2/2 + \dots$ with $x = \Delta/RI$ gives $t_{\text{sp}} \simeq \frac{\tau\Delta}{RI}(1 + \frac{\Delta}{2RI})$, so $r \simeq \frac{RI - \Delta/2}{\tau\Delta}$: linear in $I$ with slope $1/(\tau\Delta)$. At $RI = 40$ mV this gives $200$ Hz against the exact $196$ Hz. Without the $-\Delta/2$ correction the approximation would give $150$ Hz.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Gating kinetics)** A gate with $\alpha_n = 0.5\,\text{ms}^{-1}$ and $\beta_n = 0.125\,\text{ms}^{-1}$ at a given voltage starts closed ($n = 0$). Find $n(t)$ and the potassium conductance $\bar g_{\text{K}}n^4$. Why does the conductance rise with a delay (sigmoidally) while $n$ rises exponentially?
:::

::: {.solution}
$\tau_n = 1/(0.625) = 1.6$ ms and $n_\infty = 0.5\times1.6 = 0.8$, so $n(t) = 0.8(1 - e^{-t/1.6})$. The conductance $\propto n^4 = 0.41(1 - e^{-t/1.6})^4$ starts as $t^4$ for small $t$: all four gates must open, so the conductance has an initial delay and an S-shaped rise. This is exactly the delayed rise Hodgkin and Huxley observed for potassium, which led them to the fourth power.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(FitzHugh–Nagumo)** For $\dot v = v(v - a)(1 - v) - w + I$, $\dot w = \varepsilon(v - \gamma w)$ with $a = 0.1$, $\gamma = 0.5$, $\varepsilon = 0.01$: (a) find the fixed point and its stability for $I = 0$; (b) find numerically the current at which repetitive firing starts; (c) explain the shape of the limit cycle.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) With $w = v/\gamma = 2v$: $v(v - 0.1)(1 - v) = 2v$, whose only real root is $v = 0$, so the fixed point is $(0, 0)$. The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}-a & -1\\ \varepsilon & -\varepsilon\gamma\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-0.1 & -1\\0.01 & -0.005\end{pmatrix}$, with trace $-0.105$ and determinant $0.0105$: stable. (b) Simulations show a stable rest state up to $I \simeq 0.11$ and large periodic oscillations ($v$ amplitude $\simeq 1.2$) for $I \gtrsim 0.12$. (c) Because $\varepsilon \ll 1$, the orbit alternates fast horizontal jumps between the outer branches of the cubic nullcline with slow drifts along them: a **relaxation oscillation**. The upstroke and downstroke are fast; the plateau and recovery are slow, like a real spike followed by refractoriness.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Logic gates and XOR)** Build McCulloch–Pitts units for OR and NAND. Show that no single unit computes XOR, and build XOR with a hidden layer.
:::

::: {.solution}
OR: $w = (1, 1)$, $\theta = 0.5$. NAND: $w = (-1, -1)$, $\theta = -1.5$. XOR would need $0 < \theta$ (input $00 \to 0$), $w_1 > \theta$ and $w_2 > \theta$ (inputs $10$, $01 \to 1$), and $w_1 + w_2 < \theta$ (input $11 \to 0$). Adding the middle two gives $w_1 + w_2 > 2\theta > \theta$, a contradiction: XOR is not linearly separable. With a hidden layer, $\text{XOR}(x_1, x_2) = \text{AND}(\text{OR}(x_1, x_2), \text{NAND}(x_1, x_2))$: two hidden units (OR, NAND) and an output AND unit with $w = (1, 1)$, $\theta = 1.5$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Perceptron learning)** Train a perceptron on AND with $\eta = 1$, starting from $\mathbf w = 0$, $\theta = 0$, presenting $00, 01, 10, 11$ cyclically, with the convention $\Theta(0) = 0$. How many epochs does it take?
:::

::: {.solution}
Epoch 1: $00$: $y = \Theta(0) = 0 = t$. $01$: correct. $10$: correct. $11$: $y = 0$, $t = 1$, so $\mathbf w = (1, 1)$, $\theta = -1$. Epoch 2: $00$: $\Theta(0 + 1) = 1 \neq 0$, so $\theta = 0$. $01$: $\Theta(1) = 1 \neq 0$, so $\mathbf w = (1, 0)$, $\theta = 1$. $10$: $\Theta(1 - 1) = 0$, correct. $11$: $\Theta(1 - 1) = 0 \neq 1$, so $\mathbf w = (2, 1)$, $\theta = 0$. Epoch 3: $00$ correct; $01$: $\Theta(1) = 1$, error, so $\mathbf w = (2, 0)$, $\theta = 1$; $10$: $\Theta(1) = 1$, error, so $\mathbf w = (1, 0)$, $\theta = 2$; $11$: $\Theta(-1) = 0$, error, so $\mathbf w = (2, 1)$, $\theta = 1$. Epoch 4: $00$: $0$ ✓; $01$: $\Theta(0) = 0$ ✓; $10$: $\Theta(1) = 1$, error, so $\mathbf w = (1, 1)$, $\theta = 2$; $11$: $\Theta(0) = 0$, error, so $\mathbf w = (2, 2)$, $\theta = 1$. Epoch 5: $01$: $\Theta(1) = 1$, error, so $\mathbf w = (2, 1)$, $\theta = 2$; $10$: $\Theta(0) = 0$ ✓; $11$: $\Theta(1) = 1$ ✓. Epoch 6: all four are correct ($00 \to 0$, $01 \to \Theta(-1)$, $10 \to \Theta(0)$, $11 \to \Theta(1)$). The weights stop changing after five epochs with errors (the sixth epoch is error-free), converging to $\mathbf w = (2, 1)$, $\theta = 2$, as guaranteed by the convergence theorem since AND is linearly separable.
:::
