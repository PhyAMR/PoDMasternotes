4  The binomial model

The simplest self-consistent framework in derivative pricing is the Cox–Ross–Rubinstein (CRR) binomial tree. It compresses the entire logic of no-arbitrage pricing into a discrete-time discrete-space model where every step can be checked by hand — and yet in the appropriate limit it recovers Black–Scholes. This chapter derives the one-step model, extends it to \(n\) steps, and shows how it converges to continuous time.

4.1 One-step binomial model

A one-step binomial market has:

  • One risky asset with initial price \(S_0\) and time-\(T\) price \(S_T \in \{S_0 u, S_0 d\}\), where \(u > 1 > d > 0\).
  • One risk-free asset (the money-market account) with price \(B_t = e^{rt}\) (so \(B_0 = 1\) and \(B_T = e^{rT}\)).
  • A physical probability \(p_u = P(S_T = S_0 u)\) of the “up” state; \(p_d = 1 - p_u\).

The market is arbitrage-free iff \(d < e^{rT} < u\).

Proof. If \(e^{rT} \leq d\), borrow \(S_0\) and buy the stock: at \(T\) pay back \(S_0 e^{rT} \leq S_0 d \leq S_T\), keep a non-negative surplus with strictly positive probability — arbitrage. Symmetrically for \(e^{rT} \geq u\). Both inequalities together rule out sign-definite outcomes.

4.2 Replicating a payoff

Suppose we want to price a derivative with payoff \(V_T = f(S_T) \in \{f(S_0 u), f(S_0 d)\} = \{V_u, V_d\}\).

There exist unique \(\Delta \in \mathbb R\) and \(\beta \in \mathbb R\) such that the portfolio “\(\Delta\) shares of \(S\) + \(\beta\) units of bond” matches the payoff in both states: \[\Delta = \frac{V_u - V_d}{S_0(u - d)}, \qquad \beta = \frac{u V_d - d V_u}{u - d}\,e^{-rT}.\] Hence the no-arbitrage price of the derivative at time \(0\) is \[V_0 = \Delta S_0 + \beta = e^{-rT}\bigl[q V_u + (1 - q)V_d\bigr], \qquad q = \frac{e^{rT} - d}{u - d} \in (0, 1).\]

Remark (Physical meaning). The quantity \(q\) has no relation to the physical probability \(p_u\); it is a synthetic weight determined entirely by \(r\), \(u\), \(d\). It is called the risk-neutral probability of the up state, and \(V_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[V_T]\) where \(\mathbb Q\) is the measure that assigns probability \(q\) to the up state.

4.3 Risk-neutral valuation

The one-step result generalises to any dimension and any tree structure:

A finite-tree market is arbitrage-free iff there exists a probability measure \(\mathbb Q\) (equivalent to \(\mathbb P\)) under which the discounted asset price \(\tilde S_t = e^{-rt}S_t\) is a martingale: \[\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[\tilde S_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t] = \tilde S_t .\] The market is complete — every payoff can be replicated — iff \(\mathbb Q\) is unique.

In an arbitrage-free complete market, the no-arbitrage price at time \(t\) of a derivative with payoff \(V_T\) is \[V_t = e^{-r(T - t)}\,\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[V_T \mid \mathcal F_t] .\]

4.4 Multi-step binomial model

Iterate the one-step model \(n\) times. The tree has \(2^n\) paths but only \(n + 1\) terminal nodes (the number of up moves determines \(S_T\)). At each internal node the local replication argument applies with the same \((u, d, r)\), so backwards induction gives the price at every node.

For a European payoff \(V_T = f(S_T)\) in an \(n\)-step CRR tree with step size \(\Delta t\), \[V_0 = e^{-rT} \sum_{k = 0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}q^k(1 - q)^{n - k}\,f\bigl(S_0 u^k d^{n - k}\bigr),\] where \(q = (e^{r \Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) is the one-step risk-neutral probability.

4.5 American options and the free-boundary problem

For an American option the holder can exercise early. Pricing requires backwards induction with a comparison at each node between the immediate exercise value and the continuation value: \[V(S, t) = \max\bigl(f(S),\ e^{-r\Delta t}[q V^u + (1 - q)V^d]\bigr).\]

For an American put on a non-dividend stock with strike \(K\): as spot falls below a stochastic early-exercise boundary \(B(t)\), immediate exercise beats holding. The boundary shrinks toward \(K\) as \(t \to T\) (holding until maturity becomes safer as time runs out) and away from \(K\) at \(t = 0\). The exact form requires solving a free-boundary PDE; the binomial tree is the standard numerical method.

Remark. An American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early — you always prefer the time value of the strike (invest \(K\) at rate \(r\)) plus the option to walk away. This is Merton’s classical no-early-exercise theorem.

4.6 Convergence to Black–Scholes

Take the step size \(\Delta t = T/n \to 0\) and calibrate \[u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} = 1/u.\] Then the log return per step has variance \(\sigma^2 \Delta t\), so the cumulative log return \(\ln(S_T/S_0)\) has variance \(\sigma^2 T\) in the limit — matching the Black–Scholes model.

Let \(C_0^{(n)}\) denote the CRR call price with \(n\) steps as above. Then \[\lim_{n \to \infty} C_0^{(n)} = S_0\,\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT}\,\Phi(d_2),\] the Black–Scholes formula (Chapter 5), where \(\Phi\) is the standard-normal CDF.

Proof idea. Invoke the central limit theorem for the \(n\)-step binomial: the log price converges in distribution to a Brownian motion with drift \(r - \sigma^2/2\) and diffusion \(\sigma\). Discounted-expectation of \((S_T - K)^+\) under the resulting log-normal distribution gives the BS formula.

4.7 Exercises

A one-step binomial model has \(S_0 = 100\), \(u = 1.2\), \(d = 0.9\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\) year. Price a European call with strike \(K = 100\).

Solution. Risk-neutral probability: \(q = (e^{0.05} - 0.9)/(1.2 - 0.9) \approx 0.5085\). Payoffs: \(V_u = 20\), \(V_d = 0\). Price: \(V_0 = e^{-0.05}(0.5085 \cdot 20 + 0.4915 \cdot 0) \approx 9.67\). Delta: \(\Delta = 20/(100 \cdot 0.3) \approx 0.667\).

Show that for the CRR calibration \(u = 1/d\), the risk-neutral probability converges to \(1/2\) as \(\Delta t \to 0\) if the drift under \(\mathbb Q\) is zero. Comment on the implication.

Solution. \(q = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\). Taylor-expanding: \(e^{r\Delta t} \approx 1 + r\Delta t\), \(u \approx 1 + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} + \sigma^2 \Delta t/2\), \(d \approx 1 - \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} + \sigma^2\Delta t/2\). Leading order: \(q \approx 1/2 + (r - \sigma^2/2)\sqrt{\Delta t}/(2\sigma)\). As \(\Delta t \to 0\) we recover \(q = 1/2\); the drift correction lives in higher-order terms.

4.8 Callouts

The binomial model is finite — no measure theory needed to state the fundamental theorem of asset pricing. This is why it is taught first: all the abstractions of Chapter 4 have their finite analogues here.

For pricing an American option numerically, the binomial tree is very hard to beat: it is trivial to code, easy to debug, and (with an appropriate number of steps) accurate to two decimal places. Only for high-dimensional or path-dependent options do more advanced techniques win.

The convergence of CRR to BS is \(O(1/n)\) but oscillatory: prices with different parities of \(n\) approach BS from opposite sides. Averaging two adjacent \(n\) values (Richardson extrapolation) gives \(O(1/n^2)\) convergence.

Risk-neutral probability is a mathematical trick, not a prediction of what will happen. Do not tell a client that “the market thinks the up move has probability \(q\)”.
