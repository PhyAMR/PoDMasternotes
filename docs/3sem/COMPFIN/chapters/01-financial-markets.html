2  Financial markets

Before we start pricing derivatives it is worth understanding the setting in which they trade. This chapter is short on mathematics and long on institutional vocabulary: the players, the instruments, the sources of risk, and the language (“bid–ask”, “arbitrage”, “hedging”) that the rest of the course assumes.

2.1 What is a financial market?

A financial market is an organised venue where economic agents exchange contingent claims — contracts whose payoff depends on future values of certain observable random variables (prices, rates, credit events). The market’s function is to allocate risk and capital across time and across agents.

The venue can be centralised (a stock exchange), electronically distributed (an ECN), or bilateral over-the-counter (OTC). What all markets share is a mechanism for matching buyers to sellers and a settlement infrastructure that guarantees delivery.

2.2 Main sources of risk

Financial risk factors are traditionally decomposed into:

  • Market risk — losses from changes in market variables (equity prices, FX rates, interest rates, commodity prices).
  • Credit risk — losses from counterparty default or from a credit rating downgrade.
  • Liquidity risk — losses from being unable to close a position at fair value.
  • Operational risk — losses from failures in processes, people, or systems.
  • Model risk — losses from a mathematical model being wrong.

Remark. The 2007–2008 crisis was in essence a compound event: model risk (mispriced CDOs) triggered credit risk (mortgage defaults) which cascaded via liquidity risk (frozen interbank markets). Modern risk management treats these categories as strongly coupled, not orthogonal.

2.3 Buyers, sellers, and market makers

Every trade has three natural roles:

  • Buyers and sellers — agents with directional views or hedging needs.
  • Market makers — dealers who continuously post two-sided quotes (bid and ask) and profit from the spread.
  • Clearing houses — central counterparties that step between buyer and seller after the trade to mutualise counterparty risk.

For a highly liquid pair (EUR/USD) the bid–ask spread quoted to a professional counterparty is \(\sim 0.1\) pip on a \(\$1\)M ticket, i.e. transaction cost \(\sim \$10\). For a less liquid pair (USD/TRY at 3 a.m.) the spread might be \(10\) pips, i.e. \(\$1000\). The spread is the price of immediate execution: patient traders can post limit orders at mid and hope to get filled.

2.4 Asset classes

A financial market is typically split into asset classes, each with its own conventions and risk factors:

  • Equities — stocks and stock indices.
  • Fixed income — bonds and interest-rate derivatives.
  • Foreign exchange (FX) — currency spot and forwards.
  • Commodities — physical commodities and their futures.
  • Credit — CDS and cash bonds referencing corporate or sovereign issuers.

Each has its own pricing conventions (yield vs price for bonds, forward points for FX, basis quotes for commodities), but the mathematical machinery — Itô calculus, risk-neutral valuation — is universal.

2.5 The time value of money

The single most important simple idea in finance:

The discount factor \(P(t, T)\) is the value at time \(t\) of receiving 1 unit of currency with certainty at future time \(T\). In a deterministic-rate world with continuously compounded rate \(r\), \[P(t, T) = e^{-r(T - t)}.\]

The present value at time \(0\) of a deterministic cash flow \((c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n)\) paid at times \((t_1, \ldots, t_n)\) is \[PV_0 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\,P(0, t_i).\]

2.6 Arbitrage

The concept that structures every derivative-pricing argument in the rest of the course:

An arbitrage is a self-financing trading strategy \(H\) such that the initial portfolio value is zero, the final value is non-negative with probability one, and strictly positive with positive probability: \[V_0^H = 0,\quad V_T^H \geq 0 \text{ a.s.},\quad P(V_T^H > 0) > 0.\]

In a well-functioning market, arbitrages cannot exist — or if they do, they are exploited so quickly by traders that they disappear on timescales below the observation resolution. Pricing models are required to be arbitrage-free: their prices must satisfy consistency relations that rule out riskless profit.

Consider an equity \(S\) paying no dividends and a forward contract \(F\) maturing at \(T\) with strike \(K\). If \(K < S_0 e^{rT}\), buy the forward, short-sell the equity, invest the proceeds at rate \(r\), and at \(T\) take delivery of the forward using the invested cash. Net cash flow: \(S_0 e^{rT} - K > 0\) — arbitrage. Absence of arbitrage forces the forward price to equal \(F = S_0 e^{rT}\).

2.7 Hedging

Hedging is the practice of offsetting the risk of one position with another instrument whose payoff moves in the opposite direction. A perfect hedge produces a portfolio whose value is deterministic (zero variance) over some time horizon.

Hedging turns out to be the dual of pricing: as we will see in Chapter 5, the Black–Scholes price of a European option is exactly the cost of the self-financing portfolio that perfectly replicates its payoff at maturity.

2.8 Regulated versus OTC markets

Most equity trading happens on regulated exchanges; most FX, credit and interest-rate derivative trading historically happened over-the-counter, bilaterally. After 2008 the trend has been strongly toward central clearing and standardisation:

Remark. A CDS on a major sovereign now clears through ICE Clear Credit; a standardised interest-rate swap in the major currencies clears through LCH. Central clearing greatly reduces counterparty risk but concentrates it in the clearing house — a new form of systemic risk that regulators watch closely.

2.9 Exercises

A stock is trading at \(S_0 = 100\). The 3-month risk-free rate is \(r = 5\%\) (continuously compounded). What is the arbitrage-free forward price for delivery in 3 months?

Solution. \(F = S_0 e^{rT} = 100 \cdot e^{0.05 \cdot 0.25} \approx 101.26\).

The stock pays a discrete dividend \(D\) two months from now. How does the forward price change?

Solution. \(F = (S_0 - PV(D))e^{rT} = (S_0 - D e^{-rT_D})e^{rT}\) where \(T_D = 2/12\). The forward is lower because the buyer of the forward does not receive the dividend, so its value must reflect the missed cash flow.

Two zero-coupon bonds trade in the market: one maturing in 1 year at price \(B_1 = 98\) and one in 2 years at \(B_2 = 95\). What are the implied 1-year and 2-year spot rates?

Solution. \(r_1 = -\ln(B_1/100) = -\ln 0.98 \approx 2.02\%\); \(r_2 = -\tfrac12\ln(B_2/100) = -\tfrac12\ln 0.95 \approx 2.56\%\). The upward-sloping curve implies the market expects rates to rise (or a positive term premium).

2.10 Callouts

The no-arbitrage principle is an idealisation. Real markets show occasional small arbitrages (basis mispricings, ETF NAV drift) that persist for milliseconds; they are exploited by high-frequency market-makers, not by us.

Whenever you’re stuck on a pricing question, ask: “what portfolio has the same payoff?” If you can build a replicating portfolio, its cost is the price.

Discounting is at the risk-free rate only for deterministically-known cash flows. For risky flows the correct discount rate depends on the risk aversion of the investor and, in the risk-neutral world (Chapter 3), on the choice of numéraire.
