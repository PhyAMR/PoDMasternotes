10  FX options and automated market making

The previous chapters priced derivatives at their arbitrage-free “fair value”. A bank that trades with clients does something different: it makes a market, quoting a price at which it buys and a higher one at which it sells, and manages the risk left on its book. This chapter, based on a practitioner lecture by Veronika Lunina, describes how an automated market-making system for FX options works: valuation on the implied volatility surface, spreading, skewing, hedging, dealer intervention and system design. A short review of FX option pricing comes first.

10.1 FX options in brief

An FX option is written on an exchange rate \(S_t\) (units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency; for EURUSD, USD per EUR). Holding foreign currency earns the foreign interest rate \(r_f\), which acts like a continuous dividend yield. The Black–Scholes argument of Chapter 5 then gives the Garman–Kohlhagen formula: \[C = S_te^{-r_f(T-t)}\Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-r_d(T-t)}\Phi(d_2), \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(S_t/K) + (r_d - r_f \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2)(T - t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T - t}},\] with forward rate \(F_t = S_te^{(r_d - r_f)(T-t)}\) (covered interest parity). The spot Delta is \(e^{-r_f(T-t)}\Phi(d_1)\) and the vega \(S_te^{-r_f(T-t)}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T-t}\).

Prices are quoted in volatility. In the vanilla market, the price of an option is a vol: the number to plug into the Black–Scholes (Garman–Kohlhagen) formula, together with spot, rates and maturity, to obtain the premium in currency. The FX implied volatility surface shows a smile: at-the-money options have lower implied volatility than in- and out-of-the-money ones. By convention FX smiles are quoted in terms of Delta rather than strike — at-the-money, 25-delta and 10-delta risk reversals (call vol minus put vol, measuring skew) and butterflies (average wing vol minus ATM vol, measuring convexity).

10.2 What is market making?

Market making means taking the opposite side of clients’ trades. A market maker does not take directional bets: it earns the bid–offer spread, selling at the offer and buying at the bid. Some asset classes are fully electronic (FX spot, cash equities); FX options are less liquid, with much volume still traded OTC and by “voice”. Because holding times are long in illiquid products, risk management is central.

Why automate? Quoting is time-consuming, and a human’s ability to quote while managing risk is limited. Quoting a £100m request takes as much effort as a £1m one, so a voice desk naturally neglects small trades. Automation lets a bank serve more clients and increase market share, providing liquidity on a scalable, 24/7 basis. It complements the trading desk; it does not replace it.

10.3 Anatomy of a market-making system

Clients reach the bank through multi-dealer platforms (Bloomberg, Tradeweb, Digital Vega) or the bank’s own single-dealer platform, via two channels: request for quote (RFQ) and continuous price streaming.

Figure: the front-end handles connectivity, market data and routing; the back-end prices; requests outside automatic limits go to a trader.

  • The front-end handles connectivity to external platforms, receives price requests, sources market information and passes it, with the desk’s settings, to the back-end; it displays results and books trades.
  • The back-end contains the pricing logic for a collection of deals (a structure) and returns the results. It is usually written in C++ or Java.

The back-end performs four steps:

  1. Valuation: the mid price of the structure and its Greeks (Delta, vega, rho, …);
  2. Spreading: bid and offer obtained by subtracting and adding a symmetric spread to the mid;
  3. Skewing: shifting the quote towards the side that reduces the book’s risk;
  4. Hedging: an FX option is always booked Delta-hedged — the Delta is either exchanged with the client or executed in the spot market.

10.4 Where is the mid?

The mid price is an abstraction: participants buy at offers and sell at bids, and the mid must be estimated. For vanillas it is read off the implied volatility surface: observed prices of the liquid points (ATM, risk reversals, butterflies at standard tenors) plus a model to interpolate between them give a mid vol for any strike and expiry. Determining the levels of the liquid points is called marking the surface; it is done by traders or automatically, for instance with a non-linear Kalman filter that updates the surface as new quotes and trades arrive. Exotic structures are valued with smile-consistent models (local-stochastic volatility, Chapter 7).

10.5 Spreading

Spreading decides how much to charge on top of the mid: too much and nobody trades; too little and the margin does not cover hedging costs and adverse moves. In practice:

  • the desk maintains a base ATM volatility spread term structure for each currency pair (a value per tenor);
  • multipliers widen or tighten it depending on market conditions (volatile periods, events), the instrument (strike, Delta, exotic features) and the counterparty (client tiering);
  • the spread includes the cost of hedging the trade (e.g. the bid–offer of the spot hedge);
  • for a structure of several deals, spreading accounts for the netting of risk between legs.

Since prices are quoted in vols, a spread of \(\delta\sigma\) vol points translates into premium through the vega: \(\text{premium spread} \approx \mathcal V\,\delta\sigma\).

Spot \(S = 1.10\), \(r_{USD} = 4\%\), \(r_{EUR} = 2.5\%\), 6-month at-the-money-forward call (\(K = F = 1.1083\)), mid vol \(8\%\). Garman–Kohlhagen gives a mid premium of \(0.02451\) USD per EUR, spot Delta \(0.505\) and vega \(0.00306\) USD per EUR per vol point. On a 10m EUR notional: mid premium \(245\,127\) USD, vega \(30\,633\) USD per vol point. Quoting \(7.85\%\) / \(8.15\%\) (a spread of \(0.15\) vol on each side) gives bid and offer premiums of \(240\,532\) and \(249\,722\) USD: about \(4\,600\) USD of margin per side. If the client buys, the bank books the option together with a Delta hedge: buying about 5.05m EUR spot.

10.6 Skewing

A market maker does not choose its trades: if client flow is one-sided, risk accumulates. Ideally the book stays “flat”. Risk can be reduced actively by executing hedges in the market — expensive, especially for vega in illiquid options — or passively by skewing: quoting tighter (closer to mid) on the side that would reduce the current risk, and wider on the side that would increase it. This raises the probability of favourable trades without paying to hedge.

Skewing needs a single quantitative measure of the book’s risk. For a Delta-hedged option book the main risks are vega (sensitivity to implied volatility, across tenors and strikes) and sensitivity to scheduled market events (central bank rate decisions, non-farm payrolls, …). With such a measure one computes the risk of the current portfolio, of the portfolio plus the trade and of the portfolio minus the trade, and makes the skew a function of these numbers: if buying the option from the client reduces risk, the bid moves up towards mid.

10.7 Dealability and dealer intervention

The system has auto-dealable limits on trade size, depending on the currency pair, the option’s Delta, the time of day, and whether an event is under way. Requests outside the limits are re-routed to a human trader, who sees the prices suggested by the system and accepts or modifies them. Requests are also re-routed when anomalies are detected in the input market data or in the system’s outputs.

10.8 System design

The system must be object-oriented and scalable: it supports different product types with possibly different spreading logic and is easy to extend to new products. Traders configure parameters that flow in as real-time inputs. Independent pieces of functionality are encapsulated in separate classes. Every price request, together with its market data and settings, can be serialised (saved as text) for testing and for investigating issues.

10.9 Glossary

term meaning
two-way price a bid and an offer
RFQ request for quote
widen / tighten show worse / better prices to a counterparty
spreading the charge added on top of fair value
skewing decreasing the spread on one side
margin P&L profit at inception from the spread charged
position P&L profit or loss after inception from market moves
smile hedge Delta computed with a smile model
Fenics hedge Black–Scholes Delta at the strike’s quoted vol
client tiering client groups with different spreads
client P&L attribution tracing how much was made or lost per client, to adjust pricing

The difference between a smile hedge and a Fenics (Black–Scholes) hedge is a direct application of Chapter 7: when the smile moves with spot, the true Delta of an option includes the effect of the vol change, \(\Delta_{\text{smile}} = \Delta_{BS} + \mathcal V\,\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}/\partial S\).

10.10 Exercises

Show that the Garman–Kohlhagen formula follows from Black–Scholes by treating the foreign currency as an asset paying a continuous dividend yield \(r_f\).

Solution. One unit of foreign currency deposited abroad grows to \(e^{r_fT}\) units, worth \(S_Te^{r_fT}\) in domestic currency. The traded asset \(X_t = S_te^{r_ft}\) pays no dividends and, under the domestic risk-neutral measure, grows at \(r_d\); so \(S\) has risk-neutral drift \(r_d - r_f\). Repeating the Black–Scholes computation with \(\mathbb E^Q[S_T] = S_te^{(r_d - r_f)(T-t)}\) replaces \(S_t\) by \(S_te^{-r_f(T-t)}\) in the formula.

A desk is short 2m USD of vega (per vol point) in 1-year EURUSD. A client asks for a two-way price on a 1-year option with 20k USD vega per vol point. With a base spread of 0.2 vol each side, how should the quote be skewed?

Solution. Buying the option from the client (the client sells) would reduce the short vega position: the bank should improve its bid, e.g. quoting the bid only 0.1 vol below mid and the offer 0.3 vol above. The margin per side is roughly vega × spread: 2k USD on the bid side, 6k USD on the offer side.

(Hull 19.10) A bank has sold \(1000\) seven-month European calls on the yen (each on one yen). Spot \(0.80\) US cents per yen, strike \(0.81\), \(r_{USD} = 8\%\), \(r_{JPY} = 5\%\), volatility \(15\%\). Compute the Delta, Gamma, vega, Theta and rho of the position and interpret them.

Solution. With Garman–Kohlhagen, \(d_1 = 0.1016\), and per option (prices in cents): \(\Delta = e^{-r_fT}\Phi(d_1) = 0.525\), \(\Gamma = 4.206\), \(\mathcal V = 0.2355\) (per unit of volatility), \(\Theta = -0.0399\) per year, \(\rho = 0.223\) (per unit of \(r_{USD}\)). The short position has: Delta \(-525\) (the position loses \(5.25\) cents if the yen rises by \(0.01\) cent; hedge by buying 525 yen); Gamma \(-4206\) (Delta falls by \(4.2\) for each \(0.001\)-cent rise); vega \(-235.5\), i.e. \(-2.36\) cents per volatility point; Theta \(+39.9\) cents per year, i.e. about \(0.11\) cent per day earned by time decay; rho \(-223\), i.e. \(-2.23\) cents per percentage point of the US rate. Short options are short Gamma and vega and long Theta.
