9  Risk-free rates in practice

Chapter 8 treated the risk-free curve as a given mathematical object. In practice it has to be built from market quotes, under precise conventions, and since 2023 from a new family of benchmarks. This chapter, based on a practitioner lecture by Vincenzo Russo (Assicurazioni Generali), covers market conventions, the transition from interbank offered rates (IBORs) to overnight risk-free rates (RFRs), the pricing of overnight indexed swaps, swaptions and caplets on backward-looking rates, the bootstrap of an RFR curve, and the use of risk-free curves in the insurance sector under Solvency II.

9.1 The fixed-income market

The fixed-income market trades debt securities that pay predetermined cash flows: government bonds, corporate and covered bonds, municipal bonds, securitised products (MBS, ABS), money-market instruments. Issuers are sovereigns and financial or non-financial corporates; investors (asset managers, insurers, banks, pension funds) seek income, capital preservation, diversification and liability matching. Bonds differ by maturity (money market below 1 year, short 1–3, intermediate 3–7, long beyond 7), currency, structure (fixed, floating, inflation-linked, callable or putable, securitised) and credit rating (AAA down to D).

Prices and yields move inversely. The total yield of a risky bond decomposes as \[\text{yield} = \text{risk-free rate} + \text{credit spread} + \text{liquidity premium},\] so the risk-free curve is the reference for everything: discounting cash flows (time value of money without credit risk), forwarding floating rates (forward rates implied by no arbitrage), risk measures (duration, DV01, convexity), the risk-neutral drift in option pricing, and, for insurers, the fair value of both assets and liabilities.

Risk-free proxies. No asset is truly risk-free. Proxies are short-term rates anchored by central banks — government bills of top-rated sovereigns, and the new overnight benchmarks €STR (euro), SOFR (US), SONIA (UK), SARON (Switzerland), TONA (Japan) — and the derivatives written on them: futures, overnight indexed swaps (OIS). Risk-free rates are nominal: real rate plus expected inflation.

9.2 Market conventions

Day counts. The year fraction \(\tau(t, T)\) between two dates depends on the day-count convention: ACT/360 (actual days divided by 360, standard for money markets), ACT/365, ACT/ACT, 30/360. For ACT/360, \(\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i) = (T_i - T_{i-1})/360\) with dates counted in days.

Business-day rules adjust payment dates falling on holidays: Following (next business day), Modified Following (next business day unless it crosses into the next month, then the previous one), Unadjusted.

Compounding. A ZCB price \(P(t, T)\) defines a spot rate in each convention:

compounding definition rate from price
simple \(P = \dfrac{1}{1 + \tau R_s}\) \(R_s = \dfrac{1}{\tau}\Bigl(\dfrac1P - 1\Bigr)\)
annual \(P = (1 + R_a)^{-\tau}\) \(R_a = P^{-1/\tau} - 1\)
\(k\) times a year \(P = \Bigl(1 + \dfrac{R_k}{k}\Bigr)^{-k\tau}\) \(R_k = k\bigl(P^{-1/(k\tau)} - 1\bigr)\)
continuous \(P = e^{-R_c\tau}\) \(R_c = -\dfrac{\log P}{\tau}\)

with \(\tau = \tau(t, T)\). The zero curve \(T \mapsto R_g(t, T)\) depends on the convention \(g\); the discount curve \(T \mapsto P(t, T)\) does not, and is decreasing from \(P(t, t) = 1\) when rates are positive.

Interpolation. Quotes exist only for some maturities, and are sparse or illiquid at the long end. Missing points are interpolated — linearly, by cubic splines, or with parametric families such as Nelson–Siegel. For par rates \(S_{T_{i-1}}, S_{T_i}\) quoted at consecutive maturities, linear interpolation gives, for \(T_{i-1} < T_h < T_i\), \[S_{T_h} = \frac{S_{T_{i-1}}(T_i - T_h) + S_{T_i}(T_h - T_{i-1})}{T_i - T_{i-1}} .\]

Bonds. A fixed-rate bond with coupon rate \(K\) and dates \(T_1, \dots, T_n\) is worth \[B_{fx}(t, T_n) = \sum_{i=1}^nP(t, T_i)\,\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)\,K + P(t, T_n).\] For a defaultable issuer, market practice discounts with the risk-free zero rate plus a zero spread \(S\): with annual compounding \(\bar P(t, T) = (1 + R_a(t, T) + S)^{-\tau(t,T)}\), and the bond price uses \(\bar P\) in place of \(P\).

9.3 From IBORs to RFRs

IBORs (Libor, Euribor) were for decades the benchmark for loans, notes and derivatives, and together with overnight rates (Fed Funds, EONIA) were treated as risk-free. During the 2007–2009 crisis the IBOR–overnight spread widened dramatically (Chapter 1), showing that IBORs contain bank credit and liquidity risk; IBOR quotes were also manipulated. Hence:

  • 2013–2014: the Financial Stability Board reviews the major benchmarks and recommends alternatives;
  • 2017: the UK FCA announces it will stop supporting Libor panels;
  • 2019: ISDA establishes fallbacks to transaction-based overnight RFRs;
  • Libor is discontinued (USD Libor on 30 June 2023), replaced by SOFR, SONIA, SARON, TONA; in the euro area €STR replaced EONIA, while Euribor survives in reformed, transaction-based form.

The two kinds of rates differ structurally:

  • IBORs are forward-looking term rates: quoted for tenors from one week to one year as simple rates, fixed at the start of the accrual period — a market expectation.
  • RFRs are backward-looking overnight rates: published daily, with no term structure of their own; the rate for a period is obtained by compounding the daily fixings in arrears, so it is known only at the end of the period.

Correspondingly, three pricing environments have succeeded one another in a single currency:

  1. Single curve, IBOR (before 2007): IBOR rates for both discounting and forwarding.
  2. Dual curve (after 2007): overnight rates (EONIA, Fed Funds, now €STR, SOFR) for discounting, IBOR for forwarding — still the standard for Euribor derivatives.
  3. Single curve, RFR, backward-looking: the RFR curve for both discounting and forwarding. This is the setting of the rest of the chapter.

9.4 Backward-looking rates

Let \(C(t) = \exp\bigl(\int_0^tr(u)\,du\bigr)\) be the cash account driven by the instantaneous risk-free rate (Chapter 8), so that \(P(t, T) = \mathbb E^Q\bigl[e^{-\int_t^Tr(u)\,du}\bigm|\mathcal F_t\bigr]\).

For an accrual period \([T_{i-1}, T_i)\) with \(d_i\) business days, daily fixings \(r(i, j)\) and day-count fractions \(\delta(i, j)\), \[R(T_{i-1}, T_i) = \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl[\prod_{j=1}^{d_i}\bigl(1 + r(i, j)\delta(i, j)\bigr) - 1\Bigr] \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl[\frac{C(T_i)}{C(T_{i-1})} - 1\Bigr],\] the continuous-time version being the limit of vanishing daily steps.

For \(t \leq T_{i-1}\), the expectation of \(R(T_{i-1}, T_i)\) under the \(T_i\)-forward measure (whose numeraire is \(P(\cdot, T_i)\)) is \[R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i) = \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl(\frac{P(t, T_{i-1})}{P(t, T_i)} - 1\Bigr),\] the same formula as the simple forward rate of the IBOR world. For \(T_{i-1} < t < T_i\) part of the rate is already known: \[R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i) = \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl(\frac{e^{\int_{T_{i-1}}^tr(u)\,du}}{P(t, T_i)} - 1\Bigr).\]

Proof. Under the \(T_i\)-forward measure, \(\mathbb E^{T_i}[X\mid\mathcal F_t] = \mathbb E^Q\bigl[e^{-\int_t^{T_i}r}X\bigm|\mathcal F_t\bigr]/P(t, T_i)\). With \(X = C(T_i)/C(T_{i-1})\), \(e^{-\int_t^{T_i}r}X = e^{-\int_t^{T_{i-1}}r}\), whose \(Q\)-expectation is \(P(t, T_{i-1})\). \(\square\)

9.4.1 Overnight indexed swaps

An OIS exchanges a fixed rate \(K\) against the compounded RFR. A payer OIS with floating dates \(T_1, \dots, T_n\), fixed dates \(T'_1, \dots, T'_m\) (same start and end), notional \(N\), valued at \(t < T_0\): \[OIS_R(t, T_0, T_n) = N\sum_{i=1}^nP(t, T_i)\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i) - N\sum_{j=1}^mP(t, T'_j)\tau(T'_{j-1}, T'_j)K .\] By the proposition, before the start this coincides with the single-curve Libor swap formula (and the floating leg telescopes to \(N(P(t, T_0) - P(t, T_n))\)). Once an accrual period has started, the known part of the compounded rate must be included: the first floating coupon becomes \(N\bigl(e^{\int_{T_0}^tr(u)\,du} - P(t, T_1)\bigr)\).

9.4.2 Swaptions

Assume the forward swap rate \(S_R(t, T_0, T_n)\) is a log-normal martingale under the swap (annuity) measure, with numeraire the annuity \(A(t) = \sum_jP(t, T'_j)\tau(T'_{j-1}, T'_j)\): \[\frac{dS_R(t, T_0, T_n)}{S_R(t, T_0, T_n)} = \sigma_S\,dW_t .\] Then the payer swaption follows Black’s (1976) formula \[PSWT_R(t) = N\,A(t)\bigl[S_R\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(S_R/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma_S^2(T_0 - t)}{\sigma_S\sqrt{T_0 - t}} .\] Swaption valuation is unaffected by whether the underlying floating rate is forward- or backward-looking: what matters is the swap rate at \(T_0\).

9.4.3 Caplets on backward-looking rates

A caplet on \([T_{i-1}, T_i]\) with strike \(K\) pays \(N\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)\bigl(R(T_{i-1}, T_i) - K\bigr)^+\) at \(T_i\). The underlying rate keeps accruing during the period, so its uncertainty is resolved only at \(T_i\). Lyashenko and Mercurio (2019) model the forward rate as a log-normal martingale under the (extended) \(T_i\)-forward measure, with a volatility that decays linearly during the accrual period: \[\frac{dR(t, T_{i-1}, T_i)}{R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i)} = \sigma_R\min\Bigl(\frac{(T_i - t)^+}{T_i - T_{i-1}}, 1\Bigr)dW_t .\] (The extended ZCB price — the bond maturing at \(T_i\), with proceeds rolled over at the RFR after \(T_i\) — keeps the numeraire defined for \(t > T_i\).) This gives a Black formula \[CPLT_R(t) = N\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)P(t, T_i)\bigl[R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i)\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(R/K) \pm \tfrac12\Sigma_R^2}{\Sigma_R},\] with total variance \[\Sigma_R^2 = \sigma_R^2\int_t^{T_i}\min\Bigl(\frac{(T_i - s)^+}{T_i - T_{i-1}}, 1\Bigr)^2ds = \sigma_R^2\Bigl[\max(T_{i-1} - t, 0) + \frac{(T_i - \max(T_{i-1}, t))^3}{3(T_i - T_{i-1})^2}\Bigr].\] Since a forward-looking caplet’s rate is fixed at \(T_{i-1}\) (variance \(\sigma^2(T_{i-1} - t)\)), \(\Sigma_R \geq \Sigma_L\): the backward-looking caplet carries the extra variance of the accrual period, a third of its length.

On the curve bootstrapped below, the at-the-money caplet on \([1, 2]\) (forward rate \(3.407\%\)) with \(\sigma = 20\%\) is worth \(25.5\) bp of notional if the rate is forward-looking (\(\Sigma_L = 0.2\sqrt1\)) and \(29.4\) bp if backward-looking (\(\Sigma_R = 0.2\sqrt{1 + 1/3} = 0.231\)).

The same structure holds in the one- and two-factor Hull–White models, where the caplet becomes an option on \(C(T_i)/C(T_{i-1})\) and \(\Sigma_R^2\) splits into the variance of the bond’s forward price up to \(T_{i-1}\) plus the extra variance accumulated over \([T_{i-1}, T_i]\).

9.5 Bootstrapping an RFR curve

RFR curves are stripped from OIS quoted at par (zero initial value), typically with annual payments on both legs. With spot start \(T_0 = 0\) and par rates \(S_i = S_R(0, 0, T_i)\), setting the OIS value to zero for each maturity in turn, \[P(0, T_1) = \frac{1}{1 + \tau_1S_1}, \qquad P(0, T_i) = \frac{1 - S_i\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tau_jP(0, T_j)}{1 + \tau_iS_i}, \quad i = 2, \dots, n,\] with \(\tau_j = \tau(T_{j-1}, T_j)\). (This follows from the par condition \(1 - P(0, T_i) = S_i\sum_{j\leq i}\tau_jP(0, T_j)\), the telescoped floating leg equal to the fixed leg.)

Par rates (annual payments, \(\tau = 1\)): 1y \(3.00\%\), 2y \(3.20\%\), 3y \(3.35\%\), 5y \(3.50\%\); the 4y rate is linearly interpolated, \(3.425\%\).

\(T\) par rate \(P(0, T)\) zero rate (annual) zero rate (cont.)
1 3.000% 0.97087 3.000% 2.956%
2 3.200% 0.93889 3.203% 3.153%
3 3.350% 0.90568 3.357% 3.302%
4 3.425% 0.87365 3.435% 3.377%
5 3.500% 0.84143 3.513% 3.453%

With an upward-sloping curve, zero rates lie slightly above par rates, and forward rates above both (the 1-year forward from 4 to 5 years is \(3.83\%\)).

In practice (e.g. SOFR on 30 September 2024) the procedure is the same with more instruments, real day counts, a few days’ spot lag and an interpolation scheme between pillars.

9.6 Risk-free rates in insurance: Solvency II

Under Solvency II, EU insurers compute their solvency ratio from a market-value balance sheet, valuing liabilities (technical provisions) and the Solvency Capital Requirement with a regulatory risk-free curve published by EIOPA:

  1. collect par rates of IRS (Euribor) or OIS (SARON, SONIA, SOFR, …) for the liquid maturities (up to about 20 years for the euro);
  2. subtract a credit risk adjustment (10 bp for Euribor-based curves, zero for RFR-based ones);
  3. bootstrap zero rates from the adjusted par rates;
  4. extrapolate beyond the last liquid point up to 150 years with the Smith–Wilson method, converging to an ultimate forward rate;
  5. optionally add a volatility adjustment or matching adjustment, which reflect the spread insurers earn on their assets.

Economic scenario generators. Life liabilities contain financial options and guarantees, profit-sharing rules, management actions and policyholder behaviour, so their best estimate (BEL) is computed by Monte Carlo projection of the balance sheet in an asset–liability model, over thousands of risk-neutral scenarios that start from the Solvency II curve. Typical rate models are the displaced LIBOR market model with stochastic volatility, the two-factor Hull–White model and the displaced Black–Karasinski model, calibrated to OIS (level) and swaptions (implied volatility). The IBOR→RFR transition did not change these models: OIS and OIS swaptions replace IRS and IRS swaptions, with small adjustments to conventions.

Measuring interest rate risk. Since cash flows of both sides depend on rates, the relevant sensitivity is the effective duration, computed by revaluation under parallel shifts \(\pm\Delta R\) of the curve: \[D^{ef}_A = -\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A}{\partial R} \approx -\frac{A^+ - A^-}{2A\,\Delta R}, \qquad D^{ef}_L = -\frac{1}{L}\frac{\partial L}{\partial R} \approx -\frac{L^+ - L^-}{2L\,\Delta R}.\] Duration matching chooses the asset portfolio so that the value-weighted durations coincide, minimising the duration gap \[D_{Gap} = D^{ef}_A - \frac LA\,D^{ef}_L,\] so that a parallel move of rates leaves the net asset value \(A - L\) approximately unchanged. Interest rate swaps and swaptions are the main hedging instruments.

9.7 Exercises

A deposit of 100 over 182 days pays 1.60 under ACT/360 simple interest. Find the simple rate and the equivalent continuously compounded rate under ACT/365.

Solution. \(\tau_{360} = 182/360 = 0.5056\), so \(R_s = 1.60/(100 \times 0.5056) = 3.165\%\). \(P = 1/1.016\); with \(\tau_{365} = 182/365 = 0.4986\), \(R_c = \log(1.016)/0.4986 = 3.184\%\).

Verify the bootstrap formula from the par condition and recompute \(P(0, 2)\) in the example.

Solution. At par, the floating leg \(1 - P(0, T_i)\) equals the fixed leg \(S_i\sum_{j\leq i}\tau_jP(0, T_j)\); isolating \(P(0, T_i)\) gives the formula. For \(T_2\): \(P(0, 2) = (1 - 0.032 \times 0.97087)/1.032 = 0.96893/1.032 = 0.93889\).

Compute the ratio \(\Sigma_R/\Sigma_L\) for a caplet on a 3-month period starting in 1 year, at \(t = 0\), and at \(t = T_{i-1} + 1.5\) months.

Solution. At \(t = 0\): \(\Sigma_R^2/\sigma^2 = 1 + 0.25/3 = 1.0833\) against \(\Sigma_L^2/\sigma^2 = 1\), ratio \(1.041\). Midway through the accrual period the forward-looking rate is already fixed (\(\Sigma_L = 0\)), while \(\Sigma_R^2/\sigma^2 = (0.125)^3/(3\times0.25^2) = 0.0104\): the backward-looking caplet still has optionality.

(Duration matching; Hull 4.11–4.12) An insurer must pay \(100\) in 10 years. The curve is flat at \(3\%\) (continuous). It can invest in 2-year and 30-year zero-coupon bonds. Find the holdings that match present value and duration, and explain why the hedge is not perfect.

Solution. The liability is worth \(L = 100e^{-0.3} = 74.08\) with duration \(10\). Holding present values \(x\) in the 2-year and \(y\) in the 30-year bond: \(x + y = 74.08\) and \(2x + 30y = 10 \times 74.08\), so \(y = 21.17\), \(x = 52.92\). The portfolio is immunised against small parallel shifts, and since its convexity (\(\frac{4x + 900y}{L} = 260\)) exceeds the liability’s (\(100\)), it gains from larger parallel moves. It remains exposed to non-parallel moves (a steepening of the curve between 10 and 30 years), and must be rebalanced as time passes and rates change, since durations drift.

(Hull 4.9) A deposit pays \(4\%\) per annum with continuous compounding, but interest is actually paid quarterly. How much interest is paid each quarter on \(10\,000\)?

Solution. The equivalent quarterly-compounded rate is \(4(e^{0.01} - 1) = 4.0201\%\), so each quarter pays \(10\,000 \times 0.040201/4 = 100.50\).

## The fixed-income market

The fixed-income market trades debt securities that pay predetermined cash flows: government bonds, corporate and covered bonds, municipal bonds, securitised products (MBS, ABS), money-market instruments. Issuers are sovereigns and financial or non-financial corporates; investors (asset managers, insurers, banks, pension funds) seek income, capital preservation, diversification and **liability matching**. Bonds differ by maturity (money market below 1 year, short 1–3, intermediate 3–7, long beyond 7), currency, structure (fixed, floating, inflation-linked, callable or putable, securitised) and credit rating (AAA down to D).

Prices and yields move inversely. The total yield of a risky bond decomposes as
$$\text{yield} = \text{risk-free rate} + \text{credit spread} + \text{liquidity premium},$$
so the risk-free curve is the reference for everything: **discounting** cash flows (time value of money without credit risk), **forwarding** floating rates (forward rates implied by no arbitrage), risk measures (duration, DV01, convexity), the risk-neutral drift in option pricing, and, for insurers, the fair value of both assets and liabilities.

**Risk-free proxies.** No asset is truly risk-free. Proxies are short-term rates anchored by central banks — government bills of top-rated sovereigns, and the new overnight benchmarks €STR (euro), SOFR (US), SONIA (UK), SARON (Switzerland), TONA (Japan) — and the derivatives written on them: futures, overnight indexed swaps (OIS). ## Market conventions

**Day counts.** The **year fraction** $\tau(t, T)$ between two dates depends on the day-count convention: ACT/360 (actual days divided by 360, standard for money markets), ACT/365, ACT/ACT, 30/360. For ACT/360, $\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i) = (T_i - T_{i-1})/360$ with dates counted in days.

**Business-day rules** adjust payment dates falling on holidays: *Following* (next business day), *Modified Following* (next business day unless it crosses into the next month, then the previous one), *Unadjusted*.

**Compounding.** A ZCB price $P(t, T)$ defines a spot rate in each convention:

| compounding | definition | rate from price |
|:--|:--|:--|
| simple | $P = \dfrac{1}{1 + \tau R_s}$ | $R_s = \dfrac{1}{\tau}\Bigl(\dfrac1P - 1\Bigr)$ |
| annual | $P = (1 + R_a)^{-\tau}$ | $R_a = P^{-1/\tau} - 1$ |
| $k$ times a year | $P = \Bigl(1 + \dfrac{R_k}{k}\Bigr)^{-k\tau}$ | $R_k = k\bigl(P^{-1/(k\tau)} - 1\bigr)$ |
| continuous | $P = e^{-R_c\tau}$ | $R_c = -\dfrac{\log P}{\tau}$ |

with $\tau = \tau(t, T)$. The **zero curve** $T \mapsto R_g(t, T)$ depends on the convention $g$; the **discount curve** $T \mapsto P(t, T)$ does not, and is decreasing from $P(t, t) = 1$ when rates are positive.

**Interpolation.** Quotes exist only for some maturities, and are sparse or illiquid at the long end. Missing points are interpolated — linearly, by cubic splines, or with parametric families such as Nelson–Siegel. For par rates $S_{T_{i-1}}, S_{T_i}$ quoted at consecutive maturities, linear interpolation gives, for $T_{i-1} < T_h < T_i$,
$$S_{T_h} = \frac{S_{T_{i-1}}(T_i - T_h) + S_{T_i}(T_h - T_{i-1})}{T_i - T_{i-1}} .$$

**Bonds.** A fixed-rate bond with coupon rate $K$ and dates $T_1, \dots, T_n$ is worth
$$B_{fx}(t, T_n) = \sum_{i=1}^nP(t, T_i)\,\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)\,K + P(t, T_n).$$
For a **defaultable** issuer, market practice discounts with the risk-free zero rate plus a **zero spread** $S$: with annual compounding $\bar P(t, T) = (1 + R_a(t, T) + S)^{-\tau(t,T)}$, and the bond price uses $\bar P$ in place of $P$.

## From IBORs to RFRs

IBORs (Libor, Euribor) were for decades the benchmark for loans, notes and derivatives, and together with overnight rates (Fed Funds, EONIA) were treated as risk-free. During the 2007–2009 crisis the IBOR–overnight spread widened dramatically (Chapter 1), showing that IBORs contain bank credit and liquidity risk; IBOR quotes were also manipulated. Hence:

- 2013–2014: the Financial Stability Board reviews the major benchmarks and recommends alternatives;
- 2017: the UK FCA announces it will stop supporting Libor panels;
- 2019: ISDA establishes fallbacks to transaction-based overnight RFRs;
- Libor is discontinued (USD Libor on 30 June 2023), replaced by SOFR, SONIA, SARON, TONA; in the euro area €STR replaced EONIA, while **Euribor survives** in reformed, transaction-based form.

The two kinds of rates differ structurally:

- **IBORs are forward-looking term rates**: quoted for tenors from one week to one year as simple rates, fixed at the *start* of the accrual period — a market expectation.
- **RFRs are backward-looking overnight rates**: published daily, with no term structure of their own; the rate for a period is obtained by **compounding the daily fixings in arrears**, so it is known only at the *end* of the period.

Correspondingly, three pricing environments have succeeded one another in a single currency:

1. **Single curve, IBOR** (before 2007): IBOR rates for both discounting and forwarding.
2. **Dual curve** (after 2007): overnight rates (EONIA, Fed Funds, now €STR, SOFR) for discounting, IBOR for forwarding — still the standard for Euribor derivatives.
3. **Single curve, RFR, backward-looking**: the RFR curve for both discounting and forwarding. This is the setting of the rest of the chapter.

## Backward-looking rates

Let $C(t) = \exp\bigl(\int_0^tr(u)\,du\bigr)$ be the cash account driven by the instantaneous risk-free rate (Chapter 8), so that $P(t, T) = \mathbb E^Q\bigl[e^{-\int_t^Tr(u)\,du}\bigm|\mathcal F_t\bigr]$.

::: {.definition name="Compounded-in-arrears RFR"}
For an accrual period $[T_{i-1}, T_i)$ with $d_i$ business days, daily fixings $r(i, j)$ and day-count fractions $\delta(i, j)$,
$$R(T_{i-1}, T_i) = \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl[\prod_{j=1}^{d_i}\bigl(1 + r(i, j)\delta(i, j)\bigr) - 1\Bigr] \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl[\frac{C(T_i)}{C(T_{i-1})} - 1\Bigr],$$
the continuous-time version being the limit of vanishing daily steps.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Forward value of a backward-looking rate"}
For $t \leq T_{i-1}$, the expectation of $R(T_{i-1}, T_i)$ under the $T_i$-forward measure (whose numeraire is $P(\cdot, T_i)$) is
$$R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i) = \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl(\frac{P(t, T_{i-1})}{P(t, T_i)} - 1\Bigr),$$
the same formula as the simple forward rate of the IBOR world. For $T_{i-1} < t < T_i$ part of the rate is already known:
$$R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i) = \frac{1}{\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)}\Bigl(\frac{e^{\int_{T_{i-1}}^tr(u)\,du}}{P(t, T_i)} - 1\Bigr).$$
:::

*Proof.* Under the $T_i$-forward measure, $\mathbb E^{T_i}[X\mid\mathcal F_t] = \mathbb E^Q\bigl[e^{-\int_t^{T_i}r}X\bigm|\mathcal F_t\bigr]/P(t, T_i)$. With $X = C(T_i)/C(T_{i-1})$, $e^{-\int_t^{T_i}r}X = e^{-\int_t^{T_{i-1}}r}$, whose $Q$-expectation is $P(t, T_{i-1})$. $\square$

### Overnight indexed swaps

An **OIS** exchanges a fixed rate $K$ against the compounded RFR. A payer OIS with floating dates $T_1, \dots, T_n$, fixed dates $T'_1, \dots, T'_m$ (same start and end), notional $N$, valued at $t < T_0$:
$$OIS_R(t, T_0, T_n) = N\sum_{i=1}^nP(t, T_i)\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i) - N\sum_{j=1}^mP(t, T'_j)\tau(T'_{j-1}, T'_j)K .$$
By the proposition, before the start this coincides with the single-curve Libor swap formula (and the floating leg telescopes to $N(P(t, T_0) - P(t, T_n))$). Once an accrual period has started, the known part of the compounded rate must be included: the first floating coupon becomes $N\bigl(e^{\int_{T_0}^tr(u)\,du} - P(t, T_1)\bigr)$.

### Swaptions

Assume the forward swap rate $S_R(t, T_0, T_n)$ is a log-normal martingale under the **swap (annuity) measure**, with numeraire the annuity $A(t) = \sum_jP(t, T'_j)\tau(T'_{j-1}, T'_j)$:
$$\frac{dS_R(t, T_0, T_n)}{S_R(t, T_0, T_n)} = \sigma_S\,dW_t .$$
Then the payer swaption follows **Black's (1976) formula**
$$PSWT_R(t) = N\,A(t)\bigl[S_R\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(S_R/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma_S^2(T_0 - t)}{\sigma_S\sqrt{T_0 - t}} .$$
Swaption valuation is **unaffected** by whether the underlying floating rate is forward- or backward-looking: what matters is the swap rate at $T_0$.

### Caplets on backward-looking rates

A caplet on $[T_{i-1}, T_i]$ with strike $K$ pays $N\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)\bigl(R(T_{i-1}, T_i) - K\bigr)^+$ at $T_i$. The underlying rate keeps accruing during the period, so its uncertainty is resolved only at $T_i$. Lyashenko and Mercurio (2019) model the forward rate as a log-normal martingale under the (extended) $T_i$-forward measure, with a volatility that decays linearly during the accrual period:
$$\frac{dR(t, T_{i-1}, T_i)}{R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i)} = \sigma_R\min\Bigl(\frac{(T_i - t)^+}{T_i - T_{i-1}}, 1\Bigr)dW_t .$$
(The *extended* ZCB price — the bond maturing at $T_i$, with proceeds rolled over at the RFR after $T_i$ — keeps the numeraire defined for $t > T_i$.) This gives a Black formula
$$CPLT_R(t) = N\tau(T_{i-1}, T_i)P(t, T_i)\bigl[R(t, T_{i-1}, T_i)\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(R/K) \pm \tfrac12\Sigma_R^2}{\Sigma_R},$$
with total variance
$$\Sigma_R^2 = \sigma_R^2\int_t^{T_i}\min\Bigl(\frac{(T_i - s)^+}{T_i - T_{i-1}}, 1\Bigr)^2ds = \sigma_R^2\Bigl[\max(T_{i-1} - t, 0) + \frac{(T_i - \max(T_{i-1}, t))^3}{3(T_i - T_{i-1})^2}\Bigr].$$
Since a forward-looking caplet's rate is fixed at $T_{i-1}$ (variance $\sigma^2(T_{i-1} - t)$), **$\Sigma_R \geq \Sigma_L$**: the backward-looking caplet carries the extra variance of the accrual period, a third of its length.

::: {.example name="Forward- vs backward-looking caplet"}
On the curve bootstrapped below, the at-the-money caplet on $[1, 2]$ (forward rate $3.407\%$) with $\sigma = 20\%$ is worth $25.5$ bp of notional if the rate is forward-looking ($\Sigma_L = 0.2\sqrt1$) and $29.4$ bp if backward-looking ($\Sigma_R = 0.2\sqrt{1 + 1/3} = 0.231$).
:::

The same structure holds in the one- and two-factor Hull–White models, where the caplet becomes an option on $C(T_i)/C(T_{i-1})$ and $\Sigma_R^2$ splits into the variance of the bond's forward price up to $T_{i-1}$ plus the extra variance accumulated over $[T_{i-1}, T_i]$.

## Bootstrapping an RFR curve

RFR curves are stripped from OIS quoted **at par** (zero initial value), typically with annual payments on both legs. With spot start $T_0 = 0$ and par rates $S_i = S_R(0, 0, T_i)$, setting the OIS value to zero for each maturity in turn,
$$P(0, T_1) = \frac{1}{1 + \tau_1S_1}, \qquad P(0, T_i) = \frac{1 - S_i\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tau_jP(0, T_j)}{1 + \tau_iS_i}, \quad i = 2, \dots, n,$$
with $\tau_j = \tau(T_{j-1}, T_j)$. (This follows from the par condition $1 - P(0, T_i) = S_i\sum_{j\leq i}\tau_jP(0, T_j)$, the telescoped floating leg equal to the fixed leg.)

::: {.example name="A five-year OIS bootstrap"}
Par rates (annual payments, $\tau = 1$): 1y $3.00\%$, 2y $3.20\%$, 3y $3.35\%$, 5y $3.50\%$; the 4y rate is linearly interpolated, $3.425\%$.

| $T$ | par rate | $P(0, T)$ | zero rate (annual) | zero rate (cont.) |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| 1 | 3.000% | 0.97087 | 3.000% | 2.956% |
| 2 | 3.200% | 0.93889 | 3.203% | 3.153% |
| 3 | 3.350% | 0.90568 | 3.357% | 3.302% |
| 4 | 3.425% | 0.87365 | 3.435% | 3.377% |
| 5 | 3.500% | 0.84143 | 3.513% | 3.453% |

With an upward-sloping curve, zero rates lie slightly above par rates, and forward rates above both (the 1-year forward from 4 to 5 years is $3.83\%$).
:::

In practice (e.g. SOFR on 30 September 2024) the procedure is the same with more instruments, real day counts, a few days' spot lag and an interpolation scheme between pillars.

## Risk-free rates in insurance: Solvency II

Under **Solvency II**, EU insurers compute their solvency ratio from a market-value balance sheet, valuing liabilities (technical provisions) and the Solvency Capital Requirement with a **regulatory risk-free curve** published by EIOPA:

1. collect par rates of IRS (Euribor) or OIS (SARON, SONIA, SOFR, …) for the liquid maturities (up to about 20 years for the euro);
2. subtract a **credit risk adjustment** (10 bp for Euribor-based curves, zero for RFR-based ones);
3. **bootstrap** zero rates from the adjusted par rates;
4. **extrapolate** beyond the last liquid point up to 150 years with the **Smith–Wilson** method, converging to an ultimate forward rate;
5. optionally add a **volatility adjustment** or **matching adjustment**, which reflect the spread insurers earn on their assets.

**Economic scenario generators.** Life liabilities contain financial options and guarantees, profit-sharing rules, management actions and policyholder behaviour, so their best estimate (BEL) is computed by Monte Carlo projection of the balance sheet in an asset–liability model, over thousands of **risk-neutral scenarios** that start from the Solvency II curve. Typical rate models are the displaced LIBOR market model with stochastic volatility, the two-factor Hull–White model and the displaced Black–Karasinski model, calibrated to OIS (level) and swaptions (implied volatility). The IBOR→RFR transition did not change these models: OIS and OIS swaptions replace IRS and IRS swaptions, with small adjustments to conventions.

**Measuring interest rate risk.** Since cash flows of both sides depend on rates, the relevant sensitivity is the **effective duration**, computed by revaluation under parallel shifts $\pm\Delta R$ of the curve:
$$D^{ef}_A = -\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A}{\partial R} \approx -\frac{A^+ - A^-}{2A\,\Delta R}, \qquad D^{ef}_L = -\frac{1}{L}\frac{\partial L}{\partial R} \approx -\frac{L^+ - L^-}{2L\,\Delta R}.$$
**Duration matching** chooses the asset portfolio so that the value-weighted durations coincide, minimising the **duration gap**
$$D_{Gap} = D^{ef}_A - \frac LA\,D^{ef}_L,$$
so that a parallel move of rates leaves the net asset value $A - L$ approximately unchanged. Interest rate swaps and swaptions are the main hedging instruments.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
A deposit of 100 over 182 days pays 1.60 under ACT/360 simple interest. Find the simple rate and the equivalent continuously compounded rate under ACT/365.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\tau_{360} = 182/360 = 0.5056$, so $R_s = 1.60/(100 \times 0.5056) = 3.165\%$. $P = 1/1.016$; with $\tau_{365} = 182/365 = 0.4986$, $R_c = \log(1.016)/0.4986 = 3.184\%$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Verify the bootstrap formula from the par condition and recompute $P(0, 2)$ in the example.
:::

::: {.solution}
At par, the floating leg $1 - P(0, T_i)$ equals the fixed leg $S_i\sum_{j\leq i}\tau_jP(0, T_j)$; isolating $P(0, T_i)$ gives the formula. For $T_2$: $P(0, 2) = (1 - 0.032 \times 0.97087)/1.032 = 0.96893/1.032 = 0.93889$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Compute the ratio $\Sigma_R/\Sigma_L$ for a caplet on a 3-month period starting in 1 year, at $t = 0$, and at $t = T_{i-1} + 1.5$ months.
:::

::: {.solution}
At $t = 0$: $\Sigma_R^2/\sigma^2 = 1 + 0.25/3 = 1.0833$ against $\Sigma_L^2/\sigma^2 = 1$, ratio $1.041$. Midway through the accrual period the forward-looking rate is already fixed ($\Sigma_L = 0$), while $\Sigma_R^2/\sigma^2 = (0.125)^3/(3\times0.25^2) = 0.0104$: the backward-looking caplet still has optionality.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Duration matching; Hull 4.11–4.12)** An insurer must pay $100$ in 10 years. The curve is flat at $3\%$ (continuous). It can invest in 2-year and 30-year zero-coupon bonds. Find the holdings that match present value and duration, and explain why the hedge is not perfect.
:::

::: {.solution}
The liability is worth $L = 100e^{-0.3} = 74.08$ with duration $10$. Holding present values $x$ in the 2-year and $y$ in the 30-year bond: $x + y = 74.08$ and $2x + 30y = 10 \times 74.08$, so $y = 21.17$, $x = 52.92$. The portfolio is immunised against small **parallel** shifts, and since its convexity ($\frac{4x + 900y}{L} = 260$) exceeds the liability's ($100$), it gains from larger parallel moves. It remains exposed to **non-parallel** moves (a steepening of the curve between 10 and 30 years), and must be rebalanced as time passes and rates change, since durations drift.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Hull 4.9)** A deposit pays $4\%$ per annum with continuous compounding, but interest is actually paid quarterly. How much interest is paid each quarter on $10\,000$?
:::

::: {.solution}
The equivalent quarterly-compounded rate is $4(e^{0.01} - 1) = 4.0201\%$, so each quarter pays $10\,000 \times 0.040201/4 = 100.50$.
:::
