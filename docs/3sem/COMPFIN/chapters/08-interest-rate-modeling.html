8  Interest rate modelling

The Black–Scholes model takes the interest rate as a known constant. In reality rates move unpredictably, creating interest rate risk — and fixed-income derivatives make up about 80% of the OTC derivatives market. This chapter defines the various interest rates and how they are built from zero-coupon bond prices, prices the linear products (FRAs, floating-rate bonds, swaps) by pure no-arbitrage arguments, introduces the optional ones (caps, floors, swaptions), and then models the short rate with an SDE. Hedging arguments lead to the term-structure PDE and its Feynman–Kač solution; affine models (Vasiček, Cox–Ingersoll–Ross, Hull–White) make bond prices explicit.

8.1 Interest rates in the market

Several rates coexist:

  • Treasury rates: the rate at which a government borrows in its own currency; traditionally “risk-free”, but not really (sovereign spreads).
  • Interbank rates (Euribor; formerly Libor, discontinued on 1 July 2023): the rate at which major banks lend to each other for a given tenor (1, 3, 6, 12 months); reference rates for an enormous volume of loans and derivatives.
  • Overnight rates: banks adjust their central-bank reserves by lending and borrowing overnight (€STR in the euro area, the Federal Funds rate in the US).
  • Repo rates: rates on secured borrowing (repurchase agreements), e.g. SOFR in the US.

The rate applicable to a loan depends on credit risk, the risk that the borrower defaults. A truly risk-free rate does not exist, but derivative pricing needs one; overnight rates are used as proxies (Chapter 9).

8.2 Zero-coupon bonds and forward rates

The fundamental purpose of the risk-free rate is to measure the value of time. Let \(P_t(T)\) be the value at \(t\) of one unit of currency delivered at \(T \geq t\) without risk — the price of a zero-coupon bond (ZCB), with \(P_T(T) = 1\). The term structure at \(t\) is the curve \(T \mapsto P_t(T)\); modelling it means describing its random evolution over time.

For \(t \leq S < T\):

  • simple spot rate for \([S, T]\): \(\displaystyle L_S(S, T) = \frac{1}{T - S}\Bigl(\frac{1}{P_S(T)} - 1\Bigr)\), the rate for lending at \(S\) until \(T\);
  • simple forward rate for \([S, T]\) contracted at \(t\): \(\displaystyle L_t(S, T) = \frac{1}{T - S}\Bigl(\frac{P_t(S)}{P_t(T)} - 1\Bigr)\);
  • continuously compounded forward rate: \(\displaystyle F_t(S, T) = -\frac{\log P_t(T) - \log P_t(S)}{T - S}\);
  • instantaneous forward rate: \(\displaystyle f_t(T) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\log P_t(T) = \lim_{S\to T}F_t(S, T)\), the rate contracted at \(t\) for \([T, T + dT]\);
  • short rate: \(r_t = f_t(t)\), the rate for \([t, t + dt]\).

Consequently \(P_t(T) = \exp\bigl(-\int_t^Tf_t(u)\,du\bigr)\).

Why the forward rate formula. At \(t\), buy \(P_t(S)/P_t(T)\) ZCBs maturing at \(T\) and sell one ZCB maturing at \(S\): zero cost. At \(S\) pay \(1\); at \(T\) receive \(P_t(S)/P_t(T)\). The rate for \([S, T]\) has been locked in at \(t\): \(1 + (T - S)L_t(S, T) = P_t(S)/P_t(T)\).

The bank account pays the short rate continuously: \(dB_t = r_tB_t\,dt\), \(B_0 = 1\), i.e. \(B_t = \exp\bigl(\int_0^tr_u\,du\bigr)\) — the value of rolling over, at each instant, an investment in ZCBs maturing at \(t + dt\). It is locally riskless. One can model the ZCB prices \(P_t(T)\), the forward rates \(f_t(T)\) (the Heath–Jarrow–Morton approach), the simple forward rates \(L_t(S, T)\) (LIBOR market models), or the short rate \(r_t\) and deduce everything else by no arbitrage. This chapter follows the last route.

8.3 Linear interest rate products

8.3.1 Forward rate agreements

Rates change: the 1-year Euribor fixed in one year, \(L_1(1, 2)\), may differ from today’s \(L_0(0, 1)\). A borrower paying a floating rate faces interest rate risk.

A FRA for the period \([S, T]\) with fixed rate \(K\) and notional \(N\) exchanges at \(T\) the floating rate \(L_S(S, T)\) (received, the floating leg) against \(K\) (paid, the fixed leg): \[\text{payoff at } T = N(T - S)\bigl(L_S(S, T) - K\bigr).\]

Today you arrange to borrow 10 000 € in one year, repaying in two years at the 1-year Euribor fixed in one year. A FRA on \([1, 2]\) with notional 10 000 € and rate \(K\) pays \(10\,000\,L_1(1, 2)\) against \(10\,000\,K\): the net cost of the loan is \(10\,000\,K\), fixed today.

For \(t \leq S\) (unit notional), \[\Pi^{FRA}_t = P_t(S) - \bigl(1 + (T - S)K\bigr)P_t(T) = (T - S)\bigl(L_t(S, T) - K\bigr)P_t(T).\] The FRA has zero value iff \(K = L_t(S, T)\): the forward rate plays the role of the forward price.

Proof. The payoff \((T - S)(L_S(S, T) - K) = \frac{1}{P_S(T)} - 1 - (T - S)K\) is known at \(S\), so its value at \(S\) is \(P_S(T)\) times it: \(1 - (1 + (T - S)K)P_S(T)\) — one ZCB maturing at \(S\) minus \(1 + (T - S)K\) ZCBs maturing at \(T\). By the law of one price this holds at every \(t \leq S\). \(\square\)

8.3.2 Floating-rate bonds and swaps

Figure: payment schedule. The floating rate for \([T_{k-1}, T_k]\) is fixed at \(T_{k-1}\) and paid at \(T_k\).

A floating-rate bond with dates \(0 \leq T_0 < T_1 < \dots < T_N\) pays at each \(T_k\) the coupon \(c_k = (T_k - T_{k-1})\bigl(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) + \alpha\bigr)\), with a fixed spread \(\alpha\) (in basis points), plus the principal \(1\) at \(T_N\).

\[\Pi_0(\alpha) = P_0(T_0) + \alpha\sum_{k=1}^N(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k).\] In particular, a floater with zero spread is worth exactly \(P_0(T_0)\): par at the fixing date.

Proof. Split each floating coupon as \((T_k - T_{k-1})\bigl(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) - L_0(T_{k-1}, T_k)\bigr) + (T_k - T_{k-1})L_0(T_{k-1}, T_k)\). The first part is a FRA struck at the forward rate, worth zero. The second is a known amount paid at \(T_k\), worth \(\bigl(\frac{P_0(T_{k-1})}{P_0(T_k)} - 1\bigr)P_0(T_k) = P_0(T_{k-1}) - P_0(T_k)\). Summing telescopes to \(P_0(T_0) - P_0(T_N)\). The spreads are worth \(\alpha\sum_k(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k)\), and the principal \(P_0(T_N)\). \(\square\)

An IRS with dates \(T_0 < \dots < T_N\) and fixed rate \(S\) exchanges at each \(T_k\) the net amount \((T_k - T_{k-1})\bigl(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) - S\bigr)\) (payer swap: receive floating, pay fixed). It is a portfolio of \(N\) FRAs with common rate \(K = S\).

The fixed rate making the swap worthless at \(t = 0\) is \[S(T_0, \dots, T_N) = \frac{P_0(T_0) - P_0(T_N)}{\sum_{k=1}^N(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k)} .\] The denominator \(A_0 = \sum_k(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k)\) is the annuity (or PVBP).

Proof. The swap’s cash flows are those of a floater with spread \(\alpha = -S\) minus the principal, worth \(\Pi_0(-S) - P_0(T_N) = P_0(T_0) - P_0(T_N) - S\,A_0\); set it to zero. \(\square\)

For example, with a flat zero curve at 3% (continuous) and annual payments over 5 years, \(S = (1 - e^{-0.15})/\sum_{k=1}^5e^{-0.03k} = 3.045\%\) — the annually compounded equivalent of 3%.

8.4 Options on rates: caps, floors, swaptions

A caplet on \([S, T]\) with strike \(K\) pays \((T - S)\bigl(L_S(S, T) - K\bigr)^+\) at \(T\); a floorlet pays \((T - S)\bigl(K - L_S(S, T)\bigr)^+\). A cap (floor) with dates \(T_0, \dots, T_N\) is the sum of the caplets (floorlets) on \([T_{k-1}, T_k]\).

A borrower paying a floating rate who buys a cap never pays more than \(K\); a lender receiving floating who buys a floor never receives less than \(K\). A caplet is an option on a FRA, a cap a portfolio of caplets.

The caplet is worth, at time \(S\), \[\Pi^{cpl}_S = \bigl(1 + (T - S)K\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{1 + (T - S)K} - P_S(T)\Bigr)^+,\] i.e. \(1 + (T - S)K\) European puts with maturity \(S\) on the ZCB maturing at \(T\), with strike \(1/(1 + (T - S)K)\). Similarly a floorlet is a portfolio of calls on a ZCB.

Proof. \((T - S)(L_S(S,T) - K)^+ = \bigl(\frac{1}{P_S(T)} - (1 + (T - S)K)\bigr)^+\) is known at \(S\); multiplying by \(P_S(T)\) gives the value at \(S\): \(\bigl(1 - (1 + (T - S)K)P_S(T)\bigr)^+\). \(\square\)

A (payer) swaption gives the right to enter at \(T_0\) into the swap with fixed rate \(S\). Its payoff at \(T_0\) is \[\bigl(S(T_0, \dots, T_N)_{T_0} - S\bigr)^+\sum_{k=1}^N(T_k - T_{k-1})P_{T_0}(T_k),\] where \(S(T_0, \dots, T_N)_{T_0}\) is the swap rate prevailing at \(T_0\): the swap is entered only if the market rate exceeds \(S\).

Equivalently a swaption is an option on a portfolio of ZCBs, i.e. on a coupon bond. Their market pricing is described below.

8.4.1 Market practice: Black’s formula

Traders quote caplets and swaptions through Black’s (1976) formula, a log-normal model for the underlying rate rather than for the short rate. For a caplet on \([T_{k-1}, T_k]\) with accrual \(\tau_k\), strike \(K\), forward rate \(F_k = L_0(T_{k-1}, T_k)\) and volatility \(\sigma_k\): \[\mathrm{Cpl}_0 = N\tau_kP_0(T_k)\bigl[F_k\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(F_k/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma_k^2T_{k-1}}{\sigma_k\sqrt{T_{k-1}}}.\] For a payer swaption on a swap starting at \(T_0\), with forward swap rate \(S_0 = S(T_0, \dots, T_N)\) and annuity \(A_0 = \sum_k\tau_kP_0(T_k)\): \[\mathrm{Swpt}_0 = N A_0\bigl[S_0\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(S_0/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2T_0}{\sigma\sqrt{T_0}}.\]

These are not ad hoc. Under the \(T_k\)-forward measure \(Q^{T_k}\) — the probability with numeraire \(P_t(T_k)\) instead of the bank account — the forward rate \(L_t(T_{k-1}, T_k) = \frac{1}{\tau_k}\bigl(\frac{P_t(T_{k-1})}{P_t(T_k)} - 1\bigr)\) is a ratio of a traded asset to the numeraire, hence a martingale, and \[\mathrm{Cpl}_0 = N\tau_kP_0(T_k)\,\mathbb E^{Q^{T_k}}\bigl[(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) - K)^+\bigr].\] If the forward rate is log-normal under \(Q^{T_k}\), this is exactly Black’s formula (the LIBOR market model makes this assumption for all forward rates simultaneously). Likewise the swap rate is a martingale under the annuity measure with numeraire \(A_t\). Caps are then quoted by a single flat volatility applied to all their caplets, or by caplet-specific spot volatilities. When rates can be negative, the Bachelier (normal) model, \(dF = \sigma_N\,dW\), replaces the log-normal one.

8.5 Short-rate models

Model the short rate under \(\mathbb P\) by \[dr_t = \mu(t, r_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, r_t)\,dW_t .\] ZCBs are taken as the fundamental traded assets, for every maturity \(T\). Since \(r\) is Markov, look for prices of the form \(P_t(T) = F^T(t, r_t)\).

8.5.1 The term-structure PDE

  1. Portfolio of two bonds. Fix maturities \(S < T\) and a self-financing \(\theta = (\theta^S, \theta^T)\) in the two ZCBs: \(V_t = \theta^S_tP_t(S) + \theta^T_tP_t(T)\), \(dV_t = \theta^S_t\,dP_t(S) + \theta^T_t\,dP_t(T)\), where by Itô’s formula \[dF^T(t, r_t) = \Bigl(F^T_t + \mu F^T_r + \tfrac12\sigma^2F^T_{rr}\Bigr)dt + \sigma F^T_r\,dW_t .\]
  2. Make it riskless. Choose \(\theta^S_t = -\theta^T_tF^T_r/F^S_r\), which kills the \(dW_t\) term.
  3. No arbitrage. A riskless portfolio must earn the short rate: \(dV_t = r_tV_t\,dt\). After simplification, \[\frac{F^T_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2F^T_{rr} - rF^T}{F^T_r} = \frac{F^S_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2F^S_{rr} - rF^S}{F^S_r}.\]
  4. Separation. Since \(S\) and \(T\) are arbitrary, both sides equal a function \(q(t, r_t)\) independent of maturity. Writing \(q = \lambda\sigma - \mu\):

For every \(T > 0\), \[F^T_t + (\mu - \lambda\sigma)F^T_r + \frac12\sigma^2F^T_{rr} - rF^T = 0, \qquad F^T(T, r) = 1,\] where \(\lambda(t, r)\) is the market price of interest rate risk.

Why does \(\lambda\) appear? In BSM there is one source of risk and one exogenously given risky asset to hedge it; here the only exogenous asset is the bank account, and the short rate is not a traded asset. The real-world dynamics of \(r\) alone do not determine bond prices: one must also know \(\lambda\), which is set by the market — in practice, by calibration.

The solution of the term-structure PDE is \[P_t(T) = F^T(t, r_t) = \mathbb E^Q\Bigl[e^{-\int_t^Tr_u\,du}\Bigm|r_t\Bigr],\] where under \(Q\) the short rate follows \(dr_t = \bigl(\mu - \lambda\sigma\bigr)(t, r_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, r_t)\,dW^Q_t\).

Under this risk-neutral probability every ZCB has expected return \(r_t\). Prices depend on \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\) only through the risk-neutral drift \(\mu^Q = \mu - \lambda\sigma = -q\). Hence the usual practice of risk-neutral modelling: specify \(r\) directly under \(Q\) and calibrate its parameters to market prices.

8.5.2 Affine models

A model \(dr_t = \mu^Q(t, r_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, r_t)\,dW^Q_t\) is affine if \[\mu^Q(t, r) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1r, \qquad \sigma^2(t, r) = \beta_0 + \beta_1r .\]

Try the exponential-affine ansatz \(F^T(t, r) = \exp\bigl(A(T - t) - B(T - t)r\bigr)\). Substituting into the PDE (with \(\tau = T - t\), so \(\partial_t = -\partial_\tau\)), \[-A'(\tau) - \bigl(1 - B'(\tau)\bigr)r - (\alpha_0 + \alpha_1r)B(\tau) + \tfrac12(\beta_0 + \beta_1r)B(\tau)^2 = 0 .\] This must hold for all \(r\), so the constant and the coefficient of \(r\) vanish separately: \[B'(\tau) = 1 + \alpha_1B(\tau) - \tfrac12\beta_1B(\tau)^2, \qquad A'(\tau) = -\alpha_0B(\tau) + \tfrac12\beta_0B(\tau)^2, \qquad A(0) = B(0) = 0 .\] The equation for \(B\) is a Riccati ODE; \(A\) then follows by integration. The PDE has been reduced to ODEs, solvable explicitly in the classical cases.

8.5.3 Vasiček

\[dr_t = \theta(\kappa - r_t)\,dt + \gamma\,dW^Q_t,\] with speed of mean reversion \(\theta\), long-run mean \(\kappa\), volatility \(\gamma\).

This is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Chapter 4): \[r_t = r_0e^{-\theta t} + \kappa(1 - e^{-\theta t}) + \gamma\int_0^te^{-\theta(t-u)}dW^Q_u,\] Gaussian with \(\mathbb E^Q[r_t] = r_0e^{-\theta t} + \kappa(1 - e^{-\theta t})\) and \(\mathrm{Var}^Q(r_t) = \frac{\gamma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})\). The rate can become negative — once seen as a defect, but a feature after 2014. Here \(\alpha_0 = \theta\kappa\), \(\alpha_1 = -\theta\), \(\beta_0 = \gamma^2\), \(\beta_1 = 0\), and \[B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\theta\tau}}{\theta}, \qquad A(\tau) = \Bigl(\kappa - \frac{\gamma^2}{2\theta^2}\Bigr)\bigl(B(\tau) - \tau\bigr) - \frac{\gamma^2}{4\theta}B(\tau)^2 .\] (The course slides print \(\kappa - \gamma^2/2\) in the first factor; the correct factor is \(\kappa - \gamma^2/(2\theta^2)\).) Caplets and floorlets have Black–Scholes-like closed forms, since bond prices are log-normal.

8.5.4 Cox–Ingersoll–Ross

\[dr_t = \theta(\kappa - r_t)\,dt + \gamma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW^Q_t .\]

The rate stays non-negative, and strictly positive if \(2\kappa\theta \geq \gamma^2\) (Feller condition). \(r_t\) follows a non-central chi-square distribution, with the same mean as in Vasiček and variance \[\mathrm{Var}^Q(r_t) = r_0\frac{\gamma^2}{\theta}\bigl(e^{-\theta t} - e^{-2\theta t}\bigr) + \frac{\kappa\gamma^2}{2\theta}\bigl(1 - e^{-\theta t}\bigr)^2 .\] It is the same SDE as the variance in the Heston model. With \(h = \sqrt{\theta^2 + 2\gamma^2}\), \[B(\tau) = \frac{2(e^{h\tau} - 1)}{2h + (\theta + h)(e^{h\tau} - 1)}, \qquad A(\tau) = \frac{2\kappa\theta}{\gamma^2}\log\frac{2he^{(\theta + h)\tau/2}}{2h + (\theta + h)(e^{h\tau} - 1)} .\] Caplets and floorlets are semi-explicit.

With \(r_0 = 3\%\), \(\theta = 0.5\), \(\kappa = 4\%\) and \(T = 5\): Vasiček with \(\gamma = 1\%\) gives \(P_0(5) = 0.8343\) (yield \(3.62\%\)); CIR with \(\gamma = 5\%\) (Feller holds: \(0.04 > 0.0025\)) gives \(P_0(5) = 0.8342\), confirmed by a Monte Carlo simulation (\(0.8342\)). The yield lies between \(r_0\) and \(\kappa\), pulled down slightly by the convexity term \(-\gamma^2/(2\theta^2)\).

8.5.5 Hull–White: fitting today’s curve

Vasiček and CIR have three or four parameters and cannot reproduce an arbitrary observed term structure. The Hull–White model (extended Vasiček) makes the drift time-dependent: \[dr_t = \bigl(\alpha(t) - \theta r_t\bigr)dt + \gamma\,dW^Q_t,\] still Gaussian and mean-reverting, with \(\mathbb E^Q[r_t] = r_0e^{-\theta t} + \int_0^te^{-\theta(t-u)}\alpha(u)\,du\). It is affine with \(B(\tau) = (1 - e^{-\theta\tau})/\theta\) as in Vasiček and \[A(T - t) = \int_t^T\Bigl(\frac{\gamma^2}{2}B(T - u)^2 - \alpha(u)B(T - u)\Bigr)du .\] The model forward curve is \(f_0(T) = -\partial_T\log P_0(T) = -\partial_TA(T) + \partial_TB(T)\,r_0\). Requiring \(f_0(T) = f^{\mathrm{mkt}}_0(T)\) for all \(T\) determines \(\alpha\) uniquely: \[\alpha(T) = \frac{\partial}{\partial T}f^{\mathrm{mkt}}_0(T) + \theta f^{\mathrm{mkt}}_0(T) + \frac{\gamma^2}{2\theta}\bigl(1 - e^{-2\theta T}\bigr).\] The model then reproduces today’s curve exactly by construction, and \(\theta\), \(\gamma\) are calibrated to option prices (caplets, swaptions), which also have closed forms.

Short-rate models are the natural first step but have limitations: one factor implies perfectly correlated rates of all maturities. Richer approaches model the whole forward curve (Heath–Jarrow–Morton) or the market forward rates directly (LIBOR/forward market models). After 2008 the spread between interbank and overnight rates forced a multi-curve framework, and the 2023 end of Libor moved markets to backward-looking risk-free rates — the subject of Chapter 9.

8.6 Exercises

Problems from Hull (H), Chapters 7, 29 and 31, and Björk (B), Chapters 22–23.

(B 22.1) In a FRA with continuous compounding, the lender pays \(K\) at \(S\) and receives \(Ke^{R^*(T - S)}\) at \(T\). (a) Value the contract at \(t < S\) in terms of ZCB prices. (b) Show that it is worth zero iff \(R^*\) equals the forward rate.

Solution.

  1. \(\Pi_t = -KP_t(S) + Ke^{R^*(T-S)}P_t(T)\). (b) \(\Pi_t = 0\) iff \(e^{R^*(T-S)} = P_t(S)/P_t(T)\), i.e. \(R^* = -\frac{\log P_t(T) - \log P_t(S)}{T - S} = F_t(S, T)\).

(H 7.2) A swap on \(100\) million has 10 months to run: six-month Libor is received against \(4\%\) (semiannual) paid. Forward Libor rates for all maturities are \(3\%\) (semiannual); the Libor fixed two months ago was \(2.4\%\); OIS rates are \(2.7\%\) (continuous) for discounting. Value the swap for both parties.

Solution. Payments in 4 and 10 months. Fixed leg: \(2\)m each. Floating: \(0.5 \times 2.4\% \times 100 = 1.2\)m (already fixed) and \(1.5\)m (at the forward rate). To the party paying floating and receiving fixed: \((2 - 1.2)e^{-0.027/3} + (2 - 1.5)e^{-0.027\times10/12} = 1.282\)m. To the party paying fixed: \(-1.282\)m.

(H 29.1) A company caps 3-month Libor at \(2\%\) on \(20\) million. At a reset date the rate is \(4\%\). What payment results, and when?

Solution. \(20\,000\,000 \times 0.25 \times (0.04 - 0.02) = 100\,000\), paid three months after the reset date (at the end of the accrual period).

(H 29.5) Price a caplet on the 3-month rate for the period from 15 to 18 months, struck at \(13\%\) (quarterly compounding), on a principal of \(1000\). The forward rate is \(12\%\), the 18-month zero rate is \(11.5\%\) (continuous), and the forward-rate volatility is \(12\%\).

Solution. Black’s formula with \(F = 0.12\), \(K = 0.13\), \(T_{k-1} = 1.25\), \(\sigma = 0.12\): \(d_1 = \frac{\log(12/13) + \frac12\,0.12^2\times1.25}{0.12\sqrt{1.25}} = -0.5295\), \(d_2 = -0.6637\), so \(F\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2) = 0.12\times0.2982 - 0.13\times0.2534 = 0.00284\). The price is \(1000 \times 0.25 \times e^{-0.115\times1.5} \times 0.00284 = 0.60\).

(H 29.14) Value a European swaption giving the right to enter in 4 years a 3-year annual-pay swap paying \(5\%\) fixed on \(10\) million. All swap rates are \(5\%\), OIS rates \(4.7\%\) (continuous), swap-rate volatility \(20\%\).

Solution. Annuity \(A = e^{-0.047\times5} + e^{-0.047\times6} + e^{-0.047\times7} = 2.2645\). At the money (\(S_0 = K = 5\%\)): \(d_{1,2} = \pm\tfrac12\times0.2\times2 = \pm0.2\), and the swaption is worth \(10^7 \times 2.2645 \times 0.05\,(\Phi(0.2) - \Phi(-0.2)) = 179\,500\).

(H 29.11, B 23.3) (a) Derive a put–call parity for swaptions. (b) Find the fixed rate \(R\) that makes a contract exchanging \(K(e^{RT} - 1)\) against \(K(e^{\int_0^Tr_s\,ds} - 1)\) at \(T\) worthless.

Solution.

  1. Payer minus receiver swaption with the same strike \(K\) pays \((S_{T_0} - K)^+A_{T_0} - (K - S_{T_0})^+A_{T_0} = (S_{T_0} - K)A_{T_0}\), the value of a forward-starting payer swap at rate \(K\): \(\mathrm{Payer} - \mathrm{Receiver} = A_0(S_0 - K)\). (H 31.4) In the risk-neutral Vasiček model with speed \(0.1\), level \(0.03\) and volatility \(0.01\), price a 5-year ZCB with principal \(1\) when \(r_0 = 2\%\).

Solution. Vasiček: \(0.3805\). CIR with \(\gamma = 0.0632\): \(0.3799\). The models agree closely when calibrated to the same initial volatility; they differ more for long maturities and for distributions of future rates (CIR cannot go negative; its volatility falls as rates fall).

(B 22.5) Show that \(f(0, T) = y(0, T) + T\,\partial_Ty(0, T)\), where \(y\) is the zero-coupon yield, and that for an increasing yield curve \(y_M(0, T) \leq y(0, T) \leq f(0, T)\), \(y_M\) being the yield of a coupon bond. Interpret.

Solution. \(P(0, T) = e^{-Ty(0,T)}\), so \(f = -\partial_T\log P = y + Ty'\). If \(y' \geq 0\), then \(f \geq y\). A coupon bond’s yield is a weighted average of the zero yields at its payment dates, all \(\leq T\), so \(y_M \leq y(0, T)\). The forward rate is the marginal rate at \(T\), the zero yield the average over \([0, T]\), and the coupon yield a further average — marginal above average when the curve rises.

(B 22.7) A consol pays \(1\) at \(t = 1, 2, \dots\) forever, with a flat continuously compounded yield \(y\). Compute its price, duration and convexity.

Solution. With \(x = e^{-y}\): price \(P = \sum_{n\geq1}x^n = \frac{x}{1 - x} = \frac{1}{e^y - 1}\). Duration \(D = \frac1P\sum nx^n = \frac{1}{1 - x} = \frac{e^y}{e^y - 1}\) (also \(-P'(y)/P\)). Convexity \(\frac1P\sum n^2x^n = \frac{1 + x}{(1 - x)^2}\). For \(y = 5\%\): \(P = 19.50\), \(D = 20.50\) years, \(C = 820\).

(Vasiček bond price, directly) Show that \(\int_t^Tr_u\,du\) is Gaussian in the Vasiček model and recover \(P_t(T) = e^{A(\tau) - B(\tau)r_t}\) from \(\mathbb E^Q[e^{-\int_t^Tr_u\,du}\mid r_t]\).

Then \(\mathbb E[e^{-X}] = e^{-m + v/2}\), which simplifies to \(e^{A(\tau) - B(\tau)r_t}\).

The Black–Scholes model takes the interest rate as a known constant. In reality rates move unpredictably, creating **interest rate risk** — and fixed-income derivatives make up about 80% of the OTC derivatives market. This chapter defines the various interest rates and how they are built from zero-coupon bond prices, prices the linear products (FRAs, floating-rate bonds, swaps) by pure no-arbitrage arguments, introduces the optional ones (caps, floors, swaptions), and then models the short rate with an SDE. Hedging arguments lead to the term-structure PDE and its Feynman–Kač solution; affine models (Vasiček, Cox–Ingersoll–Ross, Hull–White) make bond prices explicit.

## Interest rates in the market

Several rates coexist:

- **Treasury rates**: the rate at which a government borrows in its own currency; traditionally "risk-free", but not really (sovereign spreads).
- **Interbank rates** (Euribor; formerly Libor, discontinued on 1 July 2023): the rate at which major banks lend to each other for a given **tenor** (1, 3, 6, 12 months); reference rates for an enormous volume of loans and derivatives.
- **Overnight rates**: banks adjust their central-bank reserves by lending and borrowing overnight (€STR in the euro area, the Federal Funds rate in the US).
- **Repo rates**: rates on secured borrowing (repurchase agreements), e.g. SOFR in the US.

The rate applicable to a loan depends on **credit risk**, the risk that the borrower defaults. A truly risk-free rate does not exist, but derivative pricing needs one; **overnight rates are used as proxies** (Chapter 9).

## Zero-coupon bonds and forward rates

The fundamental purpose of the risk-free rate is to measure the **value of time**. Let $P_t(T)$ be the value at $t$ of one unit of currency delivered at $T \geq t$ without risk — the price of a **zero-coupon bond** (ZCB), with $P_T(T) = 1$. The **term structure** at $t$ is the curve $T \mapsto P_t(T)$; modelling it means describing its random evolution over time.

::: {.definition name="Interest rates from bond prices"}
For $t \leq S < T$:

- **simple spot rate** for $[S, T]$: $\displaystyle L_S(S, T) = \frac{1}{T - S}\Bigl(\frac{1}{P_S(T)} - 1\Bigr)$, the rate for lending at $S$ until $T$;
- **simple forward rate** for $[S, T]$ contracted at $t$: $\displaystyle L_t(S, T) = \frac{1}{T - S}\Bigl(\frac{P_t(S)}{P_t(T)} - 1\Bigr)$;
- **continuously compounded forward rate**: $\displaystyle F_t(S, T) = -\frac{\log P_t(T) - \log P_t(S)}{T - S}$;
- **instantaneous forward rate**: $\displaystyle f_t(T) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\log P_t(T) = \lim_{S\to T}F_t(S, T)$, the rate contracted at $t$ for $[T, T + dT]$;
- **short rate**: $r_t = f_t(t)$, the rate for $[t, t + dt]$.
:::

Consequently $P_t(T) = \exp\bigl(-\int_t^Tf_t(u)\,du\bigr)$.

*Why the forward rate formula.* At $t$, buy $P_t(S)/P_t(T)$ ZCBs maturing at $T$ and sell one ZCB maturing at $S$: zero cost. At $S$ pay $1$; at $T$ receive $P_t(S)/P_t(T)$. The rate for $[S, T]$ has been locked in at $t$: $1 + (T - S)L_t(S, T) = P_t(S)/P_t(T)$.

The **bank account** pays the short rate continuously: $dB_t = r_tB_t\,dt$, $B_0 = 1$, i.e. $B_t = \exp\bigl(\int_0^tr_u\,du\bigr)$ — the value of rolling over, at each instant, an investment in ZCBs maturing at $t + dt$. It is *locally* riskless. One can model the ZCB prices $P_t(T)$, the forward rates $f_t(T)$ (the Heath–Jarrow–Morton approach), the simple forward rates $L_t(S, T)$ (LIBOR market models), or the short rate $r_t$ and deduce everything else by no arbitrage. This chapter follows the last route.

## Linear interest rate products

### Forward rate agreements

Rates change: the 1-year Euribor fixed in one year, $L_1(1, 2)$, may differ from today's $L_0(0, 1)$. A borrower paying a floating rate faces interest rate risk.

::: {.definition name="Forward rate agreement"}
A **FRA** for the period $[S, T]$ with fixed rate $K$ and notional $N$ exchanges at $T$ the floating rate $L_S(S, T)$ (received, the *floating leg*) against $K$ (paid, the *fixed leg*):
$$\text{payoff at } T = N(T - S)\bigl(L_S(S, T) - K\bigr).$$
:::

::: {.example name="Hedging a floating loan"}
Today you arrange to borrow 10 000 € in one year, repaying in two years at the 1-year Euribor fixed in one year. A FRA on $[1, 2]$ with notional 10 000 € and rate $K$ pays $10\,000\,L_1(1, 2)$ against $10\,000\,K$: the net cost of the loan is $10\,000\,K$, fixed today.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Value of a FRA"}
For $t \leq S$ (unit notional),
$$\Pi^{FRA}_t = P_t(S) - \bigl(1 + (T - S)K\bigr)P_t(T) = (T - S)\bigl(L_t(S, T) - K\bigr)P_t(T).$$
The FRA has zero value iff $K = L_t(S, T)$: the forward rate plays the role of the forward price.
:::

*Proof.* The payoff $(T - S)(L_S(S, T) - K) = \frac{1}{P_S(T)} - 1 - (T - S)K$ is known at $S$, so its value at $S$ is $P_S(T)$ times it: $1 - (1 + (T - S)K)P_S(T)$ — one ZCB maturing at $S$ minus $1 + (T - S)K$ ZCBs maturing at $T$. By the law of one price this holds at every $t \leq S$. $\square$

### Floating-rate bonds and swaps


*Figure: payment schedule. The floating rate for $[T_{k-1}, T_k]$ is fixed at $T_{k-1}$ and paid at $T_k$.*

::: {.definition name="Floating-rate bond"}
A floating-rate bond with dates $0 \leq T_0 < T_1 < \dots < T_N$ pays at each $T_k$ the coupon $c_k = (T_k - T_{k-1})\bigl(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) + \alpha\bigr)$, with a fixed **spread** $\alpha$ (in basis points), plus the principal $1$ at $T_N$.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Price of a floating-rate bond"}
$$\Pi_0(\alpha) = P_0(T_0) + \alpha\sum_{k=1}^N(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k).$$
In particular, a floater with zero spread is worth exactly $P_0(T_0)$: par at the fixing date.
:::

*Proof.* Split each floating coupon as $(T_k - T_{k-1})\bigl(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) - L_0(T_{k-1}, T_k)\bigr) + (T_k - T_{k-1})L_0(T_{k-1}, T_k)$. The first part is a FRA struck at the forward rate, worth zero. The second is a known amount paid at $T_k$, worth $\bigl(\frac{P_0(T_{k-1})}{P_0(T_k)} - 1\bigr)P_0(T_k) = P_0(T_{k-1}) - P_0(T_k)$. Summing telescopes to $P_0(T_0) - P_0(T_N)$. The spreads are worth $\alpha\sum_k(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k)$, and the principal $P_0(T_N)$. $\square$

::: {.definition name="Interest rate swap"}
An **IRS** with dates $T_0 < \dots < T_N$ and fixed rate $S$ exchanges at each $T_k$ the net amount $(T_k - T_{k-1})\bigl(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) - S\bigr)$ (payer swap: receive floating, pay fixed). It is a portfolio of $N$ FRAs with common rate $K = S$.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Swap rate"}
The fixed rate making the swap worthless at $t = 0$ is
$$S(T_0, \dots, T_N) = \frac{P_0(T_0) - P_0(T_N)}{\sum_{k=1}^N(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k)} .$$
The denominator $A_0 = \sum_k(T_k - T_{k-1})P_0(T_k)$ is the **annuity** (or PVBP).
:::

*Proof.* The swap's cash flows are those of a floater with spread $\alpha = -S$ minus the principal, worth $\Pi_0(-S) - P_0(T_N) = P_0(T_0) - P_0(T_N) - S\,A_0$; set it to zero. $\square$

For example, with a flat zero curve at 3% (continuous) and annual payments over 5 years, $S = (1 - e^{-0.15})/\sum_{k=1}^5e^{-0.03k} = 3.045\%$ — the annually compounded equivalent of 3%.

## Options on rates: caps, floors, swaptions

::: {.definition name="Caplets, caps, floorlets, floors"}
A **caplet** on $[S, T]$ with strike $K$ pays $(T - S)\bigl(L_S(S, T) - K\bigr)^+$ at $T$; a **floorlet** pays $(T - S)\bigl(K - L_S(S, T)\bigr)^+$. A **cap** (**floor**) with dates $T_0, \dots, T_N$ is the sum of the caplets (floorlets) on $[T_{k-1}, T_k]$.
:::

A borrower paying a floating rate who buys a cap never pays more than $K$; a lender receiving floating who buys a floor never receives less than $K$. A caplet is an option on a FRA, a cap a portfolio of caplets.

::: {.proposition name="Caplets are puts on bonds"}
The caplet is worth, at time $S$,
$$\Pi^{cpl}_S = \bigl(1 + (T - S)K\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{1 + (T - S)K} - P_S(T)\Bigr)^+,$$
i.e. $1 + (T - S)K$ European **puts** with maturity $S$ on the ZCB maturing at $T$, with strike $1/(1 + (T - S)K)$. Similarly a floorlet is a portfolio of **calls** on a ZCB.
:::

*Proof.* $(T - S)(L_S(S,T) - K)^+ = \bigl(\frac{1}{P_S(T)} - (1 + (T - S)K)\bigr)^+$ is known at $S$; multiplying by $P_S(T)$ gives the value at $S$: $\bigl(1 - (1 + (T - S)K)P_S(T)\bigr)^+$. $\square$

::: {.definition name="Swaption"}
A (payer) **swaption** gives the right to enter at $T_0$ into the swap with fixed rate $S$. Its payoff at $T_0$ is
$$\bigl(S(T_0, \dots, T_N)_{T_0} - S\bigr)^+\sum_{k=1}^N(T_k - T_{k-1})P_{T_0}(T_k),$$
where $S(T_0, \dots, T_N)_{T_0}$ is the swap rate prevailing at $T_0$: the swap is entered only if the market rate exceeds $S$.
:::

Equivalently a swaption is an option on a portfolio of ZCBs, i.e. on a coupon bond. Their market pricing is described below.

### Market practice: Black's formula

Traders quote caplets and swaptions through **Black's (1976) formula**, a log-normal model for the *underlying rate* rather than for the short rate. For a caplet on $[T_{k-1}, T_k]$ with accrual $\tau_k$, strike $K$, forward rate $F_k = L_0(T_{k-1}, T_k)$ and volatility $\sigma_k$:
$$\mathrm{Cpl}_0 = N\tau_kP_0(T_k)\bigl[F_k\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(F_k/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma_k^2T_{k-1}}{\sigma_k\sqrt{T_{k-1}}}.$$
For a payer swaption on a swap starting at $T_0$, with forward swap rate $S_0 = S(T_0, \dots, T_N)$ and annuity $A_0 = \sum_k\tau_kP_0(T_k)$:
$$\mathrm{Swpt}_0 = N A_0\bigl[S_0\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)\bigr], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(S_0/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2T_0}{\sigma\sqrt{T_0}}.$$

These are not ad hoc. Under the **$T_k$-forward measure** $Q^{T_k}$ — the probability with numeraire $P_t(T_k)$ instead of the bank account — the forward rate $L_t(T_{k-1}, T_k) = \frac{1}{\tau_k}\bigl(\frac{P_t(T_{k-1})}{P_t(T_k)} - 1\bigr)$ is a ratio of a traded asset to the numeraire, hence a martingale, and
$$\mathrm{Cpl}_0 = N\tau_kP_0(T_k)\,\mathbb E^{Q^{T_k}}\bigl[(L_{T_{k-1}}(T_{k-1}, T_k) - K)^+\bigr].$$
If the forward rate is log-normal under $Q^{T_k}$, this is exactly Black's formula (the LIBOR market model makes this assumption for all forward rates simultaneously). Likewise the swap rate is a martingale under the **annuity measure** with numeraire $A_t$. Caps are then quoted by a single **flat volatility** applied to all their caplets, or by caplet-specific **spot volatilities**. When rates can be negative, the **Bachelier (normal) model**, $dF = \sigma_N\,dW$, replaces the log-normal one.

## Short-rate models

Model the short rate under $\mathbb P$ by
$$dr_t = \mu(t, r_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, r_t)\,dW_t .$$
ZCBs are taken as the fundamental traded assets, for every maturity $T$. Since $r$ is Markov, look for prices of the form $P_t(T) = F^T(t, r_t)$.

### The term-structure PDE

1. **Portfolio of two bonds.** Fix maturities $S < T$ and a self-financing $\theta = (\theta^S, \theta^T)$ in the two ZCBs: $V_t = \theta^S_tP_t(S) + \theta^T_tP_t(T)$, $dV_t = \theta^S_t\,dP_t(S) + \theta^T_t\,dP_t(T)$, where by Itô's formula
$$dF^T(t, r_t) = \Bigl(F^T_t + \mu F^T_r + \tfrac12\sigma^2F^T_{rr}\Bigr)dt + \sigma F^T_r\,dW_t .$$
2. **Make it riskless.** Choose $\theta^S_t = -\theta^T_tF^T_r/F^S_r$, which kills the $dW_t$ term.
3. **No arbitrage.** A riskless portfolio must earn the short rate: $dV_t = r_tV_t\,dt$. After simplification,
$$\frac{F^T_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2F^T_{rr} - rF^T}{F^T_r} = \frac{F^S_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2F^S_{rr} - rF^S}{F^S_r}.$$
4. **Separation.** Since $S$ and $T$ are arbitrary, both sides equal a function $q(t, r_t)$ independent of maturity. Writing $q = \lambda\sigma - \mu$:

::: {.theorem name="Term-structure PDE"}
For every $T > 0$,
$$F^T_t + (\mu - \lambda\sigma)F^T_r + \frac12\sigma^2F^T_{rr} - rF^T = 0, \qquad F^T(T, r) = 1,$$
where $\lambda(t, r)$ is the **market price of interest rate risk**.
:::

Why does $\lambda$ appear? In BSM there is one source of risk and one exogenously given risky asset to hedge it; here the only exogenous asset is the bank account, and the short rate is *not* a traded asset. The real-world dynamics of $r$ alone do not determine bond prices: one must also know $\lambda$, which is set by the market — in practice, by calibration.

::: {.theorem name="Feynman–Kač with stochastic rates"}
The solution of the term-structure PDE is
$$P_t(T) = F^T(t, r_t) = \mathbb E^Q\Bigl[e^{-\int_t^Tr_u\,du}\Bigm|r_t\Bigr],$$
where under $Q$ the short rate follows $dr_t = \bigl(\mu - \lambda\sigma\bigr)(t, r_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, r_t)\,dW^Q_t$.
:::

Under this **risk-neutral** probability every ZCB has expected return $r_t$. Prices depend on $\mu$ and $\lambda$ only through the **risk-neutral drift** $\mu^Q = \mu - \lambda\sigma = -q$. Hence the usual practice of **risk-neutral modelling**: specify $r$ directly under $Q$ and calibrate its parameters to market prices.

### Affine models

::: {.definition name="Affine short-rate model"}
A model $dr_t = \mu^Q(t, r_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, r_t)\,dW^Q_t$ is **affine** if
$$\mu^Q(t, r) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1r, \qquad \sigma^2(t, r) = \beta_0 + \beta_1r .$$
:::

Try the exponential-affine ansatz $F^T(t, r) = \exp\bigl(A(T - t) - B(T - t)r\bigr)$. Substituting into the PDE (with $\tau = T - t$, so $\partial_t = -\partial_\tau$),
$$-A'(\tau) - \bigl(1 - B'(\tau)\bigr)r - (\alpha_0 + \alpha_1r)B(\tau) + \tfrac12(\beta_0 + \beta_1r)B(\tau)^2 = 0 .$$
This must hold for all $r$, so the constant and the coefficient of $r$ vanish separately:
$$B'(\tau) = 1 + \alpha_1B(\tau) - \tfrac12\beta_1B(\tau)^2, \qquad A'(\tau) = -\alpha_0B(\tau) + \tfrac12\beta_0B(\tau)^2, \qquad A(0) = B(0) = 0 .$$
The equation for $B$ is a **Riccati ODE**; $A$ then follows by integration. The PDE has been reduced to ODEs, solvable explicitly in the classical cases.

### Vasiček

::: {.definition name="Vasiček (1977)"}
$$dr_t = \theta(\kappa - r_t)\,dt + \gamma\,dW^Q_t,$$
with speed of mean reversion $\theta$, long-run mean $\kappa$, volatility $\gamma$.
:::

This is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Chapter 4):
$$r_t = r_0e^{-\theta t} + \kappa(1 - e^{-\theta t}) + \gamma\int_0^te^{-\theta(t-u)}dW^Q_u,$$
Gaussian with $\mathbb E^Q[r_t] = r_0e^{-\theta t} + \kappa(1 - e^{-\theta t})$ and $\mathrm{Var}^Q(r_t) = \frac{\gamma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})$. The rate can become negative — once seen as a defect, but a feature after 2014. Here $\alpha_0 = \theta\kappa$, $\alpha_1 = -\theta$, $\beta_0 = \gamma^2$, $\beta_1 = 0$, and
$$B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\theta\tau}}{\theta}, \qquad A(\tau) = \Bigl(\kappa - \frac{\gamma^2}{2\theta^2}\Bigr)\bigl(B(\tau) - \tau\bigr) - \frac{\gamma^2}{4\theta}B(\tau)^2 .$$
(The course slides print $\kappa - \gamma^2/2$ in the first factor; the correct factor is $\kappa - \gamma^2/(2\theta^2)$.) Caplets and floorlets have Black–Scholes-like closed forms, since bond prices are log-normal.

### Cox–Ingersoll–Ross

::: {.definition name="Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (1985)"}
$$dr_t = \theta(\kappa - r_t)\,dt + \gamma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW^Q_t .$$
:::

The rate stays non-negative, and strictly positive if $2\kappa\theta \geq \gamma^2$ (**Feller condition**). $r_t$ follows a non-central chi-square distribution, with the same mean as in Vasiček and variance
$$\mathrm{Var}^Q(r_t) = r_0\frac{\gamma^2}{\theta}\bigl(e^{-\theta t} - e^{-2\theta t}\bigr) + \frac{\kappa\gamma^2}{2\theta}\bigl(1 - e^{-\theta t}\bigr)^2 .$$
It is the same SDE as the variance in the Heston model. With $h = \sqrt{\theta^2 + 2\gamma^2}$,
$$B(\tau) = \frac{2(e^{h\tau} - 1)}{2h + (\theta + h)(e^{h\tau} - 1)}, \qquad A(\tau) = \frac{2\kappa\theta}{\gamma^2}\log\frac{2he^{(\theta + h)\tau/2}}{2h + (\theta + h)(e^{h\tau} - 1)} .$$
Caplets and floorlets are semi-explicit.

::: {.example name="Two models, one bond"}
With $r_0 = 3\%$, $\theta = 0.5$, $\kappa = 4\%$ and $T = 5$: Vasiček with $\gamma = 1\%$ gives $P_0(5) = 0.8343$ (yield $3.62\%$); CIR with $\gamma = 5\%$ (Feller holds: $0.04 > 0.0025$) gives $P_0(5) = 0.8342$, confirmed by a Monte Carlo simulation ($0.8342$). The yield lies between $r_0$ and $\kappa$, pulled down slightly by the convexity term $-\gamma^2/(2\theta^2)$.
:::

### Hull–White: fitting today's curve

Vasiček and CIR have three or four parameters and cannot reproduce an arbitrary observed term structure. The **Hull–White** model (extended Vasiček) makes the drift time-dependent:
$$dr_t = \bigl(\alpha(t) - \theta r_t\bigr)dt + \gamma\,dW^Q_t,$$
still Gaussian and mean-reverting, with $\mathbb E^Q[r_t] = r_0e^{-\theta t} + \int_0^te^{-\theta(t-u)}\alpha(u)\,du$. It is affine with $B(\tau) = (1 - e^{-\theta\tau})/\theta$ as in Vasiček and
$$A(T - t) = \int_t^T\Bigl(\frac{\gamma^2}{2}B(T - u)^2 - \alpha(u)B(T - u)\Bigr)du .$$
The model forward curve is $f_0(T) = -\partial_T\log P_0(T) = -\partial_TA(T) + \partial_TB(T)\,r_0$. Requiring $f_0(T) = f^{\mathrm{mkt}}_0(T)$ for all $T$ determines $\alpha$ uniquely:
$$\alpha(T) = \frac{\partial}{\partial T}f^{\mathrm{mkt}}_0(T) + \theta f^{\mathrm{mkt}}_0(T) + \frac{\gamma^2}{2\theta}\bigl(1 - e^{-2\theta T}\bigr).$$
The model then reproduces today's curve exactly by construction, and $\theta$, $\gamma$ are calibrated to option prices (caplets, swaptions), which also have closed forms.

::: {.callout-note}
Short-rate models are the natural first step but have limitations: one factor implies perfectly correlated rates of all maturities. Richer approaches model the whole forward curve (Heath–Jarrow–Morton) or the market forward rates directly (LIBOR/forward market models). After 2008 the spread between interbank and overnight rates forced a **multi-curve** framework, and the 2023 end of Libor moved markets to backward-looking risk-free rates — the subject of Chapter 9.
:::

## Exercises

Problems from Hull (H), Chapters 7, 29 and 31, and Björk (B), Chapters 22–23.

::: {.exercise}
**(B 22.1)** In a FRA with continuous compounding, the lender pays $K$ at $S$ and receives $Ke^{R^*(T - S)}$ at $T$. (a) Value the contract at $t < S$ in terms of ZCB prices. (b) Show that it is worth zero iff $R^*$ equals the forward rate.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $\Pi_t = -KP_t(S) + Ke^{R^*(T-S)}P_t(T)$. (b) $\Pi_t = 0$ iff $e^{R^*(T-S)} = P_t(S)/P_t(T)$, i.e. $R^* = -\frac{\log P_t(T) - \log P_t(S)}{T - S} = F_t(S, T)$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 7.2)** A swap on $100$ million has 10 months to run: six-month Libor is received against $4\%$ (semiannual) paid. Forward Libor rates for all maturities are $3\%$ (semiannual); the Libor fixed two months ago was $2.4\%$; OIS rates are $2.7\%$ (continuous) for discounting. Value the swap for both parties.
:::

::: {.solution}
Payments in 4 and 10 months. Fixed leg: $2$m each. Floating: $0.5 \times 2.4\% \times 100 = 1.2$m (already fixed) and $1.5$m (at the forward rate). To the party paying floating and receiving fixed: $(2 - 1.2)e^{-0.027/3} + (2 - 1.5)e^{-0.027\times10/12} = 1.282$m. To the party paying fixed: $-1.282$m.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 29.1)** A company caps 3-month Libor at $2\%$ on $20$ million. At a reset date the rate is $4\%$. What payment results, and when?
:::

::: {.solution}
$20\,000\,000 \times 0.25 \times (0.04 - 0.02) = 100\,000$, paid three months after the reset date (at the end of the accrual period).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 29.5)** Price a caplet on the 3-month rate for the period from 15 to 18 months, struck at $13\%$ (quarterly compounding), on a principal of $1000$. The forward rate is $12\%$, the 18-month zero rate is $11.5\%$ (continuous), and the forward-rate volatility is $12\%$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Black's formula with $F = 0.12$, $K = 0.13$, $T_{k-1} = 1.25$, $\sigma = 0.12$: $d_1 = \frac{\log(12/13) + \frac12\,0.12^2\times1.25}{0.12\sqrt{1.25}} = -0.5295$, $d_2 = -0.6637$, so $F\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2) = 0.12\times0.2982 - 0.13\times0.2534 = 0.00284$. The price is $1000 \times 0.25 \times e^{-0.115\times1.5} \times 0.00284 = 0.60$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 29.14)** Value a European swaption giving the right to enter in 4 years a 3-year annual-pay swap paying $5\%$ fixed on $10$ million. All swap rates are $5\%$, OIS rates $4.7\%$ (continuous), swap-rate volatility $20\%$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Annuity $A = e^{-0.047\times5} + e^{-0.047\times6} + e^{-0.047\times7} = 2.2645$. At the money ($S_0 = K = 5\%$): $d_{1,2} = \pm\tfrac12\times0.2\times2 = \pm0.2$, and the swaption is worth $10^7 \times 2.2645 \times 0.05\,(\Phi(0.2) - \Phi(-0.2)) = 179\,500$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 29.11, B 23.3)** (a) Derive a put–call parity for swaptions. (b) Find the fixed rate $R$ that makes a contract exchanging $K(e^{RT} - 1)$ against $K(e^{\int_0^Tr_s\,ds} - 1)$ at $T$ worthless.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Payer minus receiver swaption with the same strike $K$ pays $(S_{T_0} - K)^+A_{T_0} - (K - S_{T_0})^+A_{T_0} = (S_{T_0} - K)A_{T_0}$, the value of a forward-starting payer swap at rate $K$: $\mathrm{Payer} - \mathrm{Receiver} = A_0(S_0 - K)$. (b) The value is $K\bigl(e^{RT}P_0(T) - \mathbb E^Q[e^{-\int_0^Tr}e^{\int_0^Tr}]\bigr) = K\bigl(e^{RT}P_0(T) - 1\bigr)$, zero iff $R = -\log P_0(T)/T$: the $T$-year zero rate, whatever the short-rate model.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 31.4)** In the risk-neutral Vasiček model with speed $0.1$, level $0.03$ and volatility $0.01$, price a 5-year ZCB with principal $1$ when $r_0 = 2\%$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$B(5) = (1 - e^{-0.5})/0.1 = 3.935$, $A(5) = (0.03 - 0.01^2/0.02)(3.935 - 5) - 0.01^2\times3.935^2/0.4 = -0.03050$, so $P = e^{A - Br_0} = e^{-0.03050 - 0.0787} = 0.8966$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 31.9)** In both Vasiček and CIR take speed $0.1$, level $0.1$, $r_0 = 10\%$, and an initial short-rate volatility of $2\%$ (so $\gamma = 0.02$ in Vasiček and $\gamma\sqrt{0.1} = 0.02$ in CIR). Compare the 10-year ZCB prices.
:::

::: {.solution}
Vasiček: $0.3805$. CIR with $\gamma = 0.0632$: $0.3799$. The models agree closely when calibrated to the same initial volatility; they differ more for long maturities and for distributions of future rates (CIR cannot go negative; its volatility falls as rates fall).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 22.5)** Show that $f(0, T) = y(0, T) + T\,\partial_Ty(0, T)$, where $y$ is the zero-coupon yield, and that for an increasing yield curve $y_M(0, T) \leq y(0, T) \leq f(0, T)$, $y_M$ being the yield of a coupon bond. Interpret.
:::

::: {.solution}
$P(0, T) = e^{-Ty(0,T)}$, so $f = -\partial_T\log P = y + Ty'$. If $y' \geq 0$, then $f \geq y$. A coupon bond's yield is a weighted average of the zero yields at its payment dates, all $\leq T$, so $y_M \leq y(0, T)$. The forward rate is the *marginal* rate at $T$, the zero yield the *average* over $[0, T]$, and the coupon yield a further average — marginal above average when the curve rises.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 22.7)** A consol pays $1$ at $t = 1, 2, \dots$ forever, with a flat continuously compounded yield $y$. Compute its price, duration and convexity.
:::

::: {.solution}
With $x = e^{-y}$: price $P = \sum_{n\geq1}x^n = \frac{x}{1 - x} = \frac{1}{e^y - 1}$. Duration $D = \frac1P\sum nx^n = \frac{1}{1 - x} = \frac{e^y}{e^y - 1}$ (also $-P'(y)/P$). Convexity $\frac1P\sum n^2x^n = \frac{1 + x}{(1 - x)^2}$. For $y = 5\%$: $P = 19.50$, $D = 20.50$ years, $C = 820$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Vasiček bond price, directly)** Show that $\int_t^Tr_u\,du$ is Gaussian in the Vasiček model and recover $P_t(T) = e^{A(\tau) - B(\tau)r_t}$ from $\mathbb E^Q[e^{-\int_t^Tr_u\,du}\mid r_t]$.
:::

::: {.solution}
It is a linear functional of a Gaussian process, with mean $m = \kappa\tau + (r_t - \kappa)B(\tau)$ and variance $v = \frac{\gamma^2}{\theta^2}\bigl(\tau - 2B(\tau) + \frac{1 - e^{-2\theta\tau}}{2\theta}\bigr)$, $\tau = T - t$. Then $\mathbb E[e^{-X}] = e^{-m + v/2}$, which simplifies to $e^{A(\tau) - B(\tau)r_t}$.
:::
