9  Interest rate modelling

Modelling interest rates is at once the oldest and the most subtle problem in derivative pricing. Rates are the numéraire, so their model matters even if you only price equity options. This chapter surveys the classical one-factor short-rate models, the HJM framework, LIBOR/SOFR market models, and the post-2008 multi-curve reality.

9.1 The short rate

The short rate \(r_t\) is the annualised interest rate applicable to an instantaneous loan at time \(t\). The money-market account grows as \(dB_t = r_t B_t\,dt\), so \(B_t = \exp\bigl(\int_0^t r_s\,ds\bigr)\).

The zero-coupon bond \(P(t, T)\) is the value at time \(t\) of receiving 1 at time \(T\). Under the risk-neutral measure with numéraire \(B\), \[P(t, T) = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\bigl[e^{-\int_t^T r_s\,ds}\bigm|\mathcal F_t\bigr] .\] The yield is \(y(t, T) = -\frac{1}{T - t}\ln P(t, T)\); the forward rate is \(f(t, T) = -\partial_T \ln P(t, T)\).

9.2 Vasicek and CIR models

The two classic one-factor short-rate models:

The Vasicek model posits an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck short rate under \(\mathbb Q\): \[dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t^{\mathbb Q} .\] The short rate is Gaussian and mean-reverting; unfortunately it can go negative.

Under Vasicek, \[P(t, T) = A(t, T)\exp(-B(t, T)r_t),\] with \(B(t, T) = (1 - e^{-\kappa(T - t)})/\kappa\) and \(A(t, T)\) an explicit function of \(\kappa, \theta, \sigma\). The forward-rate curve is \[f(t, T) = e^{-\kappa(T - t)}r_t + \theta(1 - e^{-\kappa(T - t)}) - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}(1 - e^{-\kappa(T - t)})^2 .\]

The CIR model solves the negative-rate problem with a square-root diffusion: \[dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t^{\mathbb Q} .\] For \(2\kappa\theta \geq \sigma^2\) (Feller condition) the process stays strictly positive. Zero-coupon bond prices are still affine in \(r_t\).

Remark. Since 2015 several major currencies (EUR, JPY, CHF) have exhibited negative short rates. The Vasicek model, once considered defective for allowing negative rates, is now correct in this respect; the CIR model, once considered “safe”, is inconsistent with observed data.

9.3 The HJM framework

Rather than model the short rate, Heath–Jarrow–Morton (1992) model the entire forward-rate curve \(\{f(t, T) : T \geq t\}\) directly:

Under HJM, the forward-rate curve evolves as \[df(t, T) = \alpha(t, T)\,dt + \boldsymbol\sigma(t, T)^\top\,d\mathbf W_t,\] with \(d\mathbf W_t\) a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion. HJM is the framework in which every interest-rate model can be stated.

For the discounted bond prices to be martingales, the drift \(\alpha\) is not free but determined by \(\boldsymbol\sigma\): \[\alpha(t, T) = \boldsymbol\sigma(t, T)^\top\int_t^T \boldsymbol\sigma(t, u)\,du .\] This is the celebrated HJM drift condition: choosing the volatility structure \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) fully specifies the model.

9.4 LIBOR market model

For pricing swaptions and caps, the natural quantity is not the instantaneous forward rate but the forward LIBOR \(L(t; T_i, T_{i+1})\) over a discrete tenor.

Under the \(T_{i+1}\)-forward measure, each forward LIBOR \(L_i(t)\) evolves as a log-normal: \[dL_i(t) = \sigma_i(t) L_i(t)\,dW_t^{T_{i+1}} .\] Different \(L_i\) are driven by correlated Brownians. The joint dynamics is determined by the volatility profile \(\{\sigma_i\}\) and the correlation matrix.

Under LMM, a caplet payoff \((L(T_i; T_i, T_{i+1}) - K)^+\) is priced by Black’s formula: \[Caplet_0 = P(0, T_{i+1})\bigl[L_i(0)\Phi(d_1) - K \Phi(d_2)\bigr]\cdot \delta_i,\] with \(d_{1,2}\) as in BSM but on the forward rate.

9.5 Post-2008: multi-curve

Before 2008, the market used a single “zero curve” for both discounting and forward-rate projection. The LIBOR-OIS basis blew out during the crisis (LIBOR reflects bank credit risk; OIS is closer to risk-free), and the market switched to a multi-curve framework:

  • Discount curve. Built from OIS swap rates; used to discount all cash flows.
  • Projection curves. One per LIBOR tenor (1M, 3M, 6M, 12M); used to project future coupons.

The two are related but not identical, and their basis (LIBOR – OIS) is stochastic and priced separately.

Remark (After LIBOR). LIBOR is being phased out globally (2021–2023). Replacement rates are: SOFR (USD), €STR (EUR), SONIA (GBP), SARON (CHF), TONAR (JPY). Each is an overnight rate averaged over accrual periods — a very different animal from term LIBOR. The transition has forced a repricing of trillions of dollars of derivatives.

9.6 RFRs and backwards-looking rates

An RFR is an overnight interest rate benchmark based on actual transactions (SOFR is the volume-weighted median of overnight repos on US Treasury collateral). Unlike LIBOR, RFRs are backward-looking: the rate for a period \([T_{i-1}, T_i]\) is \[R_i = \frac{1}{T_i - T_{i-1}}\int_{T_{i-1}}^{T_i} r_s\,ds .\] The rate is not known until \(T_i\).

A SOFR-based coupon over 3 months compounds daily overnight rates: \[R^{comp} = \prod_{k = 1}^{n}(1 + r_{t_k} \delta_k) - 1,\] paid at the end of the accrual period. The uncertainty until the last day makes hedging non-trivial and forces a partial lookback convention (paying a couple of days after \(T_i\)) in practice.

9.7 Exercises

Derive the ZCB price formula for the Vasicek model by direct calculation of \(\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[\exp(-\int_t^T r_s\,ds)]\).

Solution. Since \(r_s\) is Gaussian under Vasicek, \(\int_t^T r_s\,ds\) is Gaussian with mean \(\mu(T-t) + (r_t - \mu)B\) and variance \(\sigma^2\int_t^T B(s, T)^2\,ds\). The expectation of \(\exp\) of a Gaussian equals \(\exp\) of mean plus half variance. Substitute and identify with the affine form.

Show that in a flat, upward-sloping yield curve \(y(T) = y_0 + s T\) the forward rate is \(f(T) = y_0 + 2 s T\) — twice the slope of the yield curve.

Solution. \(P(0, T) = e^{-y(T)T} = e^{-(y_0 T + s T^2)}\), so \(-\ln P = y_0 T + s T^2\); forward rate \(f(T) = \partial_T(y_0 T + s T^2) = y_0 + 2sT\). This is the “forward slope is twice the yield slope” rule.

9.8 Callouts

Short-rate models are elegant but ill-suited to modern rate markets: they cannot fit the volatility surface of caps and swaptions jointly. LMM or HJM is required in practice.

When calibrating a rate model, always match the yield curve exactly first; a rate model that misprices vanilla bonds is worthless. Sensitivity to skew is a distant second-order concern.

The LIBOR transition is the biggest single change to derivative markets since Black–Scholes. Any code that still hard-codes LIBOR discounting is broken; every derivative pricer had to be rewritten in 2021–2023.

Negative rates break the CIR and log-normal LMM models. If you’re going to price European rate options in a negative-rate regime, use a normal (Bachelier) model on the forward rate rather than a log-normal.
