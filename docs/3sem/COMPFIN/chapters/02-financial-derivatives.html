3  Financial derivatives

A derivative is a contract whose payoff at maturity depends on the future value of some other observable — the underlying. This chapter introduces the classical derivatives (forwards, futures, options, swaps) and the four fundamental parity relations that any consistent pricing framework must respect.

3.1 Classification

A derivative is a contract \(C\) with a specified payoff at (or before) a maturity date \(T\), where the payoff is a function of the price path \(\{S_t : 0 \leq t \leq T\}\) of an underlying \(S\). The set of possible derivatives is enormous; the ones traded in liquid markets fall into a small number of families.

Remark (Terminology zoo). The two orthogonal axes of classification are the exercise style — European (only at \(T\)), American (any time \(\leq T\)), Bermudan (a finite set of dates) — and the payoff structure — vanilla (call or put), digital, barrier, Asian, look-back, etc. In these notes we focus on European vanilla until Chapter 6.

3.2 Forwards and futures

A forward is a bilateral contract to buy (long) or sell (short) an underlying \(S\) at time \(T\) at a pre-agreed forward price \(F\). The payoff at \(T\) for the long side is \[V_T^{\text{fwd}} = S_T - F.\]

Under no-arbitrage in a market with constant risk-free rate \(r\) and no dividends, \[F = S_0 e^{rT}.\] With continuous dividends at rate \(q\), \(F = S_0 e^{(r - q)T}\); with FX, replace \(q\) by the foreign interest rate \(r_f\).

A futures is an exchange-traded standardised forward with daily margining: gains and losses on the position are settled in cash every day. Because of margining the futures price is not identical to the forward price when interest rates are stochastic, but the difference is typically small (\(< 1\) basis point on liquid contracts).

3.3 Options

A European call on \(S\) with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) has payoff at \(T\) \[C_T = (S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0).\] A European put has payoff \(P_T = (K - S_T)^+\).

A call gives the buyer the right, not obligation to buy \(S\) at price \(K\) on date \(T\).

An investor buys a 6-month call on stock \(S\) with strike \(K = 100\), paying premium \(C_0 = 5\). At maturity, if \(S_T = 120\) the investor exercises: pay \(K = 100\), receive \(S_T = 120\), net gain \(= 20 - 5 = 15\) (excluding time value of premium). If \(S_T = 90\) the call expires worthless: loss = premium \(= 5\).

For European options on a non-dividend stock with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\), \[C_0 - P_0 = S_0 - K e^{-rT}.\] This is a model-free no-arbitrage identity: the two portfolios “long call + short put” and “long stock + short zero-coupon bond of face \(K\)” have identical payoffs at \(T\).

Remark. Put–call parity is one of the most useful identities in finance. It lets you infer put prices from call prices (or vice versa) without any pricing model, and it is the basis of conversion and reversal trading strategies used by market makers to lock in tiny mispricings.

3.4 Swaps

An interest-rate swap is a bilateral contract to exchange, on a schedule of dates \(T_1 < T_2 < \ldots < T_n\), a stream of fixed-rate cash flows for a stream of floating-rate cash flows on a notional amount \(N\).

The fixed-leg cash flows are \(N K \Delta_i\) at each \(T_i\) for some fixed rate \(K\); the floating-leg cash flows are \(N L(T_{i-1}, T_i) \Delta_i\) where \(L\) is a market-observed reference rate (formerly LIBOR, now SOFR/€STR).

The par swap rate — the fixed rate \(K^*\) that makes the initial value of the swap zero — is \[K^* = \frac{P(0, T_0) - P(0, T_n)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta_i\,P(0, T_i)},\] where \(P(0, T)\) is the discount factor to time \(T\).

3.5 Payoff diagrams and structured products

Complex payoffs are conventionally described by their payoff diagram — payoff on the vertical axis vs \(S_T\) on the horizontal.

A bull spread is long a call at strike \(K_1\) and short a call at strike \(K_2 > K_1\), both with maturity \(T\). Its payoff is \[V_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^+ = \min\bigl(\max(S_T - K_1, 0), K_2 - K_1\bigr).\] The buyer profits from a modest rise in \(S\) but caps the upside above \(K_2\). Cheaper than a single call.

A straddle is long a call and a put at the same strike \(K = S_0\). Payoff: \(|S_T - S_0|\). Profits from any large move in either direction, regardless of sign — a pure volatility bet.

3.6 Payoff decomposition and static hedging

Any twice-differentiable payoff \(g(S_T)\) can be replicated by a portfolio of a bond, a forward, and calls/puts of all strikes: \[g(S_T) = g(F) + g'(F)(S_T - F) + \int_0^F g''(K)(K - S_T)^+ dK + \int_F^\infty g''(K)(S_T - K)^+ dK ,\] where \(F\) is any reference level (e.g. the forward price).

The Carr–Madan formula is the fundamental theorem of option replication: it tells you the option surface is a complete basis for payoff functions. This is the mathematical reason why variance swaps and other volatility products can be replicated by static portfolios of vanilla calls and puts.

3.7 Option Greeks (preview)

The sensitivities of a derivative price to its inputs are called Greeks:

  • \(\Delta = \partial V/\partial S\): sensitivity to spot.
  • \(\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2\): convexity in spot.
  • \(\Theta = -\partial V/\partial t\): time decay.
  • \(\mathcal V = \partial V/\partial \sigma\): vega, sensitivity to volatility.
  • \(\rho = \partial V/\partial r\): sensitivity to interest rate.

Greeks are what traders quote and hedge, not prices. Chapter 5 computes them in closed form for the Black–Scholes model.

3.8 Exercises

Show that the payoff of a long forward at price \(F\) equals the payoff of a long call minus a long put at strike \(F\): \((S_T - F) = (S_T - F)^+ - (F - S_T)^+\).

Solution. Case 1: \(S_T > F\). Left: \(S_T - F > 0\). Right: \((S_T - F) - 0 = S_T - F\). ✓ Case 2: \(S_T < F\). Left: \(S_T - F < 0\). Right: \(0 - (F - S_T) = S_T - F\). ✓ So the identity holds for all \(S_T\). Rearranging is put–call parity in payoff form.

A European call with strike \(K = 100\) and maturity \(T = 1\) year trades at \(C_0 = 8\); the corresponding put trades at \(P_0 = 5\). The spot is \(S_0 = 102\). What is the implied risk-free rate?

Solution. Put–call parity: \(C_0 - P_0 = S_0 - K e^{-rT}\), so \(8 - 5 = 102 - 100 e^{-r}\), giving \(e^{-r} = 0.99\), i.e. \(r \approx 1.005\%\).

Use the Carr–Madan formula to replicate the log payoff \(\ln(S_T / F)\) with a portfolio of calls and puts.

Solution. \(g(S) = \ln(S/F)\): \(g(F) = 0\), \(g'(F) = 1/F\), \(g''(K) = -1/K^2\). The replication is \[\ln(S_T/F) = \frac{S_T - F}{F} - \int_0^F \frac{(K - S_T)^+}{K^2} dK - \int_F^\infty \frac{(S_T - K)^+}{K^2} dK.\] This is the variance-swap replication formula: the log payoff is the crucial primitive in variance-swap pricing.

3.9 Callouts

Put–call parity is model-free: it holds whatever the true price process. Every option-pricing model must respect it, and this is a useful sanity check on numerical implementations.

When designing a structured payoff, always draw the payoff diagram first. If the diagram looks “kinked” you need vanilla options; if it looks “smooth” you need a continuum of strikes.

American-style options cannot be replicated by static portfolios: their exercise boundary is stochastic. Chapters 3 and 5 briefly discuss the free-boundary problem for American options.

Options are quoted in implied volatility, not price. When a trader says “vol is up 2 vols” she means the Black–Scholes IV has moved by 200 bp — a bigger deal than a $2 change in spot.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Show that the payoff of a long forward at price $F$ equals the payoff of a long call minus a long put at strike $F$: $(S_T - F) = (S_T - F)^+ - (F - S_T)^+$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Case 1: $S_T > F$. Left: $S_T - F > 0$. Right: $(S_T - F) - 0 = S_T - F$. ✓
Case 2: $S_T < F$. Left: $S_T - F < 0$. Right: $0 - (F - S_T) = S_T - F$. ✓
So the identity holds for all $S_T$. Rearranging is put–call parity in payoff form.
:::

::: {.exercise}
A European call with strike $K = 100$ and maturity $T = 1$ year trades at $C_0 = 8$; the corresponding put trades at $P_0 = 5$. The spot is $S_0 = 102$. What is the implied risk-free rate?
:::

::: {.solution}
Put–call parity: $C_0 - P_0 = S_0 - K e^{-rT}$, so $8 - 5 = 102 - 100 e^{-r}$, giving $e^{-r} = 0.99$, i.e.\ $r \approx 1.005\%$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Use the Carr–Madan formula to replicate the log payoff $\ln(S_T / F)$ with a portfolio of calls and puts.
:::

::: {.solution}
$g(S) = \ln(S/F)$: $g(F) = 0$, $g'(F) = 1/F$, $g''(K) = -1/K^2$. The replication is
$$\ln(S_T/F) = \frac{S_T - F}{F} - \int_0^F \frac{(K - S_T)^+}{K^2} dK - \int_F^\infty \frac{(S_T - K)^+}{K^2} dK.$$
This is the *variance-swap replication* formula: the log payoff is the crucial primitive in variance-swap pricing.
:::
