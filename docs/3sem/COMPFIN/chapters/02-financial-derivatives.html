2  Financial derivatives

Derivatives are the central object of the course: contracts whose payoff depends on the value of an underlying asset. This chapter introduces the main types — forwards, futures and options, vanilla and exotic — and the three kinds of traders who use them. Its main tool is the no-arbitrage principle, which already yields a surprising number of exact results without any probabilistic model: the law of one price, forward and futures prices, put–call parity, model-free bounds on option prices, and the fact that an American call on a non-dividend stock should never be exercised early. Static option strategies close the chapter.

2.1 Hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs

Three broad categories of market participants trade derivatives:

  • hedgers reduce their exposure to adverse price movements (companies, banks, funds);
  • speculators take positions to bet on future price movements (banks, hedge funds, investors);
  • arbitrageurs look for riskless profits by combining transactions across counterparties or markets (banks, hedge funds).

An Italian textile company will receive 100k USD from a US client in six months. Today 1 USD = 0.86 EUR, but the future rate is unknown: the company is exposed to currency risk, and its business is textiles, not betting on exchange rates.

  • Forward contract. The company agrees today, at zero cost, to pay 100k USD in six months and receive 86k EUR. Its income is locked at 86k EUR whatever happens. The price of certainty: if the rate rises to 0.90, the company forgoes the 90k EUR it would have received.
  • Put option. For 2k EUR today, the company buys the right (not the obligation) to sell 100k USD for 86k EUR in six months. If the rate falls below 0.86 it exercises and receives 86k EUR; otherwise it lets the option expire and converts at the market rate. Its net income is at least \(86 - 2 = 84\)k EUR, with unlimited upside.

The forward eliminates risk in both directions for free; the option removes only the downside, at a price.

An investor believes the USD/EUR rate (now 0.86) will fall within six months.

  1. Borrow 100k USD, convert into 86k EUR. At 0.81 the debt is repaid with 81k EUR: profit 5k EUR. At 0.88 it costs 88k EUR: loss 2k EUR. Gains and losses are small relative to the volume, and a large credit line is needed.
  2. Forward contract to pay 100k USD and receive 86k EUR: the same profile, but with no cash flow today.
  3. Borrow 10k EUR and buy five puts (each on 100k USD at 0.86). At 0.81 the puts are exercised: buy 500k USD for 405k EUR, sell them for 430k EUR, gain 25k EUR, net 15k EUR after the premium. At 0.88 the puts expire worthless: loss 10k EUR.

Options amplify gains and losses (leverage); losses are limited to the premium, while gains are potentially unlimited.

A US bank pays 2% on 6-month USD deposits, a European bank lends EUR at 1% over 6 months, and the forward above (100k USD for 86k EUR) is available in any quantity. Today:

  • borrow 86k EUR, convert into 100k USD, deposit them in the US;
  • enter into 1.02 forward contracts.

Net cost today: zero. In six months the deposit holds 102k USD; the forwards convert them into 87 720 EUR; repaying the loan costs 86 860 EUR. The riskless profit is 860 EUR, whatever the exchange rate does.

This is an arbitrage opportunity: zero initial cost, no risk of loss, a positive profit with positive probability. Scaling all transactions by 100 yields 86k EUR. In reality there are transaction costs and some default risk, and above all every market participant would exploit the opportunity, moving prices through supply and demand until it disappears. If arbitrage opportunities ever exist, they vanish almost immediately.

2.2 General features and the no-arbitrage principle

Every derivative has a maturity \(T\) (the termination date), an underlying (usually a traded asset: a stock, an index, a currency), a payoff (the cash flow it delivers, as a function of the underlying, usually at maturity) and a price (its market value before maturity). We denote by \(S_t\) the price of the underlying at time \(t\); for \(T > t\), \(S_T\) is unknown at \(t\), i.e. random. \(t\) denotes a generic valuation date.

An arbitrage opportunity is a trading strategy with zero initial cost, no risk of losses at any time, and a strictly positive profit with positive probability at some future time.

The financial market admits no arbitrage opportunities, however assets and derivatives are combined into an investment strategy.

We assume the principle throughout. In this chapter it yields model-free results: valid whatever the probabilistic dynamics of the underlying.

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be the payoffs at \(T\) of two assets or portfolios, with prices \(\Pi^X_t\), \(\Pi^Y_t\). If \(\mathbb P(X = Y) = 1\) and there is no arbitrage, then \(\mathbb P(\Pi^X_t = \Pi^Y_t) = 1\) for all \(t \in [0, T]\).

Proof. Suppose that \(\mathbb P(\Pi^X_{t_0} \neq \Pi^Y_{t_0}) > 0\) for some \(t_0\). Consider the strategy: do nothing until \(t_0\); at \(t_0\), (a) if \(\Pi^X_{t_0} > \Pi^Y_{t_0}\), buy one unit of \(Y\), sell one unit of \(X\) and deposit \(\Pi^X_{t_0} - \Pi^Y_{t_0}\) in the bank until \(T\); (b) if the prices are equal, do nothing; (c) if \(\Pi^X_{t_0} < \Pi^Y_{t_0}\), do the opposite of (a). The cost at \(t_0\) is always zero. At \(T\) the outcome is, in case (a), \[Y - X + (\Pi^X_{t_0} - \Pi^Y_{t_0})\,e^{(T - t_0)R_{t_0}(T - t_0)} = (\Pi^X_{t_0} - \Pi^Y_{t_0})\,e^{(T - t_0)R_{t_0}(T - t_0)} > 0\] almost surely (since \(X = Y\)), zero in case (b), and positive in case (c). The strategy is an arbitrage with positive probability of profit, a contradiction. \(\square\)

Here \(R_t(\tau)\) is the zero rate prevailing at time \(t\) for horizon \(\tau\), and \(P_t(T) = e^{-(T - t)R_t(T - t)}\) the price at \(t\) of the ZCB maturing at \(T\).

2.3 Forward contracts

A forward contract with maturity \(T\) and delivery price \(K\) pays \(S_T - K\) at \(T\): it is an agreement to buy the underlying at \(T\) for the price \(K\) fixed today. Its value at \(t \leq T\) is denoted \(\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K)\). The forward price \(F_t(T)\) is the delivery price that makes the contract worthless at \(t\): \(\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(F_t(T)) = 0\).

Forwards are typically traded OTC; currency forwards are widespread, and in interest-rate markets their analogue is the forward rate agreement (Chapter 8).

If the underlying pays no cash flows over \([t, T]\), then under no arbitrage \[\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K) = S_t - K P_t(T), \qquad F_t(T) = \frac{S_t}{P_t(T)} = S_t\,e^{(T - t)R_t(T - t)},\] and \(\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K) = P_t(T)\bigl(F_t(T) - K\bigr)\).

Proof via the law of one price. The forward’s payoff \(X = S_T - K\) coincides with that of the portfolio “one unit of the underlying, minus \(K\) zero-coupon bonds maturing at \(T\)”, whose value at \(t\) is \(S_t - KP_t(T)\). Hence \(\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K) = S_t - KP_t(T)\) for every \(K\), and setting it to zero gives \(F_t(T) = S_t/P_t(T)\). \(\square\)

Direct arbitrage proof. If \(F_t(T) < S_t e^{(T-t)R_t(T-t)}\): at \(t\) go long the forward (free), sell the underlying short and deposit \(S_t\); at \(T\) receive \(S_T - F_t(T)\) from the forward, buy back the underlying at \(S_T\), and collect \(S_te^{(T-t)R_t(T-t)}\) from the bank. The net cash flow \(S_te^{(T-t)R_t(T-t)} - F_t(T) > 0\) is an arbitrage. Reversing all positions excludes the opposite inequality.

If the underlying pays cash flows (dividends, coupons) with present value \(I_t\) over \([t, T]\), the buyer of the forward does not receive them, and \(F_t(T) = (S_t - I_t)/P_t(T)\).

2.3.1 Cost of carry

The argument above extends to underlyings that generate income or require costs while they are held. In each case replicate the forward by buying the asset with borrowed money and account for what holding it brings in or costs.

For a forward with maturity \(T\) entered at \(t = 0\), with continuously compounded rate \(r\):

  • known cash income with present value \(I\) (dividends, coupons): \(F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}\);
  • known continuous yield \(q\) (dividend yield of an index): \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r - q)T}\);
  • foreign currency with foreign rate \(r_f\) (covered interest parity): \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}\);
  • commodity with storage costs of present value \(U\): \(F_0 \leq (S_0 + U)e^{rT}\), with equality for investment assets such as gold.

Proof for the yield case. Buy \(e^{-qT}\) units of the asset and reinvest the yield continuously: the holding grows to exactly one unit at \(T\). The cost is \(S_0e^{-qT}\); financing it by borrowing, the debt at \(T\) is \(S_0e^{-qT}e^{rT}\). Holding the unit and paying the debt replicates a forward with delivery price \(S_0e^{(r-q)T}\). Any other forward price is an arbitrage. A foreign currency is an asset with yield \(r_f\). \(\square\)

Summarising with the cost of carry \(c\) — interest paid to finance the asset, plus storage, minus income — \(F_0 = S_0e^{cT}\): \(c = r\) for a non-dividend stock, \(r - q\) for an index, \(r - r_f\) for a currency, \(r + u\) for a commodity with proportional storage cost \(u\). For consumption commodities (oil, copper) only the inequality holds, because holders do not sell inventory to exploit a low forward price: the gap is measured by the convenience yield \(y\), \(F_0 = S_0e^{(c - y)T}\).

Forward price and expected spot price. The forward price is not the market’s forecast of \(S_T\): since the forward costs nothing, \(F_0 = \mathbb E[S_T]\) only if investors require no risk premium on the asset. For assets with positive systematic risk, \(\mathbb E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T} > F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\).

2.4 Futures

A futures contract is, like a forward, an agreement to buy the underlying at a future date for a price fixed today — the futures price \(f_t(T)\), set so that entering the contract costs nothing. The differences: futures are standardised and exchange-traded; gains and losses are marked to market daily (Chapter 1); most futures are closed before maturity by taking the opposite position.

If zero rates are deterministic, then \(f_t(T) = F_t(T)\).

Proof. Let \(t = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T\) be the trading days. Holding \(\theta_i\) futures over \([t_{i-1}, t_i]\) generates the cash flow \(\theta_i\bigl(f_{t_i}(T) - f_{t_{i-1}}(T)\bigr)\) at \(t_i\), which we invest until \(T\), where it is worth \(\theta_i\bigl(f_{t_i}(T) - f_{t_{i-1}}(T)\bigr)/P_{t_i}(T)\). The terminal wealth of the dynamic futures strategy \(\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n)\) is \[V_T(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n\theta_i\bigl(f_{t_i}(T) - f_{t_{i-1}}(T)\bigr)\frac{1}{P_{t_i}(T)} .\] Choose \(\bar\theta_i = P_{t_i}(T)\) — possible only if \(P_{t_i}(T)\) is known at \(t_{i-1}\), which holds when rates are deterministic. Then the sum telescopes: \[V_T(\bar\theta) = f_T(T) - f_t(T) = S_T - f_t(T),\] the payoff of a forward with delivery price \(f_t(T)\). Since entering futures positions costs nothing, \(V_t(\bar\theta) = 0\), and by the law of one price \(0 = \Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(f_t(T))\), i.e. \(f_t(T) = F_t(T)\). \(\square\)

With random rates the daily settlement creates a correlation effect and the two prices differ; for short maturities (a few months) the difference is usually negligible.

2.5 Options

Forwards and futures are linear derivatives. Options are non-linear: they give the right, not the obligation, to do something with the underlying. Their price can never be negative. The exercise date is when the right is used; the strike \(K\) is the fixed price appearing in the payoff.

Let \(g\) be the payoff function. A European option pays \(g(S_T)\) at maturity \(T\). An American option can be exercised at any time \(\tau \leq T\) chosen by the holder, paying \(g(S_\tau)\) at \(\tau\).

An American option is worth at least as much as its European counterpart: the holder can always wait until \(T\).

A call with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) gives the right to buy one unit of the underlying at \(K\); its payoff is \((S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0)\). A put gives the right to sell at \(K\); its payoff is \((K - S_T)^+\). The American versions pay \((S_\tau - K)^+\) or \((K - S_\tau)^+\) at the chosen exercise time \(\tau\).

2.5.1 Exotic options

Vanilla options are the standard calls and puts; all others are exotic, typically OTC and possibly very complex. Both kinds come in European and American versions.

  • Asian options depend on the average price over \([0, T]\): e.g. \(\bigl(\tfrac1T\int_0^T S_t\,dt - K\bigr)^+\) (average price) or \(\bigl(S_T - \tfrac1T\int_0^TS_t\,dt\bigr)^+\) (average strike). In practice the integral is a discrete average over trading days, \(\tfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^NS_{t_i}\). Geometric-average versions, \(\bigl(\exp(\tfrac1T\int_0^T\log S_u\,du) - K\bigr)^+\), also exist and are useful for computation (Chapter 6). Averaging reduces the impact of price manipulation and volatility near maturity.
  • Lookback options depend on the extremes of the path: \(\bigl(S_T - \min_{t\leq T}S_t\bigr)^+\) (“buy at the lowest price”) or \(\bigl(\max_{t\leq T}S_t - S_T\bigr)^+\).
  • Barrier options are activated (“in”) or deactivated (“out”) when the underlying crosses a barrier. A down-and-out put pays \(\mathbf 1_{\{\min_{t\leq T}S_t \geq H_1\}}(K - S_T)^+\) with \(H_1 < K\): the put payoff, unless the price ever falls below \(H_1\). An up-and-out call pays \(\mathbf 1_{\{\max_{t \leq T}S_t \leq H_2\}}(S_T - K)^+\) with \(H_2 > K\). Combining barrier position (up/down) and effect (in/out) gives four types: up-and-out, up-and-in, down-and-out, down-and-in. Barrier options are cheaper than the corresponding vanilla, since the payoff may be cancelled.
  • Compound options have an option as underlying: a put on a call pays \(\bigl(K - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_T(K', T')\bigr)^+\) at \(T\), the right to sell at \(K\) a call with strike \(K'\) and maturity \(T' > T\).
  • Chooser options let the holder decide at \(T' < T\) whether the option is a call or a put.

2.6 Put–call parity

For European options with the same underlying, strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\), under no arbitrage, \[\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_t(K) - \Pi^{\mathrm{Put}}_t(K) = S_t - KP_t(T), \qquad t \in [0, T].\]

Proof. \((S_T - K)^+ - (K - S_T)^+ = S_T - K\) for every \(S_T\): a long call plus a short put has the payoff of a forward with delivery price \(K\), worth \(S_t - KP_t(T)\). Apply the law of one price. \(\square\)

Parity holds only for European options. It is useful in both directions: a call price gives the put price, and vice versa; deviations in market quotes reveal arbitrage (or transaction costs).

2.7 Model-free bounds

Let \(\Pi^{EC}_t, \Pi^{EP}_t, \Pi^{AC}_t, \Pi^{AP}_t\) denote the prices of European and American calls and puts with the same underlying, strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\).

Under no arbitrage:

  1. American options are worth at least their intrinsic value: \(\Pi^{AC}_t \geq (S_t - K)^+\), \(\Pi^{AP}_t \geq (K - S_t)^+\).
  2. American options are worth at least their European versions: \(\Pi^{EC}_t \leq \Pi^{AC}_t \leq S_t\) and \(\Pi^{EP}_t \leq \Pi^{AP}_t \leq K\).
  3. European put: \(\bigl(KP_t(T) - S_t\bigr)^+ \leq \Pi^{EP}_t \leq KP_t(T)\).
  4. European call on a non-dividend-paying underlying: \(\bigl(S_t - KP_t(T)\bigr)^+ \leq \Pi^{EC}_t = \Pi^{AC}_t\).

All proofs follow the same scheme: assume the opposite inequality, buy what is too cheap, sell what is too expensive, invest the difference in the bank, and show that the result is an arbitrage. Two examples:

Proof that \(\Pi^{EP}_t \leq KP_t(T)\). If \(\Pi^{EP}_t > KP_t(T)\): buy \(K\) ZCBs maturing at \(T\), sell one put, deposit \(\Pi^{EP}_t - KP_t(T)\). Zero cost. At \(T\): \(K - (K - S_T)^+ + (\Pi^{EP}_t - KP_t(T))/P_t(T) > 0\), since the first two terms are \(\geq 0\). Arbitrage.

Proof that \(\Pi^{EP}_t \geq KP_t(T) - S_t\). If \(\Pi^{EP}_t < KP_t(T) - S_t\): buy a put and one unit of the underlying, and borrow \(\Pi^{EP}_t + S_t\) until \(T\). Zero cost. At \(T\): \((K - S_T)^+ + S_T - (\Pi^{EP}_t + S_t)/P_t(T)\); the debt is less than \(K\), while \((K - S_T)^+ + S_T = \max(K, S_T) \geq K\). Arbitrage. Since also \(\Pi^{EP}_t \geq 0\), the lower bound \((KP_t(T) - S_t)^+\) follows.

The lower bound for the call, \(\Pi^{EC}_t \geq (S_t - KP_t(T))^+\), is proved in the same way (exercise).

2.7.1 The American call is never exercised early

If the underlying pays no dividends, \(\Pi^{EC}_t(K) = \Pi^{AC}_t(K)\) for all \(t\): it is never optimal to exercise an American call before maturity.

Proof. We know \(\Pi^{EC}_t \leq \Pi^{AC}_t\). Suppose \(\Pi^{EC}_t < \Pi^{AC}_t\): buy a European call, sell an American call, deposit the difference — zero cost. If the American call is exercised at some \(\tau \in [t, T]\), we must pay \((S_\tau - K)^+\); selling our European call covers it, since by bound 4 \[\Pi^{EC}_\tau - (S_\tau - K)^+ \geq (S_\tau - KP_\tau(T))^+ - (S_\tau - K)^+ \geq (S_\tau - K)^+ - (S_\tau - K)^+ = 0,\] using \(P_\tau(T) \leq 1\). We can always honour the obligation, and the bank deposit is pure profit: an arbitrage. \(\square\)

Interpretation. Before maturity, a call on a non-dividend stock is always worth more than its intrinsic value: exercising early throws away the time value of money on the strike (paying \(K\) now rather than later) and the insurance against a fall of the stock. This is not true for an American put: if the underlying is very low, receiving \(K\) immediately can be better than waiting, so American puts may be exercised early (Chapter 3).

2.7.2 Bounds with dividends

If the underlying pays dividends with present value \(D\) during the option’s life, the holder of an option does not receive them, and the bounds shift by \(D\): \[\Pi^{EC}_t \geq S_t - D - KP_t(T), \qquad \Pi^{EP}_t \geq D + KP_t(T) - S_t, \qquad S_t - D - K \leq \Pi^{AC}_t - \Pi^{AP}_t \leq S_t - KP_t(T),\] and put–call parity becomes \(\Pi^{EC}_t - \Pi^{EP}_t = S_t - D - KP_t(T)\). The no-early-exercise argument for American calls breaks down: it can be optimal to exercise immediately before an ex-dividend date, when the dividend about to be lost exceeds the remaining time value (approximately, when the dividend exceeds \(K(1 - e^{-r\tau})\), \(\tau\) being the time to the next dividend date or maturity).

2.8 Static strategies with options

Combining options, the underlying and bonds creates payoff profiles tailored to a market view. Profit means payoff minus the initial cost (compounded to \(T\), or ignoring interest for simplicity).

strategy positions payoff at \(T\) view
covered call long stock, short call \(K\) \(\min(S_T, K)\) mildly bullish, earn premium
protective put long stock, long put \(K\) \(\max(S_T, K)\) bullish with insurance
bull spread long call \(K_1\), short call \(K_2 > K_1\) \(\min\bigl((S_T - K_1)^+, K_2 - K_1\bigr)\) moderate rise
bear spread long put \(K_2\), short put \(K_1 < K_2\) \(\min\bigl((K_2 - S_T)^+, K_2 - K_1\bigr)\) moderate fall
butterfly long calls \(K_1\), \(K_3\), short 2 calls \(K_2 = \tfrac{K_1 + K_3}{2}\) tent peaked at \(K_2\) little movement
straddle long call and put at \(K\) \(\lvert S_T - K\rvert\) large movement, any direction
strip / strap 1 call + 2 puts / 2 calls + 1 put asymmetric V large move, biased down / up
principal-protected note long call \(K\), \(K\) ZCBs \(\max(S_T, K)\) upside with guaranteed principal

Some details:

  • Bull spread. Profit is capped at \(K_2 - K_1 - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_1) + \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_2)\) and losses at the initial cost \(\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_1) - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_2) > 0\) (call prices decrease in the strike, since \((S_T - K_1)^+ \geq (S_T - K_2)^+\)). Compared with a single call, the trader gives up profits above \(K_2\) to reduce the cost.
  • Butterfly. The payoff is \((S_T - K_1)\mathbf 1_{\{S_T \in [K_1, K_2]\}} + (K_3 - S_T)\mathbf 1_{\{S_T \in (K_2, K_3]\}}\); the cost \(\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_1) - 2\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_2) + \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_3)\) is small but positive (convexity of the call price in the strike). The same payoff can be built from puts.
  • Principal-protected notes are the basic structure of many retail products: the worst case is \(K - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K) - KP_0(T)\) relative to the initial investment, i.e. the principal \(K\) is returned.

These static strategies are set up once and held. Chapter 3 introduces dynamic strategies, rebalanced over time, which can replicate any payoff — and with them, option prices instead of option bounds.

2.9 Exercises

Problems from Hull (H) and Björk (B), Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time.

(H 5.7) A 1-year long forward on a non-dividend stock is entered when \(S_0 = 40\) and \(r = 5\%\). (a) Find the forward price and the initial value of the contract. (b) Six months later \(S = 45\) and \(r\) is unchanged: find the forward price and the value of the contract.

Solution.

  1. \(F_0 = 40e^{0.05} = 42.05\); the initial value is \(0\). (b) The new forward price for delivery at \(T = 1\) is \(45e^{0.025} = 46.14\). The contract’s value is \(S_t - KP_t(T) = 45 - 42.05e^{-0.025} = 3.99\).

(H 5.10) \(r = 6\%\), the dividend yield of an index is \(4\%\), the index is at \(400\) and the 4-month futures price is \(405\). What arbitrage exists?

Solution. The theoretical price is \(400e^{(0.06 - 0.04)/3} = 402.68 < 405\): futures are too expensive. Sell futures, borrow to buy the index (reinvesting dividends), deliver at maturity; the profit is about \(405 - 402.68 = 2.32\) per unit of the index.

(H 5.12) Two-month rates are \(1\%\) in Switzerland and \(2\%\) in the US (continuous). The spot CHF is \(1.0500\) USD and the 2-month futures price is also \(1.0500\). What arbitrage exists?

Solution. Covered interest parity gives \(F = 1.05e^{(0.02 - 0.01)/6} = 1.0518 > 1.0500\): futures are too cheap. Buy CHF futures, borrow CHF and sell them spot for USD, invest the USD at 2%; at maturity take delivery of CHF through the futures to repay the CHF loan. Profit \(\approx 0.0018\) USD per CHF.

(H 5.13) Silver trades at \(25\) per ounce; storage costs \(0.24\) per ounce per year, payable quarterly in advance; \(r = 5\%\) for all maturities. Find the 9-month futures price.

Solution. Storage payments of \(0.06\) at \(0\), \(0.25\), \(0.5\): \(U = 0.06(1 + e^{-0.0125} + e^{-0.025}) = 0.1778\). Then \(F_0 = (25 + 0.1778)e^{0.05\times0.75} = 26.14\).

(H 11.1, 11.2) Find a lower bound for (a) a 4-month call with \(S = 28\), \(K = 25\), \(r = 8\%\); (b) a 1-month European put with \(S = 12\), \(K = 15\), \(r = 6\%\) (no dividends).

Solution.

  1. \(S - Ke^{-rT} = 28 - 25e^{-0.08/3} = 3.66\). (b) \(Ke^{-rT} - S = 15e^{-0.005} - 12 = 2.93\).

(H 11.6, 11.11) (a) \(S = 19\), a 3-month European call with \(K = 20\) costs \(1\), \(r = 4\%\). Price the put with the same strike. (b) A 1-month European put with \(K = 50\) trades at \(2.50\) when \(S = 47\) and \(r = 6\%\). What arbitrage is available?

Solution.

  1. Parity: \(P = C + Ke^{-rT} - S = 1 + 20e^{-0.01} - 19 = 1.80\). (b) The lower bound is \(50e^{-0.005} - 47 = 2.75 > 2.50\). Borrow \(49.50\) for one month to buy the put and the stock. At maturity, if \(S_T < 50\) exercise the put and receive \(50\); otherwise sell the stock for more than \(50\). The loan costs \(49.50e^{0.005} = 49.75\), leaving at least \(0.25\).

(H 11.15) An American call on a non-dividend stock costs \(4\), \(S = 31\), \(K = 30\), \(T = 3\) months, \(r = 8\%\). Find bounds for the American put with the same strike and maturity.

Solution. From \(S - K \leq C - P \leq S - Ke^{-rT}\): \(1 \leq 4 - P \leq 31 - 30e^{-0.02} = 1.594\). Hence \(2.41 \leq P \leq 3\).

(H 11.25) Let \(c_1, c_2, c_3\) be European calls with strikes \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\), equally spaced, and the same maturity. Show that \(c_2 \leq \tfrac12(c_1 + c_3)\).

Solution. The butterfly “long \(K_1\), long \(K_3\), short two \(K_2\)” has a non-negative payoff (the tent-shaped payoff of the static strategies above). By no arbitrage its price \(c_1 + c_3 - 2c_2 \geq 0\). Call prices are therefore convex in the strike — a fact used by Breeden–Litzenberger in Chapter 7.

(H 12.5) Puts with strikes \(30\) and \(35\) cost \(4\) and \(7\). Build (a) a bull spread and (b) a bear spread with them, and tabulate payoff and profit.

Solution.

  1. Bull spread: buy the 30 put, sell the 35 put — an initial credit of \(3\). Payoff: \(0\) if \(S_T \geq 35\), \(-(35 - S_T)\) if \(30 < S_T < 35\), \(-5\) if \(S_T \leq 30\); profit \(3\), \(S_T - 32\), \(-2\). (b) Bear spread: buy the 35 put, sell the 30 put, cost \(3\). Payoff \(0\), \(35 - S_T\), \(5\); profit \(-3\), \(32 - S_T\), \(2\).

(B 9.1) A contract pays \(K\) if \(S_T \leq A\), \(K + A - S_T\) if \(A < S_T < K + A\), and \(0\) if \(S_T \geq K + A\). Find a static replicating portfolio of bonds and calls, and its price in the Black–Scholes model.

Solution. \(X = K - (S_T - A)^+ + (S_T - K - A)^+\) (check the three regions). Replicate with \(K\) zero-coupon bonds, a short call struck at \(A\) and a long call struck at \(K + A\). Price: \(Ke^{-r(T-t)} - C(t, S_t; A) + C(t, S_t; K + A)\), with \(C\) the Black–Scholes call price. The replication is model-free; only the final price uses Black–Scholes.

Derivatives are the central object of the course: contracts whose payoff depends on the value of an underlying asset. This chapter introduces the main types — forwards, futures and options, vanilla and exotic — and the three kinds of traders who use them. Its main tool is the **no-arbitrage principle**, which already yields a surprising number of exact results without any probabilistic model: the law of one price, forward and futures prices, put–call parity, model-free bounds on option prices, and the fact that an American call on a non-dividend stock should never be exercised early. ## Hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs

Three broad categories of market participants trade derivatives:

- **hedgers** reduce their exposure to adverse price movements (companies, banks, funds);
- **speculators** take positions to bet on future price movements (banks, hedge funds, investors);
- **arbitrageurs** look for riskless profits by combining transactions across counterparties or markets (banks, hedge funds).

::: {.example name="A hedger: forward vs. put"}
An Italian textile company will receive 100k USD from a US client in six months. Today 1 USD = 0.86 EUR, but the future rate is unknown: the company is exposed to **currency risk**, and its business is textiles, not betting on exchange rates.

- **Forward contract.** The company agrees today, at zero cost, to pay 100k USD in six months and receive 86k EUR. Its income is locked at 86k EUR whatever happens. The price of certainty: if the rate rises to 0.90, the company forgoes the 90k EUR it would have received.
- **Put option.** For 2k EUR today, the company buys the *right* (not the obligation) to sell 100k USD for 86k EUR in six months. If the rate falls below 0.86 it exercises and receives 86k EUR; otherwise it lets the option expire and converts at the market rate. Its net income is at least $86 - 2 = 84$k EUR, with unlimited upside.

The forward eliminates risk in both directions for free; the option removes only the downside, at a price.
:::

::: {.example name="A speculator: leverage"}
An investor believes the USD/EUR rate (now 0.86) will fall within six months.

1. *Borrow 100k USD, convert into 86k EUR.* At 0.81 the debt is repaid with 81k EUR: profit 5k EUR. At 0.88 it costs 88k EUR: loss 2k EUR. Gains and losses are small relative to the volume, and a large credit line is needed.
2. *Forward contract* to pay 100k USD and receive 86k EUR: the same profile, but with no cash flow today.
3. *Borrow 10k EUR and buy five puts* (each on 100k USD at 0.86). At 0.81 the puts are exercised: buy 500k USD for 405k EUR, sell them for 430k EUR, gain 25k EUR, net 15k EUR after the premium. At 0.88 the puts expire worthless: loss 10k EUR.

Options **amplify** gains and losses (*leverage*); losses are limited to the premium, while gains are potentially unlimited.
:::

::: {.example name="An arbitrageur"}
A US bank pays 2% on 6-month USD deposits, a European bank lends EUR at 1% over 6 months, and the forward above (100k USD for 86k EUR) is available in any quantity. Today:

- borrow 86k EUR, convert into 100k USD, deposit them in the US;
- enter into 1.02 forward contracts.

Net cost today: zero. In six months the deposit holds 102k USD; the forwards convert them into 87 720 EUR; repaying the loan costs 86 860 EUR. The **riskless profit** is 860 EUR, whatever the exchange rate does.
:::

This is an **arbitrage opportunity**: zero initial cost, no risk of loss, a positive profit with positive probability. Scaling all transactions by 100 yields 86k EUR. In reality there are transaction costs and some default risk, and above all every market participant would exploit the opportunity, moving prices through supply and demand until it disappears. **If arbitrage opportunities ever exist, they vanish almost immediately.**

## General features and the no-arbitrage principle

Every derivative has a **maturity** $T$ (the termination date), an **underlying** (usually a traded asset: a stock, an index, a currency), a **payoff** (the cash flow it delivers, as a function of the underlying, usually at maturity) and a **price** (its market value before maturity). We denote by $S_t$ the price of the underlying at time $t$; for $T > t$, $S_T$ is unknown at $t$, i.e. random. $t$ denotes a generic valuation date.

::: {.definition name="Arbitrage opportunity"}
An **arbitrage opportunity** is a trading strategy with zero initial cost, no risk of losses at any time, and a strictly positive profit with positive probability at some future time.
:::

::: {.definition name="No-arbitrage principle"}
The financial market admits no arbitrage opportunities, however assets and derivatives are combined into an investment strategy.
:::

We assume the principle throughout. In this chapter it yields **model-free** results: valid whatever the probabilistic dynamics of the underlying.

::: {.theorem name="Law of one price"}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be the payoffs at $T$ of two assets or portfolios, with prices $\Pi^X_t$, $\Pi^Y_t$. If $\mathbb P(X = Y) = 1$ and there is no arbitrage, then $\mathbb P(\Pi^X_t = \Pi^Y_t) = 1$ for all $t \in [0, T]$.
:::

*Proof.* Suppose that $\mathbb P(\Pi^X_{t_0} \neq \Pi^Y_{t_0}) > 0$ for some $t_0$. Consider the strategy: do nothing until $t_0$; at $t_0$, (a) if $\Pi^X_{t_0} > \Pi^Y_{t_0}$, buy one unit of $Y$, sell one unit of $X$ and deposit $\Pi^X_{t_0} - \Pi^Y_{t_0}$ in the bank until $T$; (b) if the prices are equal, do nothing; (c) if $\Pi^X_{t_0} < \Pi^Y_{t_0}$, do the opposite of (a). The cost at $t_0$ is always zero. At $T$ the outcome is, in case (a),
$$Y - X + (\Pi^X_{t_0} - \Pi^Y_{t_0})\,e^{(T - t_0)R_{t_0}(T - t_0)} = (\Pi^X_{t_0} - \Pi^Y_{t_0})\,e^{(T - t_0)R_{t_0}(T - t_0)} > 0$$
almost surely (since $X = Y$), zero in case (b), and positive in case (c). The strategy is an arbitrage with positive probability of profit, a contradiction. $\square$

Here $R_t(\tau)$ is the zero rate prevailing at time $t$ for horizon $\tau$, and $P_t(T) = e^{-(T - t)R_t(T - t)}$ the price at $t$ of the ZCB maturing at $T$.

## Forward contracts

::: {.definition name="Forward contract and forward price"}
A **forward contract** with maturity $T$ and delivery price $K$ pays $S_T - K$ at $T$: it is an agreement to buy the underlying at $T$ for the price $K$ fixed today. Its value at $t \leq T$ is denoted $\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K)$. The **forward price** $F_t(T)$ is the delivery price that makes the contract worthless at $t$: $\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(F_t(T)) = 0$.
:::

Forwards are typically traded OTC; currency forwards are widespread, and in interest-rate markets their analogue is the forward rate agreement (Chapter 8).

::: {.theorem name="Forward price and value of a forward"}
If the underlying pays no cash flows over $[t, T]$, then under no arbitrage
$$\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K) = S_t - K P_t(T), \qquad F_t(T) = \frac{S_t}{P_t(T)} = S_t\,e^{(T - t)R_t(T - t)},$$
and $\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K) = P_t(T)\bigl(F_t(T) - K\bigr)$.
:::

*Proof via the law of one price.* The forward's payoff $X = S_T - K$ coincides with that of the portfolio "one unit of the underlying, minus $K$ zero-coupon bonds maturing at $T$", whose value at $t$ is $S_t - KP_t(T)$. Hence $\Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(K) = S_t - KP_t(T)$ for every $K$, and setting it to zero gives $F_t(T) = S_t/P_t(T)$. $\square$

*Direct arbitrage proof.* If $F_t(T) < S_t e^{(T-t)R_t(T-t)}$: at $t$ go long the forward (free), sell the underlying short and deposit $S_t$; at $T$ receive $S_T - F_t(T)$ from the forward, buy back the underlying at $S_T$, and collect $S_te^{(T-t)R_t(T-t)}$ from the bank. The net cash flow $S_te^{(T-t)R_t(T-t)} - F_t(T) > 0$ is an arbitrage. Reversing all positions excludes the opposite inequality.

If the underlying pays cash flows (dividends, coupons) with present value $I_t$ over $[t, T]$, the buyer of the forward does not receive them, and $F_t(T) = (S_t - I_t)/P_t(T)$.

### Cost of carry

The argument above extends to underlyings that generate income or require costs while they are held. In each case replicate the forward by buying the asset with borrowed money and account for what holding it brings in or costs.

::: {.proposition name="Forward prices with income, yields and storage"}
For a forward with maturity $T$ entered at $t = 0$, with continuously compounded rate $r$:

- known cash **income** with present value $I$ (dividends, coupons): $F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}$;
- known continuous **yield** $q$ (dividend yield of an index): $F_0 = S_0e^{(r - q)T}$;
- **foreign currency** with foreign rate $r_f$ (covered interest parity): $F_0 = S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$;
- commodity with **storage costs** of present value $U$: $F_0 \leq (S_0 + U)e^{rT}$, with equality for investment assets such as gold.
:::

*Proof for the yield case.* Buy $e^{-qT}$ units of the asset and reinvest the yield continuously: the holding grows to exactly one unit at $T$. The cost is $S_0e^{-qT}$; financing it by borrowing, the debt at $T$ is $S_0e^{-qT}e^{rT}$. Holding the unit and paying the debt replicates a forward with delivery price $S_0e^{(r-q)T}$. Any other forward price is an arbitrage. A foreign currency is an asset with yield $r_f$. $\square$

Summarising with the **cost of carry** $c$ — interest paid to finance the asset, plus storage, minus income — $F_0 = S_0e^{cT}$: $c = r$ for a non-dividend stock, $r - q$ for an index, $r - r_f$ for a currency, $r + u$ for a commodity with proportional storage cost $u$. For **consumption commodities** (oil, copper) only the inequality holds, because holders do not sell inventory to exploit a low forward price: the gap is measured by the **convenience yield** $y$, $F_0 = S_0e^{(c - y)T}$.

**Forward price and expected spot price.** The forward price is *not* the market's forecast of $S_T$: since the forward costs nothing, $F_0 = \mathbb E[S_T]$ only if investors require no risk premium on the asset. For assets with positive systematic risk, $\mathbb E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T} > F_0 = S_0e^{rT}$.

## Futures

A **futures contract** is, like a forward, an agreement to buy the underlying at a future date for a price fixed today — the **futures price** $f_t(T)$, set so that entering the contract costs nothing. The differences: futures are standardised and exchange-traded; gains and losses are **marked to market daily** (Chapter 1); most futures are closed before maturity by taking the opposite position.

::: {.theorem name="Futures and forward prices"}
If zero rates are deterministic, then $f_t(T) = F_t(T)$.
:::

*Proof.* Let $t = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ be the trading days. Holding $\theta_i$ futures over $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$ generates the cash flow $\theta_i\bigl(f_{t_i}(T) - f_{t_{i-1}}(T)\bigr)$ at $t_i$, which we invest until $T$, where it is worth $\theta_i\bigl(f_{t_i}(T) - f_{t_{i-1}}(T)\bigr)/P_{t_i}(T)$. The terminal wealth of the **dynamic futures strategy** $\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n)$ is
$$V_T(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n\theta_i\bigl(f_{t_i}(T) - f_{t_{i-1}}(T)\bigr)\frac{1}{P_{t_i}(T)} .$$
Choose $\bar\theta_i = P_{t_i}(T)$ — possible only if $P_{t_i}(T)$ is known at $t_{i-1}$, which holds when rates are deterministic. Then the sum telescopes:
$$V_T(\bar\theta) = f_T(T) - f_t(T) = S_T - f_t(T),$$
the payoff of a forward with delivery price $f_t(T)$. Since entering futures positions costs nothing, $V_t(\bar\theta) = 0$, and by the law of one price $0 = \Pi^{\mathrm{fwd}}_t(f_t(T))$, i.e. $f_t(T) = F_t(T)$. $\square$

With random rates the daily settlement creates a correlation effect and the two prices differ; for short maturities (a few months) the difference is usually negligible.

## Options

Forwards and futures are **linear** derivatives. Options are **non-linear**: they give the right, not the obligation, to do something with the underlying. Their price can never be negative. The **exercise date** is when the right is used; the **strike** $K$ is the fixed price appearing in the payoff.

::: {.definition name="European and American options"}
Let $g$ be the payoff function. A **European** option pays $g(S_T)$ at maturity $T$. An **American** option can be exercised at any time $\tau \leq T$ chosen by the holder, paying $g(S_\tau)$ at $\tau$.
:::

An American option is worth at least as much as its European counterpart: the holder can always wait until $T$.

::: {.definition name="Call and put options"}
A **call** with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ gives the right to buy one unit of the underlying at $K$; its payoff is $(S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0)$. A **put** gives the right to sell at $K$; its payoff is $(K - S_T)^+$. The American versions pay $(S_\tau - K)^+$ or $(K - S_\tau)^+$ at the chosen exercise time $\tau$.
:::

### Exotic options

**Vanilla** options are the standard calls and puts; all others are **exotic**, typically OTC and possibly very complex. Both kinds come in European and American versions.

- **Asian options** depend on the average price over $[0, T]$: e.g. $\bigl(\tfrac1T\int_0^T S_t\,dt - K\bigr)^+$ (average price) or $\bigl(S_T - \tfrac1T\int_0^TS_t\,dt\bigr)^+$ (average strike). In practice the integral is a discrete average over trading days, $\tfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^NS_{t_i}$. Geometric-average versions, $\bigl(\exp(\tfrac1T\int_0^T\log S_u\,du) - K\bigr)^+$, also exist and are useful for computation (Chapter 6). Averaging reduces the impact of price manipulation and volatility near maturity.
- **Lookback options** depend on the extremes of the path: $\bigl(S_T - \min_{t\leq T}S_t\bigr)^+$ ("buy at the lowest price") or $\bigl(\max_{t\leq T}S_t - S_T\bigr)^+$.
- **Barrier options** are activated ("in") or deactivated ("out") when the underlying crosses a barrier. A *down-and-out put* pays $\mathbf 1_{\{\min_{t\leq T}S_t \geq H_1\}}(K - S_T)^+$ with $H_1 < K$: the put payoff, unless the price ever falls below $H_1$. An *up-and-out call* pays $\mathbf 1_{\{\max_{t \leq T}S_t \leq H_2\}}(S_T - K)^+$ with $H_2 > K$. Combining barrier position (up/down) and effect (in/out) gives four types: up-and-out, up-and-in, down-and-out, down-and-in. Barrier options are cheaper than the corresponding vanilla, since the payoff may be cancelled.
- **Compound options** have an option as underlying: a put on a call pays $\bigl(K - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_T(K', T')\bigr)^+$ at $T$, the right to sell at $K$ a call with strike $K'$ and maturity $T' > T$.
- **Chooser options** let the holder decide at $T' < T$ whether the option is a call or a put.

## Put–call parity

::: {.theorem name="Put–call parity"}
For European options with the same underlying, strike $K$ and maturity $T$, under no arbitrage,
$$\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_t(K) - \Pi^{\mathrm{Put}}_t(K) = S_t - KP_t(T), \qquad t \in [0, T].$$
:::

*Proof.* $(S_T - K)^+ - (K - S_T)^+ = S_T - K$ for every $S_T$: a long call plus a short put has the payoff of a forward with delivery price $K$, worth $S_t - KP_t(T)$. Apply the law of one price. $\square$

Parity holds only for European options. It is useful in both directions: a call price gives the put price, and vice versa; deviations in market quotes reveal arbitrage (or transaction costs).

## Model-free bounds

Let $\Pi^{EC}_t, \Pi^{EP}_t, \Pi^{AC}_t, \Pi^{AP}_t$ denote the prices of European and American calls and puts with the same underlying, strike $K$ and maturity $T$.

::: {.theorem name="No-arbitrage bounds"}
Under no arbitrage:

1. American options are worth at least their **intrinsic value**: $\Pi^{AC}_t \geq (S_t - K)^+$, $\Pi^{AP}_t \geq (K - S_t)^+$.
2. American options are worth at least their European versions: $\Pi^{EC}_t \leq \Pi^{AC}_t \leq S_t$ and $\Pi^{EP}_t \leq \Pi^{AP}_t \leq K$.
3. European put: $\bigl(KP_t(T) - S_t\bigr)^+ \leq \Pi^{EP}_t \leq KP_t(T)$.
4. European call on a non-dividend-paying underlying: $\bigl(S_t - KP_t(T)\bigr)^+ \leq \Pi^{EC}_t = \Pi^{AC}_t$.
:::

All proofs follow the same scheme: assume the opposite inequality, buy what is too cheap, sell what is too expensive, invest the difference in the bank, and show that the result is an arbitrage. Two examples:

*Proof that $\Pi^{EP}_t \leq KP_t(T)$.* If $\Pi^{EP}_t > KP_t(T)$: buy $K$ ZCBs maturing at $T$, sell one put, deposit $\Pi^{EP}_t - KP_t(T)$. Zero cost. At $T$: $K - (K - S_T)^+ + (\Pi^{EP}_t - KP_t(T))/P_t(T) > 0$, since the first two terms are $\geq 0$. Arbitrage.

*Proof that $\Pi^{EP}_t \geq KP_t(T) - S_t$.* If $\Pi^{EP}_t < KP_t(T) - S_t$: buy a put and one unit of the underlying, and borrow $\Pi^{EP}_t + S_t$ until $T$. Zero cost. At $T$: $(K - S_T)^+ + S_T - (\Pi^{EP}_t + S_t)/P_t(T)$; the debt is less than $K$, while $(K - S_T)^+ + S_T = \max(K, S_T) \geq K$. Arbitrage. Since also $\Pi^{EP}_t \geq 0$, the lower bound $(KP_t(T) - S_t)^+$ follows.

The lower bound for the call, $\Pi^{EC}_t \geq (S_t - KP_t(T))^+$, is proved in the same way (exercise).

### The American call is never exercised early

::: {.proposition name="No early exercise of American calls"}
If the underlying pays no dividends, $\Pi^{EC}_t(K) = \Pi^{AC}_t(K)$ for all $t$: it is never optimal to exercise an American call before maturity.
:::

*Proof.* We know $\Pi^{EC}_t \leq \Pi^{AC}_t$. Suppose $\Pi^{EC}_t < \Pi^{AC}_t$: buy a European call, sell an American call, deposit the difference — zero cost. If the American call is exercised at some $\tau \in [t, T]$, we must pay $(S_\tau - K)^+$; selling our European call covers it, since by bound 4
$$\Pi^{EC}_\tau - (S_\tau - K)^+ \geq (S_\tau - KP_\tau(T))^+ - (S_\tau - K)^+ \geq (S_\tau - K)^+ - (S_\tau - K)^+ = 0,$$
using $P_\tau(T) \leq 1$. We can always honour the obligation, and the bank deposit is pure profit: an arbitrage. $\square$

*Interpretation.* Before maturity, a call on a non-dividend stock is always worth more than its intrinsic value: exercising early throws away the time value of money on the strike (paying $K$ now rather than later) and the insurance against a fall of the stock. This is not true for an American put: if the underlying is very low, receiving $K$ immediately can be better than waiting, so American puts may be exercised early (Chapter 3).

### Bounds with dividends

If the underlying pays dividends with present value $D$ during the option's life, the holder of an option does not receive them, and the bounds shift by $D$:
$$\Pi^{EC}_t \geq S_t - D - KP_t(T), \qquad \Pi^{EP}_t \geq D + KP_t(T) - S_t, \qquad S_t - D - K \leq \Pi^{AC}_t - \Pi^{AP}_t \leq S_t - KP_t(T),$$
and put–call parity becomes $\Pi^{EC}_t - \Pi^{EP}_t = S_t - D - KP_t(T)$. The no-early-exercise argument for American calls breaks down: it can be optimal to exercise **immediately before an ex-dividend date**, when the dividend about to be lost exceeds the remaining time value (approximately, when the dividend exceeds $K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$, $\tau$ being the time to the next dividend date or maturity).

## Static strategies with options

Combining options, the underlying and bonds creates payoff profiles tailored to a market view. Profit means payoff minus the initial cost (compounded to $T$, or ignoring interest for simplicity).

| strategy | positions | payoff at $T$ | view |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| covered call | long stock, short call $K$ | $\min(S_T, K)$ | mildly bullish, earn premium |
| protective put | long stock, long put $K$ | $\max(S_T, K)$ | bullish with insurance |
| bull spread | long call $K_1$, short call $K_2 > K_1$ | $\min\bigl((S_T - K_1)^+, K_2 - K_1\bigr)$ | moderate rise |
| bear spread | long put $K_2$, short put $K_1 < K_2$ | $\min\bigl((K_2 - S_T)^+, K_2 - K_1\bigr)$ | moderate fall |
| butterfly | long calls $K_1$, $K_3$, short 2 calls $K_2 = \tfrac{K_1 + K_3}{2}$ | tent peaked at $K_2$ | little movement |
| straddle | long call and put at $K$ | $\lvert S_T - K\rvert$ | large movement, any direction |
| strip / strap | 1 call + 2 puts / 2 calls + 1 put | asymmetric V | large move, biased down / up |
| principal-protected note | long call $K$, $K$ ZCBs | $\max(S_T, K)$ | upside with guaranteed principal |

Some details:

- **Bull spread.** Profit is capped at $K_2 - K_1 - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_1) + \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_2)$ and losses at the initial cost $\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_1) - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_2) > 0$ (call prices decrease in the strike, since $(S_T - K_1)^+ \geq (S_T - K_2)^+$). Compared with a single call, the trader gives up profits above $K_2$ to reduce the cost.
- **Butterfly.** The payoff is $(S_T - K_1)\mathbf 1_{\{S_T \in [K_1, K_2]\}} + (K_3 - S_T)\mathbf 1_{\{S_T \in (K_2, K_3]\}}$; the cost $\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_1) - 2\Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_2) + \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K_3)$ is small but positive (convexity of the call price in the strike). The same payoff can be built from puts.
- **Principal-protected notes** are the basic structure of many retail products: the worst case is $K - \Pi^{\mathrm{Call}}_0(K) - KP_0(T)$ relative to the initial investment, i.e. the principal $K$ is returned.

These **static** strategies are set up once and held. Chapter 3 introduces **dynamic** strategies, rebalanced over time, which can replicate any payoff — and with them, option prices instead of option bounds.

## Exercises

Problems from Hull (H) and Björk (B), *Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time*.

::: {.exercise}
**(H 5.7)** A 1-year long forward on a non-dividend stock is entered when $S_0 = 40$ and $r = 5\%$. (a) Find the forward price and the initial value of the contract. (b) Six months later $S = 45$ and $r$ is unchanged: find the forward price and the value of the contract.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $F_0 = 40e^{0.05} = 42.05$; the initial value is $0$. (b) The new forward price for delivery at $T = 1$ is $45e^{0.025} = 46.14$. The contract's value is $S_t - KP_t(T) = 45 - 42.05e^{-0.025} = 3.99$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 5.10)** $r = 6\%$, the dividend yield of an index is $4\%$, the index is at $400$ and the 4-month futures price is $405$. What arbitrage exists?
:::

::: {.solution}
The theoretical price is $400e^{(0.06 - 0.04)/3} = 402.68 < 405$: futures are too expensive. Sell futures, borrow to buy the index (reinvesting dividends), deliver at maturity; the profit is about $405 - 402.68 = 2.32$ per unit of the index.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 5.12)** Two-month rates are $1\%$ in Switzerland and $2\%$ in the US (continuous). The spot CHF is $1.0500$ USD and the 2-month futures price is also $1.0500$. What arbitrage exists?
:::

::: {.solution}
Covered interest parity gives $F = 1.05e^{(0.02 - 0.01)/6} = 1.0518 > 1.0500$: futures are too cheap. Buy CHF futures, borrow CHF and sell them spot for USD, invest the USD at 2%; at maturity take delivery of CHF through the futures to repay the CHF loan. Profit $\approx 0.0018$ USD per CHF.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 5.13)** Silver trades at $25$ per ounce; storage costs $0.24$ per ounce per year, payable quarterly in advance; $r = 5\%$ for all maturities. Find the 9-month futures price.
:::

::: {.solution}
Storage payments of $0.06$ at $0$, $0.25$, $0.5$: $U = 0.06(1 + e^{-0.0125} + e^{-0.025}) = 0.1778$. Then $F_0 = (25 + 0.1778)e^{0.05\times0.75} = 26.14$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 11.1, 11.2)** Find a lower bound for (a) a 4-month call with $S = 28$, $K = 25$, $r = 8\%$; (b) a 1-month European put with $S = 12$, $K = 15$, $r = 6\%$ (no dividends).
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $S - Ke^{-rT} = 28 - 25e^{-0.08/3} = 3.66$. (b) $Ke^{-rT} - S = 15e^{-0.005} - 12 = 2.93$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 11.6, 11.11)** (a) $S = 19$, a 3-month European call with $K = 20$ costs $1$, $r = 4\%$. Price the put with the same strike. (b) A 1-month European put with $K = 50$ trades at $2.50$ when $S = 47$ and $r = 6\%$. What arbitrage is available?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Parity: $P = C + Ke^{-rT} - S = 1 + 20e^{-0.01} - 19 = 1.80$. (b) The lower bound is $50e^{-0.005} - 47 = 2.75 > 2.50$. Borrow $49.50$ for one month to buy the put and the stock. At maturity, if $S_T < 50$ exercise the put and receive $50$; otherwise sell the stock for more than $50$. The loan costs $49.50e^{0.005} = 49.75$, leaving at least $0.25$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 11.15)** An American call on a non-dividend stock costs $4$, $S = 31$, $K = 30$, $T = 3$ months, $r = 8\%$. Find bounds for the American put with the same strike and maturity.
:::

::: {.solution}
From $S - K \leq C - P \leq S - Ke^{-rT}$: $1 \leq 4 - P \leq 31 - 30e^{-0.02} = 1.594$. Hence $2.41 \leq P \leq 3$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 11.25)** Let $c_1, c_2, c_3$ be European calls with strikes $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$, equally spaced, and the same maturity. Show that $c_2 \leq \tfrac12(c_1 + c_3)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The butterfly "long $K_1$, long $K_3$, short two $K_2$" has a non-negative payoff (the tent-shaped payoff of the static strategies above). By no arbitrage its price $c_1 + c_3 - 2c_2 \geq 0$. Call prices are therefore convex in the strike — a fact used by Breeden–Litzenberger in Chapter 7.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(H 12.5)** Puts with strikes $30$ and $35$ cost $4$ and $7$. Build (a) a bull spread and (b) a bear spread with them, and tabulate payoff and profit.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Bull spread: buy the 30 put, sell the 35 put — an initial **credit** of $3$. Payoff: $0$ if $S_T \geq 35$, $-(35 - S_T)$ if $30 < S_T < 35$, $-5$ if $S_T \leq 30$; profit $3$, $S_T - 32$, $-2$. (b) Bear spread: buy the 35 put, sell the 30 put, cost $3$. Payoff $0$, $35 - S_T$, $5$; profit $-3$, $32 - S_T$, $2$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 9.1)** A contract pays $K$ if $S_T \leq A$, $K + A - S_T$ if $A < S_T < K + A$, and $0$ if $S_T \geq K + A$. Find a static replicating portfolio of bonds and calls, and its price in the Black–Scholes model.
:::

::: {.solution}
$X = K - (S_T - A)^+ + (S_T - K - A)^+$ (check the three regions). Replicate with $K$ zero-coupon bonds, a short call struck at $A$ and a long call struck at $K + A$. Price: $Ke^{-r(T-t)} - C(t, S_t; A) + C(t, S_t; K + A)$, with $C$ the Black–Scholes call price. The replication is model-free; only the final price uses Black–Scholes.
:::
