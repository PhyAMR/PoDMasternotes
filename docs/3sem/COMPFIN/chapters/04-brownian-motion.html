4  Brownian motion and stochastic calculus

What happens to the binomial model when the number of periods goes to infinity? Time becomes continuous, and the random walk of the stock becomes a process driven by Brownian motion (“the task of art today is to bring chaos into order”, Adorno). Brownian paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable, so ordinary calculus fails: to write differential equations driven by noise we need the Itô integral and its chain rule, Itô’s formula. This chapter builds these tools, which underlie the Black–Scholes model of Chapter 5.

4.1 Filtrations, processes, martingales

From now on time runs continuously over \([0, T]\).

A filtration \(\mathbb F = (\mathcal F_t)_{t\in[0,T]}\) is an increasing family of \(\sigma\)-algebras (\(\mathcal F_s \subseteq \mathcal F_t\) for \(s \leq t\)): the information available at each time. A stochastic process \(X = (X_t)_{t\in[0,T]}\) is a family of random variables indexed by time; it is adapted if \(X_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable for every \(t\).

Recall the rules of conditional expectation: if \(\xi\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_t\) then \(\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t] = \mathbb E[\xi]\) (the information is useless); if \(\xi\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable then \(\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t] = \xi\); and for \(s \leq t\) the tower property \(\mathbb E\bigl[\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t]\bigm|\mathcal F_s\bigr] = \mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_s]\) holds.

An adapted process \(X\) is a martingale if \(\mathbb E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = X_s\) for all \(s \leq t\) — constant on average, a “fair game”. It is a Markov process if \(\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s]\) for all \(s \leq t\) and bounded \(f\): the future depends on the past only through the present value.

4.2 Brownian motion

An adapted process \(W = (W_t)_{t\geq0}\) with \(W_0 = 0\) is a (standard) Brownian motion if

  1. \(W_t - W_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\) for all \(s \leq t\) (independent increments);
  2. \(W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal N(0, t - s)\) for all \(s \leq t\) (stationary Gaussian increments);
  3. \(t \mapsto W_t\) is continuous.

A short history: Robert Brown observes the erratic motion of pollen particles in water (1827); Louis Bachelier’s thesis Théorie de la spéculation (1900) uses it to model stock prices; Einstein explains it physically (1905); Wiener gives a rigorous construction (1923, hence “Wiener process”); Samuelson rediscovers Bachelier in the 1960s; Black, Scholes and Merton publish their option-pricing theory in 1973.

Figure: a simulated Brownian path — continuous, but rough at every scale.

For \(s \leq t\): \(\mathbb E[W_t] = 0\), \(\mathrm{Var}(W_t) = t\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = s\), and \(\mathbb E[e^{\lambda(W_t - W_s)}] = e^{\lambda^2(t-s)/2}\). The processes \[W_t, \qquad W_t^2 - t, \qquad M_t = e^{\lambda W_t - \lambda^2t/2}\] are martingales, and \(W\) is a Markov process.

Proof. Covariance: write \(W_t = W_s + (W_t - W_s)\); since \(W_s\) is \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable and the increment is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\) with mean zero, \(\mathbb E[W_sW_t] = \mathbb E[W_s^2] + \mathbb E[W_t - W_s]\mathbb E[W_s] = s\).

Martingale: \(\mathbb E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s + \mathbb E[W_t - W_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s + \mathbb E[W_t - W_s] = W_s\).

\(W_t^2 - t\): \(\mathbb E[W_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s^2 + 2W_s\mathbb E[W_t - W_s] + \mathbb E[(W_t - W_s)^2] = W_s^2 + t - s\).

Exponential martingale: \(\mathbb E[e^{\lambda W_t}\mid\mathcal F_s] = e^{\lambda W_s}\,\mathbb E[e^{\lambda(W_t - W_s)}] = e^{\lambda W_s}e^{\lambda^2(t-s)/2}\), using the moment generating function of \(\mathcal N(0, t-s)\); multiply by \(e^{-\lambda^2t/2}\).

Markov: for bounded \(f\), \(\mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(x + W_t - W_s)]\big|_{x = W_s}\), a function of \(W_s\) only; taking \(\mathbb E[\,\cdot\mid W_s]\) of both sides gives \(\mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid W_s]\). \(\square\)

4.2.1 Nowhere differentiability and infinite variation

Brownian paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable — “a continuous function made entirely of corners” (think of \(|x|\) at \(0\), everywhere). Heuristically, \(W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t \sim \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\), so \[\frac{W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t}{\Delta t} \sim \frac{Z}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} \xrightarrow[\Delta t\to0]{} \pm\infty .\] Paths also have infinite variation: they cover an infinite length in any finite interval (proved below from the quadratic variation). Why should we care? Because we want Brownian motion to drive differential equations.

4.3 Differential equations driven by noise

Suppose a quantity evolves over a short interval \(\Delta t\) through a locally deterministic drift proportional to \(\Delta t\) and a Gaussian noise with variance proportional to \(\Delta t\): \[X_{t+\Delta t} - X_t = \alpha(t, X_t)\,\Delta t + \beta(t, X_t)\,(W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t), \qquad X_0 = x .\] Dividing by \(\Delta t\) and letting \(\Delta t \to 0\) fails, because \(dW_t/dt\) does not exist. Instead, on a grid \(t_k = k\Delta t\) sum the increments, \[X_{t_n} = x + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha(t_k, X_{t_k})\Delta t + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta(t_k, X_{t_k})\Delta W_{t_{k+1}},\] and let \(\Delta t \to 0\). The first sum converges to an ordinary (Riemann–Stieltjes) integral \(\int_0^t\alpha(u, X_u)\,du\). The second should converge to “\(\int_0^t\beta(u, X_u)\,dW_u\)”, but this cannot be defined path by path as a Riemann–Stieltjes integral, precisely because \(W\) has infinite variation. Itô (1944) solved the problem by giving up the pathwise definition and constructing the integral in the space of square-integrable random variables.

4.4 The Itô integral

\(L^2\) is the space of random variables with \(\mathbb E[X^2] < \infty\), with norm \(\|X\| = \mathbb E[X^2]^{1/2}\). \(X_n \to X\) in \(L^2\) (in mean square) if \(\|X_n - X\| \to 0\). \(L^2\) is complete: every Cauchy sequence converges to a unique limit. Moreover, \(L^2\) limits of Gaussian random variables are Gaussian, and \(L^2\) convergence implies \(\mathbb E[X_n^2] \to \mathbb E[X^2]\) and \(\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal G] \to \mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\) in \(L^2\) for any \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal G\).

The plan: define \(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\) for simple integrands, prove its properties, then extend by approximation. Integrands are always adapted.

Step 0. For a deterministic indicator \(\beta_t = c\,\mathbf 1_{[a,b)}(t)\) the only reasonable definition is \(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u = c(W_b - W_a)\).

\(\beta\) is simple if there are times \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T\) such that \[\beta_t = \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{t_{i-1}}\mathbf 1_{[t_{i-1}, t_i)}(t),\] with each \(\beta_{t_{i-1}}\) square-integrable and \(\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}}\)-measurable: a piecewise-constant function of time with random heights. Its stochastic integral is \[\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u := \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{t_{i-1}}\bigl(W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}\bigr),\] and \(\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u = \sum_i\beta_{t_{i-1}}(W_{t_i\wedge t} - W_{t_{i-1}\wedge t})\) for \(t \leq T\).

Figure: a simple integrand — constant on each interval, with a random height known at the left endpoint.

It is essential that the integrand is evaluated at the left endpoint of each interval, against the forward increment of \(W\): the height is decided before the noise of that interval is revealed. (In finance: you choose your portfolio before prices move.)

For simple \(\beta\):

  1. Linearity: \(\int(\beta + \gamma)\,dW = \int\beta\,dW + \int\gamma\,dW\).
  2. Martingale property: \(\mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u\bigm|\mathcal F_s\bigr] = \int_0^s\beta_u\,dW_u\) for \(s \leq t\); in particular \(\mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr] = 0\).
  3. Itô isometry: \(\mathbb E\Bigl[\bigl(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr)^2\Bigr] = \mathbb E\Bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\Bigr]\); hence \(\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr) = \mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\bigr]\).
  4. If \(\beta\) is deterministic, \(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u \sim \mathcal N\bigl(0, \int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\bigr)\).

Proof of 2. Each term contributes \(\beta_{t_{i-1}}(W_{t_i\wedge t} - W_{t_{i-1}\wedge t})\). Terms entirely before \(s\) are \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable and stay. For the interval \([t_j, t_{j+1})\) containing \(s\), \(\beta_{t_j}\) is \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable and \(\mathbb E[W_{t_{j+1}\wedge t}\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s\), giving \(\beta_{t_j}(W_s - W_{t_j})\). For later intervals, condition first on \(\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}} \supseteq \mathcal F_s\): \(\mathbb E\bigl[\beta_{t_{i-1}}(W_{t_i\wedge t} - W_{t_{i-1}\wedge t})\bigm|\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}}\bigr] = \beta_{t_{i-1}}\cdot 0\), and the tower rule kills them. What remains is exactly \(\int_0^s\beta_u\,dW_u\).

Proof of 3. Expand the square: \(\sum_i\beta_{t_{i-1}}^2(\Delta W_i)^2 + 2\sum_{i<j}\beta_{t_{i-1}}\beta_{t_{j-1}}\Delta W_i\,\Delta W_j\). For the diagonal terms, conditioning on \(\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}}\), \(\mathbb E[\beta_{t_{i-1}}^2(\Delta W_i)^2] = \mathbb E[\beta_{t_{i-1}}^2]\,(t_i - t_{i-1})\), whose sum is \(\mathbb E\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\). For \(i < j\) everything but \(\Delta W_j\) is \(\mathcal F_{t_{j-1}}\)-measurable and \(\mathbb E[\Delta W_j\mid\mathcal F_{t_{j-1}}] = 0\), so cross terms vanish.

Proof of 4. The integral is a finite sum of independent Gaussians \(\beta_{t_{i-1}}\Delta W_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \beta_{t_{i-1}}^2(t_i - t_{i-1}))\). \(\square\)

An adapted process \(\beta\) is integrable if \(\|\beta\|_T^2 := \mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\bigr] < \infty\). (\(\|\cdot\|_T\) is a norm on processes and satisfies the triangle inequality.)

If \(\beta\) is integrable, there exist simple processes \(\beta_n\) with \(\|\beta - \beta_n\|_T \to 0\). The integrals \(X_n = \int_0^T\beta_{n,u}\,dW_u\) form a Cauchy sequence in \(L^2\), and \(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\) is defined as their \(L^2\) limit, which does not depend on the approximating sequence.

Proof. \((\beta_n)\) is Cauchy for \(\|\cdot\|_T\): \(\|\beta_m - \beta_n\|_T \leq \|\beta_m - \beta\|_T + \|\beta - \beta_n\|_T\). By linearity and the Itô isometry for simple processes, \[\|X_m - X_n\|_{L^2} = \Bigl\|\int_0^T(\beta_{m,u} - \beta_{n,u})\,dW_u\Bigr\|_{L^2} = \|\beta_m - \beta_n\|_T,\] so \((X_n)\) is Cauchy in \(L^2\), and completeness gives a unique limit. \(\square\)

The limit is a random variable, not built path by path; letting the upper limit vary gives the stochastic integral process \(\bigl(\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr)_{t\in[0,T]}\). All four properties pass to the limit: the Itô isometry because \(L^2\) convergence implies convergence of second moments; the martingale property because conditional expectations converge in \(L^2\) and limits are unique; Gaussianity for deterministic integrands because \(L^2\) limits of Gaussians are Gaussian.

Executive summary. For an adapted \(\beta\) with \(\mathbb E\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du < \infty\): the Itô integral exists; \(\bigl(\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr)_t\) is a martingale with zero mean; its variance is \(\mathbb E\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\) (Itô isometry); for deterministic \(\beta\) it is Gaussian.

4.5 Stochastic differential equations

We can now make sense of \[X_t = x + \int_0^t\alpha(u, X_u)\,du + \int_0^t\beta(u, X_u)\,dW_u, \qquad t \in [0, T],\] written in short-hand as the stochastic differential equation (SDE) \[dX_t = \alpha(t, X_t)\,dt + \beta(t, X_t)\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x .\] \(\alpha\) is the drift and \(\beta\) the diffusion (volatility) coefficient. If \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are Lipschitz in \(x\) with linear growth, a unique solution exists (and in many more general cases); the solution is a Markov process.

4.6 Quadratic variation

For a partition \(\pi = \{0 = t_0 < \dots < t_n = t\}\) with mesh \(|\pi| = \max_i(t_i - t_{i-1})\), define \[[W]_t := \lim_{|\pi|\to0}\sum_{i=1}^n(W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}})^2 \qquad\text{(limit in } L^2).\]

\([W]_t = t\) for every \(t \geq 0\).

Proof. Write \(\Delta W_i = W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}\), \(\Delta t_i = t_i - t_{i-1}\) and \(Z_i = \Delta W_i/\sqrt{\Delta t_i}\), i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). Then \(\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 - t = \sum_i\Delta t_i(Z_i^2 - 1)\), a sum of independent zero-mean terms, so \[\Bigl\|\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 - t\Bigr\|_{L^2}^2 = \sum_i\Delta t_i^2\,\mathbb E[(Z_i^2 - 1)^2] = 2\sum_i\Delta t_i^2 \leq 2|\pi|\sum_i\Delta t_i = 2|\pi|t \to 0. \qquad\square\]

Note that \([W]_t = t = \mathrm{Var}(W_t)\). Locally: \(\mathbb E[(\Delta W_t)^2] = \Delta t\) while \(\mathrm{Var}((\Delta W_t)^2) = 2\Delta t^2\), so as \(\Delta t \to 0\) the squared increment becomes deterministic, \((\Delta W_t)^2 \approx \Delta t\). In differential form, \[d[W]_t = dt, \qquad (dW_t)^2 = dt .\]

Brownian paths have infinite first variation \(V_W(t) = \sup_\pi\sum_i|W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}|\) on every interval \([0, t]\).

Proof. \(\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 \leq \max_i|\Delta W_i|\,\sum_i|\Delta W_i| \leq \max_i|\Delta W_i|\,V_W(t)\). As \(|\pi| \to 0\) the left side tends to \(t > 0\), while \(\max_i|\Delta W_i| \to 0\) by continuity of the path. This forces \(V_W(t) = +\infty\). \(\square\)

(For a \(C^1\) function \(f\), \(V_f(t) = \int_0^t|f'(u)|\,du < \infty\) and its quadratic variation is zero.)

4.7 Itô’s formula

If \(X\) solves an SDE, what SDE does \(f(t, X_t)\) solve? Write \(f_t, f_x, f_{xx}\) for the partial derivatives.

Let \(f \in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb R)\) and \(dX_t = \alpha(t, X_t)\,dt + \beta(t, X_t)\,dW_t\). Then \[df(t, X_t) = \Bigl(f_t + \alpha f_x + \frac12\beta^2f_{xx}\Bigr)(t, X_t)\,dt + \beta(t, X_t)\,f_x(t, X_t)\,dW_t .\]

The extra term \(\tfrac12\beta^2f_{xx}\), the Itô correction, is absent from ordinary calculus. It exists because Brownian motion has non-zero quadratic variation.

Heuristic derivation. Over \([t, t + \Delta t]\), \(\Delta X \approx \alpha\Delta t + \beta\Delta W\). A second-order Taylor expansion gives \[f(t + \Delta t, X_t + \Delta X) - f(t, X_t) \approx f_t\Delta t + f_x\Delta X + \tfrac12f_{tt}\Delta t^2 + \tfrac12f_{xx}(\Delta X)^2 + f_{tx}\Delta t\,\Delta X .\] Substituting \(\Delta X\): \((\Delta X)^2 = \alpha^2\Delta t^2 + 2\alpha\beta\Delta t\,\Delta W + \beta^2(\Delta W)^2\). All terms of order higher than \(\Delta t\) — \(\Delta t^2\), \(\Delta t\,\Delta W \sim \Delta t^{3/2}\) — vanish in the limit, but \((\Delta W)^2 \approx \Delta t\) does not: it contributes \(\tfrac12f_{xx}\beta^2\Delta t\). Keeping the first-order terms gives the formula. The rigorous proof makes this precise with the quadratic variation. A convenient mnemonic is the multiplication table \(dt\cdot dt = 0\), \(dt\cdot dW_t = 0\), \(dW_t\cdot dW_t = dt\).

With \(f(t, x) = tx\) and \(X = W\): \(f_t = x\), \(f_x = t\), \(f_{xx} = 0\), so \(d(tW_t) = W_t\,dt + t\,dW_t\), i.e. \(\int_0^ts\,dW_s = tW_t - \int_0^tW_s\,ds\). With \(f(x) = x^2\): \(d(W_t^2) = dt + 2W_t\,dW_t\), i.e. \(\int_0^tW_s\,dW_s = \tfrac12(W_t^2 - t)\) — not \(\tfrac12W_t^2\) as ordinary calculus would suggest.

4.8 Geometric Brownian motion

The SDE \[dX_t = \mu X_t\,dt + \sigma X_t\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x_0 > 0,\] with drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma > 0\) defines geometric Brownian motion (GBM). It is the case \(\alpha(t, x) = \mu x\), \(\beta(t, x) = \sigma x\) of the general SDE, and the starting point of the Black–Scholes model.

\[X_t = x_0\exp\Bigl(\Bigl(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Bigr)t + \sigma W_t\Bigr), \qquad t \geq 0.\]

First derivation (separation of variables). Since the solution is Markov, look for \(X_t = f(t, W_t)\) with \(f(t, x) = g(t)h(x)\). Itô’s formula with \(X = W\) gives \[dX_t = \Bigl(g'(t)h(W_t) + \tfrac12g(t)h''(W_t)\Bigr)dt + g(t)h'(W_t)\,dW_t .\] Matching with \(dX_t = \mu g h\,dt + \sigma gh\,dW_t\): the \(dW_t\) terms give \(h' = \sigma h\), so \(h(x) = e^{\sigma x}\); the \(dt\) terms give \(g' + \tfrac12\sigma^2g = \mu g\), so \(g(t) = g(0)e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t}\); and \(g(0) = x_0\).

Second derivation (logarithm). If \(X > 0\), set \(Y_t = \log X_t\). Itô’s formula with \(f(x) = \log x\) (\(f' = 1/x\), \(f'' = -1/x^2\)): \[dY_t = \frac{1}{X_t}dX_t - \frac{1}{2X_t^2}\sigma^2X_t^2\,dt = \Bigl(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Bigr)dt + \sigma\,dW_t,\] which integrates directly: \(Y_t = \log x_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t\). \(\square\)

Consequences: \(X_t > 0\); \(\log X_t \sim \mathcal N\bigl(\log x_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t, \sigma^2t\bigr)\), i.e. \(X_t\) is log-normal; and \(\mathbb E[X_t] = x_0e^{\mu t}\) (by the exponential martingale), so \(\mu\) is the expected growth rate. The term \(-\sigma^2/2\) in the exponent is the Itô correction: the median grows at rate \(\mu - \sigma^2/2 < \mu\).

4.9 Diffusions and partial differential equations

Itô’s formula connects SDEs with PDEs; this connection is the bridge between the probabilistic (expectation) and analytic (PDE) views of pricing used from Chapter 5 on.

If \(dX_t = \mu_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t\) with \(\mathbb E\int_0^T\sigma_t^2dt < \infty\), then \(X\) is a martingale iff \(\mu_t = 0\) (a.s., for a.e. \(t\)); if \(\mu_t \geq 0\) it is a submartingale.

Proof. \(X_t - X_s = \int_s^t\mu_u\,du + \int_s^t\sigma_u\,dW_u\); the stochastic integral has zero conditional expectation, so \(\mathbb E[X_t - X_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[\int_s^t\mu_u\,du\mid\mathcal F_s]\), which is zero for all \(s < t\) iff \(\mu = 0\), and non-negative if \(\mu \geq 0\). \(\square\)

Consider a diffusion \(dX_s = \mu(s, X_s)\,ds + \sigma(s, X_s)\,dW_s\) started at \(X_t = x\), and its infinitesimal generator \[\mathcal A F(t, x) = \mu(t, x)\,F_x(t, x) + \tfrac12\sigma^2(t, x)\,F_{xx}(t, x).\] By Itô’s formula \(dF(s, X_s) = (F_t + \mathcal AF)(s, X_s)\,ds + \sigma F_x\,dW_s\).

Let \(F\) solve the boundary-value problem \[F_t + \mathcal AF - r(t, x)F = 0, \qquad F(T, x) = \Phi(x).\] Under integrability conditions, \[F(t, x) = \mathbb E_{t,x}\Bigl[e^{-\int_t^Tr(s, X_s)\,ds}\,\Phi(X_T)\Bigr],\] where \(\mathbb E_{t,x}\) denotes expectation for the diffusion started at \(X_t = x\). With a source term, \(F_t + \mathcal AF + k = 0\) and \(F(T, \cdot) = \Phi\) (and \(r = 0\)), \(F(t, x) = \mathbb E_{t,x}[\Phi(X_T)] + \int_t^T\mathbb E_{t,x}[k(s, X_s)]\,ds\).

Proof. Apply Itô’s formula to \(Z_s = e^{-\int_t^sr(u, X_u)du}F(s, X_s)\) on \([t, T]\): the \(ds\)-term is \(e^{-\int r}(F_t + \mathcal AF - rF) = 0\), so \(Z\) is a martingale (given integrability), and \(F(t, x) = Z_t = \mathbb E_{t,x}[Z_T]\). For the source term, \(F(T, X_T) - F(t, x) = -\int_t^Tk\,ds + \int_t^T\sigma F_x\,dW\); take expectations. \(\square\)

The Black–Scholes PDE is the case \(\mu = rx\), \(\sigma(x) = \sigma x\), constant \(r\). Two further PDEs describe the transition density \(p(s, y; t, x)\) of \(X_t\) given \(X_s = y\):

  • Kolmogorov backward equation (in the initial variables): \(\partial_sp + \mu(s, y)\partial_yp + \tfrac12\sigma^2(s, y)\partial_{yy}p = 0\), with \(p(s, \cdot; t, x) \to \delta_x\) as \(s \to t\). It is Feynman–Kač with \(\Phi = \delta_x\).
  • Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) equation (in the final variables): \(\partial_tp = -\partial_x\bigl(\mu(t, x)p\bigr) + \tfrac12\partial_{xx}\bigl(\sigma^2(t, x)p\bigr)\), with \(p \to \delta_y\) as \(t \to s\). It follows from \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[h(X_t)] = \mathbb E[\mathcal Ah(X_t)]\) for test functions \(h\), after integrating by parts.

For Brownian motion with drift (\(\mu = \alpha\), \(\sigma\) constant) the solution is the Gaussian kernel \(p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2(t-s)}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(x - y - \alpha(t-s))^2}{2\sigma^2(t-s)}\bigr)\); Dupire’s equation of Chapter 7 is a forward equation for call prices.

4.10 Exercises

Problems from Björk (B), Chapters 4–5.

(B 4.1) Compute \(dZ\) for (a) \(Z_t = e^{\alpha t}\); (b) \(Z_t = \int_0^tg_s\,dW_s\) with \(g\) adapted; (c) \(Z_t = e^{\alpha W_t}\); (d) \(Z_t = e^{\alpha X_t}\) with \(dX = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW\); (e) \(Z_t = X_t^2\) with \(dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW\).

Solution.

  1. \(dZ = \alpha Z\,dt\). (b) \(dZ = g_t\,dW_t\). (c) \(dZ = \tfrac12\alpha^2Z\,dt + \alpha Z\,dW\). (d) \(dZ = Z\bigl(\alpha\mu + \tfrac12\alpha^2\sigma^2\bigr)dt + \alpha\sigma Z\,dW\). (e) \(dZ = 2X\,dX + (dX)^2 = (2\alpha + \sigma^2)Z\,dt + 2\sigma Z\,dW\): the square of a GBM is a GBM.

(B 4.2) If \(dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW\), find the SDE satisfied by \(Z = 1/X\).

Solution. With \(f(x) = 1/x\), \(f' = -1/x^2\), \(f'' = 2/x^3\): \(dZ = -\frac{dX}{X^2} + \frac{\sigma^2X^2}{X^3}dt = (\sigma^2 - \alpha)Z\,dt - \sigma Z\,dW\). \(Z\) is again a GBM — relevant for exchange rates, where \(1/S\) is the rate quoted the other way round.

(B 4.3) Let \(X_t = \int_0^t\sigma(s)\,dW_s\) with \(\sigma\) deterministic. Using Itô’s formula on \(e^{iuX_t}\), show that \(\mathbb E[e^{iuX_t}] = \exp\bigl(-\tfrac{u^2}{2}\int_0^t\sigma^2(s)\,ds\bigr)\), so that \(X_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \int_0^t\sigma^2)\).

Solution. \(d e^{iuX} = iue^{iuX}\sigma\,dW - \tfrac12u^2\sigma^2e^{iuX}dt\). Taking expectations, \(m(t) = \mathbb E[e^{iuX_t}]\) satisfies \(m' = -\tfrac12u^2\sigma^2(t)m\), \(m(0) = 1\), so \(m(t) = \exp(-\tfrac{u^2}{2}\int_0^t\sigma^2)\), the characteristic function of the stated Gaussian.

(B 4.7) Let \(W^1, W^2\) be independent Brownian motions and \(Q_n = \sum_{k=1}^n\Delta W^1_k\,\Delta W^2_k\) on an equidistant partition of \([0, t]\). Show that \(Q_n \to 0\) in \(L^2\), justifying \(dW^1\,dW^2 = 0\).

Solution. The products are independent with mean \(0\) and variance \(\mathbb E[(\Delta W^1)^2]\mathbb E[(\Delta W^2)^2] = (t/n)^2\). Hence \(\mathbb E[Q_n] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Q_n) = n(t/n)^2 = t^2/n \to 0\).

(B 5.5) If \(dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW\) and \(Y = X^\beta\), show that \(Y\) is a GBM and identify its drift and volatility.

Solution. \(dY = \beta X^{\beta-1}dX + \tfrac12\beta(\beta - 1)X^{\beta-2}\sigma^2X^2dt = Y\bigl(\beta\alpha + \tfrac12\beta(\beta-1)\sigma^2\bigr)dt + \beta\sigma Y\,dW\).

(B 5.1) Show that \(X_t = e^{\alpha t}x_0 + \sigma\int_0^te^{\alpha(t-s)}dW_s\) solves \(dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma\,dW\), \(X_0 = x_0\), and find its mean and variance.

Solution. Write \(X_t = e^{\alpha t}(x_0 + \sigma R_t)\) with \(R_t = \int_0^te^{-\alpha s}dW_s\). By the product rule, \(dX = \alpha X\,dt + e^{\alpha t}\sigma e^{-\alpha t}dW = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma\,dW\). Mean \(e^{\alpha t}x_0\); variance \(\sigma^2\int_0^te^{2\alpha(t-s)}ds = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}(e^{2\alpha t} - 1)\). For \(\alpha = -\kappa < 0\) this is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, with stationary variance \(\sigma^2/(2\kappa)\).

(B 5.11) Solve \(F_t + \tfrac12x^2F_{xx} + x = 0\), \(F(T, x) = \log(x^2)\).

Solution. Feynman–Kač with source \(k = x\) and the diffusion \(dX = X\,dW\), so \(X_s = x\exp(W_s - W_t - (s - t)/2)\) and \(\mathbb E_{t,x}[X_s] = x\). Then \(\mathbb E_{t,x}[\log X_T^2] = 2\log x - (T - t)\) and \[F(t, x) = \log x^2 - (T - t) + x(T - t).\] Check: \(F_t = 1 - x\), \(\tfrac12x^2F_{xx} = \tfrac12x^2(-2/x^2) = -1\), and \(1 - x - 1 + x = 0\). ✓

(B 5.15) For \(dX = \alpha\,dt + \sigma\,dW\), compute the transition density \(p(s, y; t, x)\) and verify the Fokker–Planck equation.

Solution. \(X_t = y + \alpha(t - s) + \sigma(W_t - W_s)\), Gaussian: \(p = (2\pi\sigma^2\tau)^{-1/2}e^{-(x - y - \alpha\tau)^2/(2\sigma^2\tau)}\), \(\tau = t - s\). With \(z = x - y - \alpha\tau\): \(\partial_xp = -\frac{z}{\sigma^2\tau}p\), \(\partial_{xx}p = \bigl(\frac{z^2}{\sigma^4\tau^2} - \frac{1}{\sigma^2\tau}\bigr)p\), \(\partial_tp = \bigl(-\frac{1}{2\tau} + \frac{z^2}{2\sigma^2\tau^2} + \frac{\alpha z}{\sigma^2\tau}\bigr)p\), and indeed \(\partial_tp = -\alpha\,\partial_xp + \tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}p\).

What happens to the binomial model when the number of periods goes to infinity? Time becomes continuous, and the random walk of the stock becomes a process driven by **Brownian motion** ("the task of art today is to bring chaos into order", Adorno). Brownian paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable, so ordinary calculus fails: to write differential equations driven by noise we need the **Itô integral** and its chain rule, **Itô's formula**. This chapter builds these tools, which underlie the Black–Scholes model of Chapter 5.

## Filtrations, processes, martingales

From now on time runs continuously over $[0, T]$.

::: {.definition name="Filtration and adapted process"}
A **filtration** $\mathbb F = (\mathcal F_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ is an increasing family of $\sigma$-algebras ($\mathcal F_s \subseteq \mathcal F_t$ for $s \leq t$): the information available at each time. A **stochastic process** $X = (X_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ is a family of random variables indexed by time; it is **adapted** if $X_t$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable for every $t$.
:::

Recall the rules of conditional expectation: if $\xi$ is independent of $\mathcal F_t$ then $\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t] = \mathbb E[\xi]$ (the information is useless); if $\xi$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable then $\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t] = \xi$; and for $s \leq t$ the **tower property** $\mathbb E\bigl[\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t]\bigm|\mathcal F_s\bigr] = \mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_s]$ holds.

::: {.definition name="Martingale and Markov process"}
An adapted process $X$ is a **martingale** if $\mathbb E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = X_s$ for all $s \leq t$ — constant on average, a "fair game". It is a **Markov process** if $\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s]$ for all $s \leq t$ and bounded $f$: the future depends on the past only through the present value.
:::

## Brownian motion

::: {.definition name="Brownian motion"}
An adapted process $W = (W_t)_{t\geq0}$ with $W_0 = 0$ is a (standard) **Brownian motion** if

1. $W_t - W_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ for all $s \leq t$ (independent increments);
2. $W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal N(0, t - s)$ for all $s \leq t$ (stationary Gaussian increments);
3. $t \mapsto W_t$ is continuous.
:::

A short history: Robert Brown observes the erratic motion of pollen particles in water (1827); Louis Bachelier's thesis *Théorie de la spéculation* (1900) uses it to model stock prices; Einstein explains it physically (1905); Wiener gives a rigorous construction (1923, hence "Wiener process"); Samuelson rediscovers Bachelier in the 1960s; Black, Scholes and Merton publish their option-pricing theory in 1973.

::: {.proposition name="Basic properties"}
For $s \leq t$: $\mathbb E[W_t] = 0$, $\mathrm{Var}(W_t) = t$, $\mathrm{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = s$, and $\mathbb E[e^{\lambda(W_t - W_s)}] = e^{\lambda^2(t-s)/2}$. The processes
$$W_t, \qquad W_t^2 - t, \qquad M_t = e^{\lambda W_t - \lambda^2t/2}$$
are martingales, and $W$ is a Markov process.
:::

*Proof.* Covariance: write $W_t = W_s + (W_t - W_s)$; since $W_s$ is $\mathcal F_s$-measurable and the increment is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ with mean zero, $\mathbb E[W_sW_t] = \mathbb E[W_s^2] + \mathbb E[W_t - W_s]\mathbb E[W_s] = s$.

Martingale: $\mathbb E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s + \mathbb E[W_t - W_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s + \mathbb E[W_t - W_s] = W_s$.

$W_t^2 - t$: $\mathbb E[W_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s^2 + 2W_s\mathbb E[W_t - W_s] + \mathbb E[(W_t - W_s)^2] = W_s^2 + t - s$.

Exponential martingale: $\mathbb E[e^{\lambda W_t}\mid\mathcal F_s] = e^{\lambda W_s}\,\mathbb E[e^{\lambda(W_t - W_s)}] = e^{\lambda W_s}e^{\lambda^2(t-s)/2}$, using the moment generating function of $\mathcal N(0, t-s)$; multiply by $e^{-\lambda^2t/2}$.

Markov: for bounded $f$, $\mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(x + W_t - W_s)]\big|_{x = W_s}$, a function of $W_s$ only; taking $\mathbb E[\,\cdot\mid W_s]$ of both sides gives $\mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid W_s]$. $\square$

### Nowhere differentiability and infinite variation

Brownian paths are continuous but **nowhere differentiable** — "a continuous function made entirely of corners" (think of $|x|$ at $0$, everywhere). Heuristically, $W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t \sim \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$, so
$$\frac{W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t}{\Delta t} \sim \frac{Z}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} \xrightarrow[\Delta t\to0]{} \pm\infty .$$
Paths also have **infinite variation**: they cover an infinite length in any finite interval (proved below from the quadratic variation). Why should we care? Because we want Brownian motion to drive differential equations.

## Differential equations driven by noise

Suppose a quantity evolves over a short interval $\Delta t$ through a locally deterministic **drift** proportional to $\Delta t$ and a Gaussian **noise** with variance proportional to $\Delta t$:
$$X_{t+\Delta t} - X_t = \alpha(t, X_t)\,\Delta t + \beta(t, X_t)\,(W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t), \qquad X_0 = x .$$
Dividing by $\Delta t$ and letting $\Delta t \to 0$ fails, because $dW_t/dt$ does not exist. Instead, on a grid $t_k = k\Delta t$ sum the increments,
$$X_{t_n} = x + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha(t_k, X_{t_k})\Delta t + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta(t_k, X_{t_k})\Delta W_{t_{k+1}},$$
and let $\Delta t \to 0$. The first sum converges to an ordinary (Riemann–Stieltjes) integral $\int_0^t\alpha(u, X_u)\,du$. The second should converge to "$\int_0^t\beta(u, X_u)\,dW_u$", but this cannot be defined path by path as a Riemann–Stieltjes integral, precisely because $W$ has infinite variation. Itô (1944) solved the problem by giving up the pathwise definition and constructing the integral in the space of square-integrable random variables.

## The Itô integral

::: {.definition name="$L^2$ space"}
$L^2$ is the space of random variables with $\mathbb E[X^2] < \infty$, with norm $\|X\| = \mathbb E[X^2]^{1/2}$. $X_n \to X$ **in $L^2$** (in mean square) if $\|X_n - X\| \to 0$. $L^2$ is **complete**: every Cauchy sequence converges to a unique limit. Moreover, $L^2$ limits of Gaussian random variables are Gaussian, and $L^2$ convergence implies $\mathbb E[X_n^2] \to \mathbb E[X^2]$ and $\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal G] \to \mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ in $L^2$ for any $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G$.
:::

The plan: define $\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u$ for simple integrands, prove its properties, then extend by approximation. Integrands are always adapted.

**Step 0.** For a deterministic indicator $\beta_t = c\,\mathbf 1_{[a,b)}(t)$ the only reasonable definition is $\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u = c(W_b - W_a)$.

::: {.definition name="Simple process and its integral"}
$\beta$ is **simple** if there are times $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ such that
$$\beta_t = \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{t_{i-1}}\mathbf 1_{[t_{i-1}, t_i)}(t),$$
with each $\beta_{t_{i-1}}$ square-integrable and $\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}}$-measurable: a piecewise-constant function of time with random heights. Its stochastic integral is
$$\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u := \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_{t_{i-1}}\bigl(W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}\bigr),$$
and $\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u = \sum_i\beta_{t_{i-1}}(W_{t_i\wedge t} - W_{t_{i-1}\wedge t})$ for $t \leq T$.
:::

It is essential that the integrand is evaluated at the **left endpoint** of each interval, against the **forward** increment of $W$: the height is decided before the noise of that interval is revealed. (In finance: you choose your portfolio before prices move.)

::: {.proposition name="Properties for simple integrands"}
For simple $\beta$:

1. **Linearity:** $\int(\beta + \gamma)\,dW = \int\beta\,dW + \int\gamma\,dW$.
2. **Martingale property:** $\mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u\bigm|\mathcal F_s\bigr] = \int_0^s\beta_u\,dW_u$ for $s \leq t$; in particular $\mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr] = 0$.
3. **Itô isometry:** $\mathbb E\Bigl[\bigl(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr)^2\Bigr] = \mathbb E\Bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\Bigr]$; hence $\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr) = \mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\bigr]$.
4. If $\beta$ is **deterministic**, $\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u \sim \mathcal N\bigl(0, \int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\bigr)$.
:::

*Proof of 2.* Each term contributes $\beta_{t_{i-1}}(W_{t_i\wedge t} - W_{t_{i-1}\wedge t})$. Terms entirely before $s$ are $\mathcal F_s$-measurable and stay. For the interval $[t_j, t_{j+1})$ containing $s$, $\beta_{t_j}$ is $\mathcal F_s$-measurable and $\mathbb E[W_{t_{j+1}\wedge t}\mid\mathcal F_s] = W_s$, giving $\beta_{t_j}(W_s - W_{t_j})$. For later intervals, condition first on $\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}} \supseteq \mathcal F_s$: $\mathbb E\bigl[\beta_{t_{i-1}}(W_{t_i\wedge t} - W_{t_{i-1}\wedge t})\bigm|\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}}\bigr] = \beta_{t_{i-1}}\cdot 0$, and the tower rule kills them. What remains is exactly $\int_0^s\beta_u\,dW_u$.

*Proof of 3.* Expand the square: $\sum_i\beta_{t_{i-1}}^2(\Delta W_i)^2 + 2\sum_{i<j}\beta_{t_{i-1}}\beta_{t_{j-1}}\Delta W_i\,\Delta W_j$. For the diagonal terms, conditioning on $\mathcal F_{t_{i-1}}$, $\mathbb E[\beta_{t_{i-1}}^2(\Delta W_i)^2] = \mathbb E[\beta_{t_{i-1}}^2]\,(t_i - t_{i-1})$, whose sum is $\mathbb E\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du$. For $i < j$ everything but $\Delta W_j$ is $\mathcal F_{t_{j-1}}$-measurable and $\mathbb E[\Delta W_j\mid\mathcal F_{t_{j-1}}] = 0$, so cross terms vanish.

*Proof of 4.* The integral is a finite sum of independent Gaussians $\beta_{t_{i-1}}\Delta W_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \beta_{t_{i-1}}^2(t_i - t_{i-1}))$. $\square$

::: {.definition name="Integrable processes"}
An adapted process $\beta$ is **integrable** if $\|\beta\|_T^2 := \mathbb E\bigl[\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du\bigr] < \infty$. ($\|\cdot\|_T$ is a norm on processes and satisfies the triangle inequality.)
:::

::: {.theorem name="Construction of the Itô integral"}
If $\beta$ is integrable, there exist simple processes $\beta_n$ with $\|\beta - \beta_n\|_T \to 0$. The integrals $X_n = \int_0^T\beta_{n,u}\,dW_u$ form a Cauchy sequence in $L^2$, and $\int_0^T\beta_u\,dW_u$ is **defined** as their $L^2$ limit, which does not depend on the approximating sequence.
:::

*Proof.* $(\beta_n)$ is Cauchy for $\|\cdot\|_T$: $\|\beta_m - \beta_n\|_T \leq \|\beta_m - \beta\|_T + \|\beta - \beta_n\|_T$. By linearity and the Itô isometry for simple processes,
$$\|X_m - X_n\|_{L^2} = \Bigl\|\int_0^T(\beta_{m,u} - \beta_{n,u})\,dW_u\Bigr\|_{L^2} = \|\beta_m - \beta_n\|_T,$$
so $(X_n)$ is Cauchy in $L^2$, and completeness gives a unique limit. $\square$

The limit is a random variable, not built path by path; letting the upper limit vary gives the **stochastic integral process** $\bigl(\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr)_{t\in[0,T]}$. All four properties pass to the limit: the Itô isometry because $L^2$ convergence implies convergence of second moments; the martingale property because conditional expectations converge in $L^2$ and limits are unique; Gaussianity for deterministic integrands because $L^2$ limits of Gaussians are Gaussian.

::: {.callout-note}
**Executive summary.** For an adapted $\beta$ with $\mathbb E\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du < \infty$: the Itô integral exists; $\bigl(\int_0^t\beta_u\,dW_u\bigr)_t$ is a martingale with zero mean; its variance is $\mathbb E\int_0^T\beta_u^2\,du$ (Itô isometry); for deterministic $\beta$ it is Gaussian.
:::

## Stochastic differential equations

We can now make sense of
$$X_t = x + \int_0^t\alpha(u, X_u)\,du + \int_0^t\beta(u, X_u)\,dW_u, \qquad t \in [0, T],$$
written in short-hand as the **stochastic differential equation** (SDE)
$$dX_t = \alpha(t, X_t)\,dt + \beta(t, X_t)\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x .$$
$\alpha$ is the **drift** and $\beta$ the **diffusion** (volatility) coefficient. If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are Lipschitz in $x$ with linear growth, a unique solution exists (and in many more general cases); the solution is a **Markov process**.

## Quadratic variation

For a partition $\pi = \{0 = t_0 < \dots < t_n = t\}$ with mesh $|\pi| = \max_i(t_i - t_{i-1})$, define
$$[W]_t := \lim_{|\pi|\to0}\sum_{i=1}^n(W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}})^2 \qquad\text{(limit in } L^2).$$

::: {.theorem name="Quadratic variation of Brownian motion"}
$[W]_t = t$ for every $t \geq 0$.
:::

*Proof.* Write $\Delta W_i = W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}$, $\Delta t_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$ and $Z_i = \Delta W_i/\sqrt{\Delta t_i}$, i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0,1)$. Then $\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 - t = \sum_i\Delta t_i(Z_i^2 - 1)$, a sum of independent zero-mean terms, so
$$\Bigl\|\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 - t\Bigr\|_{L^2}^2 = \sum_i\Delta t_i^2\,\mathbb E[(Z_i^2 - 1)^2] = 2\sum_i\Delta t_i^2 \leq 2|\pi|\sum_i\Delta t_i = 2|\pi|t \to 0. \qquad\square$$

Note that $[W]_t = t = \mathrm{Var}(W_t)$. Locally: $\mathbb E[(\Delta W_t)^2] = \Delta t$ while $\mathrm{Var}((\Delta W_t)^2) = 2\Delta t^2$, so as $\Delta t \to 0$ the squared increment becomes deterministic, $(\Delta W_t)^2 \approx \Delta t$. In differential form,
$$d[W]_t = dt, \qquad (dW_t)^2 = dt .$$

::: {.corollary name="Infinite variation"}
Brownian paths have infinite first variation $V_W(t) = \sup_\pi\sum_i|W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}|$ on every interval $[0, t]$.
:::

*Proof.* $\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 \leq \max_i|\Delta W_i|\,\sum_i|\Delta W_i| \leq \max_i|\Delta W_i|\,V_W(t)$. As $|\pi| \to 0$ the left side tends to $t > 0$, while $\max_i|\Delta W_i| \to 0$ by continuity of the path. This forces $V_W(t) = +\infty$. $\square$

(For a $C^1$ function $f$, $V_f(t) = \int_0^t|f'(u)|\,du < \infty$ and its quadratic variation is zero.)

## Itô's formula

If $X$ solves an SDE, what SDE does $f(t, X_t)$ solve? Write $f_t, f_x, f_{xx}$ for the partial derivatives.

::: {.theorem name="Itô's formula"}
Let $f \in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb R)$ and $dX_t = \alpha(t, X_t)\,dt + \beta(t, X_t)\,dW_t$. Then
$$df(t, X_t) = \Bigl(f_t + \alpha f_x + \frac12\beta^2f_{xx}\Bigr)(t, X_t)\,dt + \beta(t, X_t)\,f_x(t, X_t)\,dW_t .$$
:::

The extra term $\tfrac12\beta^2f_{xx}$, the **Itô correction**, is absent from ordinary calculus. It exists because Brownian motion has non-zero quadratic variation.

*Heuristic derivation.* Over $[t, t + \Delta t]$, $\Delta X \approx \alpha\Delta t + \beta\Delta W$. A second-order Taylor expansion gives
$$f(t + \Delta t, X_t + \Delta X) - f(t, X_t) \approx f_t\Delta t + f_x\Delta X + \tfrac12f_{tt}\Delta t^2 + \tfrac12f_{xx}(\Delta X)^2 + f_{tx}\Delta t\,\Delta X .$$
Substituting $\Delta X$: $(\Delta X)^2 = \alpha^2\Delta t^2 + 2\alpha\beta\Delta t\,\Delta W + \beta^2(\Delta W)^2$. All terms of order higher than $\Delta t$ — $\Delta t^2$, $\Delta t\,\Delta W \sim \Delta t^{3/2}$ — vanish in the limit, but $(\Delta W)^2 \approx \Delta t$ does **not**: it contributes $\tfrac12f_{xx}\beta^2\Delta t$. Keeping the first-order terms gives the formula. The rigorous proof makes this precise with the quadratic variation. A convenient mnemonic is the multiplication table $dt\cdot dt = 0$, $dt\cdot dW_t = 0$, $dW_t\cdot dW_t = dt$.

::: {.example name="Integration by parts"}
With $f(t, x) = tx$ and $X = W$: $f_t = x$, $f_x = t$, $f_{xx} = 0$, so $d(tW_t) = W_t\,dt + t\,dW_t$, i.e. $\int_0^ts\,dW_s = tW_t - \int_0^tW_s\,ds$. With $f(x) = x^2$: $d(W_t^2) = dt + 2W_t\,dW_t$, i.e. $\int_0^tW_s\,dW_s = \tfrac12(W_t^2 - t)$ — not $\tfrac12W_t^2$ as ordinary calculus would suggest.
:::

## Geometric Brownian motion

::: {.definition name="Geometric Brownian motion with drift"}
The SDE
$$dX_t = \mu X_t\,dt + \sigma X_t\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x_0 > 0,$$
with **drift** $\mu$ and **volatility** $\sigma > 0$ defines **geometric Brownian motion** (GBM). It is the case $\alpha(t, x) = \mu x$, $\beta(t, x) = \sigma x$ of the general SDE, and the starting point of the Black–Scholes model.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Explicit solution"}
$$X_t = x_0\exp\Bigl(\Bigl(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Bigr)t + \sigma W_t\Bigr), \qquad t \geq 0.$$
:::

*First derivation (separation of variables).* Since the solution is Markov, look for $X_t = f(t, W_t)$ with $f(t, x) = g(t)h(x)$. Itô's formula with $X = W$ gives
$$dX_t = \Bigl(g'(t)h(W_t) + \tfrac12g(t)h''(W_t)\Bigr)dt + g(t)h'(W_t)\,dW_t .$$
Matching with $dX_t = \mu g h\,dt + \sigma gh\,dW_t$: the $dW_t$ terms give $h' = \sigma h$, so $h(x) = e^{\sigma x}$; the $dt$ terms give $g' + \tfrac12\sigma^2g = \mu g$, so $g(t) = g(0)e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t}$; and $g(0) = x_0$.

*Second derivation (logarithm).* If $X > 0$, set $Y_t = \log X_t$. Itô's formula with $f(x) = \log x$ ($f' = 1/x$, $f'' = -1/x^2$):
$$dY_t = \frac{1}{X_t}dX_t - \frac{1}{2X_t^2}\sigma^2X_t^2\,dt = \Bigl(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Bigr)dt + \sigma\,dW_t,$$
which integrates directly: $Y_t = \log x_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t$. $\square$

Consequences: $X_t > 0$; $\log X_t \sim \mathcal N\bigl(\log x_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t, \sigma^2t\bigr)$, i.e. $X_t$ is **log-normal**; and $\mathbb E[X_t] = x_0e^{\mu t}$ (by the exponential martingale), so $\mu$ is the expected growth rate. The term $-\sigma^2/2$ in the exponent is the Itô correction: the *median* grows at rate $\mu - \sigma^2/2 < \mu$.

## Diffusions and partial differential equations

Itô's formula connects SDEs with PDEs; this connection is the bridge between the probabilistic (expectation) and analytic (PDE) views of pricing used from Chapter 5 on.

::: {.proposition name="Martingales and drift"}
If $dX_t = \mu_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t$ with $\mathbb E\int_0^T\sigma_t^2dt < \infty$, then $X$ is a martingale iff $\mu_t = 0$ (a.s., for a.e. $t$); if $\mu_t \geq 0$ it is a submartingale.
:::

*Proof.* $X_t - X_s = \int_s^t\mu_u\,du + \int_s^t\sigma_u\,dW_u$; the stochastic integral has zero conditional expectation, so $\mathbb E[X_t - X_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[\int_s^t\mu_u\,du\mid\mathcal F_s]$, which is zero for all $s < t$ iff $\mu = 0$, and non-negative if $\mu \geq 0$. $\square$

Consider a diffusion $dX_s = \mu(s, X_s)\,ds + \sigma(s, X_s)\,dW_s$ started at $X_t = x$, and its **infinitesimal generator**
$$\mathcal A F(t, x) = \mu(t, x)\,F_x(t, x) + \tfrac12\sigma^2(t, x)\,F_{xx}(t, x).$$
By Itô's formula $dF(s, X_s) = (F_t + \mathcal AF)(s, X_s)\,ds + \sigma F_x\,dW_s$.

::: {.theorem name="Feynman–Kač representation"}
Let $F$ solve the boundary-value problem
$$F_t + \mathcal AF - r(t, x)F = 0, \qquad F(T, x) = \Phi(x).$$
Under integrability conditions,
$$F(t, x) = \mathbb E_{t,x}\Bigl[e^{-\int_t^Tr(s, X_s)\,ds}\,\Phi(X_T)\Bigr],$$
where $\mathbb E_{t,x}$ denotes expectation for the diffusion started at $X_t = x$. With a source term, $F_t + \mathcal AF + k = 0$ and $F(T, \cdot) = \Phi$ (and $r = 0$), $F(t, x) = \mathbb E_{t,x}[\Phi(X_T)] + \int_t^T\mathbb E_{t,x}[k(s, X_s)]\,ds$.
:::

*Proof.* Apply Itô's formula to $Z_s = e^{-\int_t^sr(u, X_u)du}F(s, X_s)$ on $[t, T]$: the $ds$-term is $e^{-\int r}(F_t + \mathcal AF - rF) = 0$, so $Z$ is a martingale (given integrability), and $F(t, x) = Z_t = \mathbb E_{t,x}[Z_T]$. For the source term, $F(T, X_T) - F(t, x) = -\int_t^Tk\,ds + \int_t^T\sigma F_x\,dW$; take expectations. $\square$

The Black–Scholes PDE is the case $\mu = rx$, $\sigma(x) = \sigma x$, constant $r$. Two further PDEs describe the **transition density** $p(s, y; t, x)$ of $X_t$ given $X_s = y$:

- **Kolmogorov backward equation** (in the initial variables): $\partial_sp + \mu(s, y)\partial_yp + \tfrac12\sigma^2(s, y)\partial_{yy}p = 0$, with $p(s, \cdot; t, x) \to \delta_x$ as $s \to t$. It is Feynman–Kač with $\Phi = \delta_x$.
- **Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) equation** (in the final variables): $\partial_tp = -\partial_x\bigl(\mu(t, x)p\bigr) + \tfrac12\partial_{xx}\bigl(\sigma^2(t, x)p\bigr)$, with $p \to \delta_y$ as $t \to s$. It follows from $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[h(X_t)] = \mathbb E[\mathcal Ah(X_t)]$ for test functions $h$, after integrating by parts.

For Brownian motion with drift ($\mu = \alpha$, $\sigma$ constant) the solution is the Gaussian kernel $p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2(t-s)}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(x - y - \alpha(t-s))^2}{2\sigma^2(t-s)}\bigr)$; Dupire's equation of Chapter 7 is a forward equation for call prices.

## Exercises

Problems from Björk (B), Chapters 4–5.

::: {.exercise}
**(B 4.1)** Compute $dZ$ for (a) $Z_t = e^{\alpha t}$; (b) $Z_t = \int_0^tg_s\,dW_s$ with $g$ adapted; (c) $Z_t = e^{\alpha W_t}$; (d) $Z_t = e^{\alpha X_t}$ with $dX = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$; (e) $Z_t = X_t^2$ with $dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW$.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $dZ = \alpha Z\,dt$. (b) $dZ = g_t\,dW_t$. (c) $dZ = \tfrac12\alpha^2Z\,dt + \alpha Z\,dW$. (d) $dZ = Z\bigl(\alpha\mu + \tfrac12\alpha^2\sigma^2\bigr)dt + \alpha\sigma Z\,dW$. (e) $dZ = 2X\,dX + (dX)^2 = (2\alpha + \sigma^2)Z\,dt + 2\sigma Z\,dW$: the square of a GBM is a GBM.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 4.2)** If $dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW$, find the SDE satisfied by $Z = 1/X$.
:::

::: {.solution}
With $f(x) = 1/x$, $f' = -1/x^2$, $f'' = 2/x^3$: $dZ = -\frac{dX}{X^2} + \frac{\sigma^2X^2}{X^3}dt = (\sigma^2 - \alpha)Z\,dt - \sigma Z\,dW$. $Z$ is again a GBM — relevant for exchange rates, where $1/S$ is the rate quoted the other way round.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 4.3)** Let $X_t = \int_0^t\sigma(s)\,dW_s$ with $\sigma$ deterministic. Using Itô's formula on $e^{iuX_t}$, show that $\mathbb E[e^{iuX_t}] = \exp\bigl(-\tfrac{u^2}{2}\int_0^t\sigma^2(s)\,ds\bigr)$, so that $X_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \int_0^t\sigma^2)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$d e^{iuX} = iue^{iuX}\sigma\,dW - \tfrac12u^2\sigma^2e^{iuX}dt$. Taking expectations, $m(t) = \mathbb E[e^{iuX_t}]$ satisfies $m' = -\tfrac12u^2\sigma^2(t)m$, $m(0) = 1$, so $m(t) = \exp(-\tfrac{u^2}{2}\int_0^t\sigma^2)$, the characteristic function of the stated Gaussian.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 4.7)** Let $W^1, W^2$ be independent Brownian motions and $Q_n = \sum_{k=1}^n\Delta W^1_k\,\Delta W^2_k$ on an equidistant partition of $[0, t]$. Show that $Q_n \to 0$ in $L^2$, justifying $dW^1\,dW^2 = 0$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The products are independent with mean $0$ and variance $\mathbb E[(\Delta W^1)^2]\mathbb E[(\Delta W^2)^2] = (t/n)^2$. Hence $\mathbb E[Q_n] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(Q_n) = n(t/n)^2 = t^2/n \to 0$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 5.5)** If $dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW$ and $Y = X^\beta$, show that $Y$ is a GBM and identify its drift and volatility.
:::

::: {.solution}
$dY = \beta X^{\beta-1}dX + \tfrac12\beta(\beta - 1)X^{\beta-2}\sigma^2X^2dt = Y\bigl(\beta\alpha + \tfrac12\beta(\beta-1)\sigma^2\bigr)dt + \beta\sigma Y\,dW$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 5.1)** Show that $X_t = e^{\alpha t}x_0 + \sigma\int_0^te^{\alpha(t-s)}dW_s$ solves $dX = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, $X_0 = x_0$, and find its mean and variance.
:::

::: {.solution}
Write $X_t = e^{\alpha t}(x_0 + \sigma R_t)$ with $R_t = \int_0^te^{-\alpha s}dW_s$. By the product rule, $dX = \alpha X\,dt + e^{\alpha t}\sigma e^{-\alpha t}dW = \alpha X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$. Mean $e^{\alpha t}x_0$; variance $\sigma^2\int_0^te^{2\alpha(t-s)}ds = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}(e^{2\alpha t} - 1)$. For $\alpha = -\kappa < 0$ this is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, with stationary variance $\sigma^2/(2\kappa)$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 5.11)** Solve $F_t + \tfrac12x^2F_{xx} + x = 0$, $F(T, x) = \log(x^2)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Feynman–Kač with source $k = x$ and the diffusion $dX = X\,dW$, so $X_s = x\exp(W_s - W_t - (s - t)/2)$ and $\mathbb E_{t,x}[X_s] = x$. Then $\mathbb E_{t,x}[\log X_T^2] = 2\log x - (T - t)$ and
$$F(t, x) = \log x^2 - (T - t) + x(T - t).$$
Check: $F_t = 1 - x$, $\tfrac12x^2F_{xx} = \tfrac12x^2(-2/x^2) = -1$, and $1 - x - 1 + x = 0$. ✓
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(B 5.15)** For $dX = \alpha\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, compute the transition density $p(s, y; t, x)$ and verify the Fokker–Planck equation.
:::

::: {.solution}
$X_t = y + \alpha(t - s) + \sigma(W_t - W_s)$, Gaussian: $p = (2\pi\sigma^2\tau)^{-1/2}e^{-(x - y - \alpha\tau)^2/(2\sigma^2\tau)}$, $\tau = t - s$. With $z = x - y - \alpha\tau$: $\partial_xp = -\frac{z}{\sigma^2\tau}p$, $\partial_{xx}p = \bigl(\frac{z^2}{\sigma^4\tau^2} - \frac{1}{\sigma^2\tau}\bigr)p$, $\partial_tp = \bigl(-\frac{1}{2\tau} + \frac{z^2}{2\sigma^2\tau^2} + \frac{\alpha z}{\sigma^2\tau}\bigr)p$, and indeed $\partial_tp = -\alpha\,\partial_xp + \tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}p$.
:::
