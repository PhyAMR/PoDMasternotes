7  Monte Carlo methods for derivatives

The Black–Scholes PDE admits a closed-form solution only for the simplest payoffs on the simplest underlyings. For anything more realistic — path-dependent payoffs (Asian, look-back), high-dimensional baskets, or non-Gaussian dynamics — the only tractable route is Monte Carlo. This chapter is about how to do it well.

7.1 Why Monte Carlo?

The risk-neutral pricing formula \(V_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[f(\mathbf S_T)]\) is an expectation. Monte Carlo replaces it with a sample average:

Given \(N\) i.i.d. samples \(\mathbf S_T^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf S_T^{(N)}\) drawn from the \(\mathbb Q\)-distribution of the terminal state, \[\widehat V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f\bigl(\mathbf S_T^{(i)}\bigr).\] By the CLT, \(\widehat V_0 \to V_0\) with error \(O(\sigma_f/\sqrt N)\), independent of the dimension of \(\mathbf S\).

The rate \(1/\sqrt N\) is the dimension-independent advantage of Monte Carlo over quadrature. For a 10-basket option, quadrature needs \(M^{10}\) points where \(M\) is per-dimension resolution; Monte Carlo needs \(\sim 10^6\) for 2-3 significant digits regardless of the basket size.

7.2 Simulating GBM

For a single log-normal underlying under \(\mathbb Q\), \(S_T = S_0 \exp((r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma\sqrt T\,Z)\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\). Simulation is trivial: draw \(Z\) once per path.

For a path-dependent option we need the whole trajectory. The Euler–Maruyama scheme with step \(\Delta t\):

For \(dX_t = b(X_t)dt + \sigma(X_t)dW_t\) with \(X_0 = x_0\), generate the discrete trajectory \[X_{t + \Delta t} = X_t + b(X_t)\Delta t + \sigma(X_t)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_t, \quad Z_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\text{ i.i.d.}\] For GBM, this is \[S_{t + \Delta t} = S_t(1 + r\Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_t).\]

Remark (Log-scheme). For GBM it is better to simulate \(\ln S\) rather than \(S\), so the scheme has exact increments and works at any \(\Delta t\): \[\ln S_{t + \Delta t} = \ln S_t + (r - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_t .\] This avoids the small-Δt bias of the level scheme.

7.3 Variance reduction

The Monte Carlo error is \(\sigma_f/\sqrt N\). Halving the error means \(4\times\) more samples — very expensive. Variance reduction techniques exploit structure of the problem to reduce \(\sigma_f\) instead.

7.3.1 Antithetic variates

Simulate paths in pairs: \(\mathbf Z^{(i)}\) and \(-\mathbf Z^{(i)}\). The estimator \[\widehat V^{av} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \tfrac12\bigl[f(\mathbf S_T(\mathbf Z^{(i)})) + f(\mathbf S_T(-\mathbf Z^{(i)}))\bigr]\] is unbiased. If \(f\) is monotone in each coordinate of \(\mathbf Z\), the pair has negative correlation and the variance is reduced by up to \(50\%\).

7.3.2 Control variates

Suppose \(Y = f(\mathbf S_T)\) is the target and \(Y' = g(\mathbf S_T)\) is a variable with known expectation \(\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[Y'] = \mu'\). Then for any \(\beta\), \[\widehat V^{cv} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \bigl[Y^{(i)} - \beta(Y'^{(i)} - \mu')\bigr]\] is unbiased with variance \(\text{Var}(\widehat V^{cv}) = \text{Var}(Y)(1 - \rho^2)\), minimised at \(\beta^* = \text{Cov}(Y, Y')/\text{Var}(Y')\). If \(Y'\) is chosen close to \(Y\) (\(\rho \approx 1\)) the variance reduction is dramatic.

For an arithmetic-average Asian call \(\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[(\bar S_T - K)^+]\), the analogous geometric-average call has a closed form (log-normal average). Use it as \(Y'\); typical variance reduction factor \(\sim 50\times\).

7.3.3 Importance sampling

Suppose the payoff \(f\) is non-zero only on a rare event (\(f(\mathbf S_T) > 0\) has probability \(\epsilon \ll 1\)). Under a change of measure \(\mathbb Q \to \tilde{\mathbb Q}\) with density \(L = d\tilde{\mathbb Q}/d\mathbb Q\), \[\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[f(\mathbf S_T)] = \mathbb E^{\tilde{\mathbb Q}}[f(\mathbf S_T)/L(\mathbf S_T)] .\] If \(\tilde{\mathbb Q}\) concentrates on the region where \(f > 0\), the variance of the transformed estimator drops sharply.

For a deep OTM call, \(q = P^{\mathbb Q}(S_T > K)\) is tiny; the Monte Carlo estimator has variance \(\sim K^2 q\). Shift the drift of the log-price by \(\mu \to \mu + \delta\) under an importance measure — this brings more sample paths into the money and can reduce variance by orders of magnitude.

7.4 Low-discrepancy sequences

Sobol’ or Halton sequences are deterministic sequences whose empirical distribution is more uniform than random samples. Using them in place of pseudo-random numbers gives quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC):

For a function \(f\) of bounded Hardy–Krause variation, the QMC error using an \(N\)-point low-discrepancy sequence is bounded by \[|\widehat V^{QMC} - V| \leq V(f)\,D_N^*, \qquad D_N^* = O((\log N)^d/N),\] essentially \(O(1/N)\) up to logarithmic factors, versus \(O(1/\sqrt N)\) for random Monte Carlo.

Remark. QMC is asymptotically faster than plain Monte Carlo but requires “smooth” integrands. For payoffs with kinks (a vanilla call at the strike) the gain is modest; for smooth payoffs (average-strike Asians) the gain can be huge.

7.5 American options: Longstaff–Schwartz

Monte Carlo natively handles European options. American options require a policy choice at every node — an early-exercise rule — that cannot be found by forward simulation alone. The Longstaff–Schwartz algorithm (2001) fits this policy by regression:

For an American option with payoff \(f_t\):

  1. Simulate \(N\) paths \(S^{(i)}_0, \ldots, S^{(i)}_T\) under \(\mathbb Q\).
  2. At maturity \(T\), set the continuation value \(V^{(i)}_T = f_T(S_T^{(i)})\).
  3. Iterating backwards from \(t = T - \Delta t\) to \(0\):
    • Regress the discounted future value \(e^{-r\Delta t} V_{t+\Delta t}^{(i)}\) on a basis of functions of \(S_t^{(i)}\) (typically polynomial), giving an estimated continuation value \(\hat V^{cont}_t(S)\).
    • Set \(V^{(i)}_t = \max(f_t(S^{(i)}_t), \hat V^{cont}_t(S^{(i)}_t))\).
  4. Return \(V_0 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i V^{(i)}_0\).

For an American put on a non-dividend stock, a quadratic regression basis \(\{1, S, S^2\}\) typically suffices to price to \(\sim 1\%\). The optimal exercise boundary is recovered as the level \(S^*\) at which immediate exercise equals continuation.

7.6 Sensitivities and Greeks by Monte Carlo

Estimating derivatives of \(V\) with respect to inputs (Greeks) requires care.

For a smooth payoff \(f\), differentiate inside the expectation: \[\partial_\theta V = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\bigl[\partial_\theta f(S_T(\theta))\bigr] .\] Estimated by MC as \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \partial_\theta f(S_T^{(i)}(\theta))\). Fast but requires \(f\) smooth (fails at the strike of a vanilla call).

For any payoff \(f\), use the identity \[\partial_\theta V = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\bigl[f(S_T)\cdot \partial_\theta \log p(S_T; \theta)\bigr] .\] The score function \(\partial_\theta \log p\) absorbs the derivative into the density; the payoff is not differentiated. Works for any payoff but has higher variance.

7.7 Exercises

Estimate the value of a European call with \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(T = 1\) using Monte Carlo with \(N = 10^4\). Compare with the Black–Scholes formula and the antithetic-variate estimator.

Solution. BS: \(d_1 = (0.05 + 0.02)/0.2 = 0.35\), \(\Phi(d_1) = 0.637\); \(d_2 = 0.15\), \(\Phi(d_2) = 0.560\); \(C_{BS} = 100 \cdot 0.637 - 100 e^{-0.05} \cdot 0.560 = 10.4506\). Plain MC with \(10^4\) paths typically gives \(\hat C = 10.4 \pm 0.3\) (95% CI). Antithetic MC gives \(\hat C = 10.44 \pm 0.2\): about \(30\%\) variance reduction because the call payoff is monotone in \(Z\).

For the Asian call with payoff \((\bar S - K)^+\) where \(\bar S = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n S_{t_i}\): derive the corresponding geometric-average call, express its price in closed form, and use it as a control variate.

Solution. Geometric average: \(S^g = \bigl(\prod S_{t_i}\bigr)^{1/n}\). Under GBM, \(\ln S^g\) is Gaussian with easily-computed mean and variance, so the geometric Asian call has a closed-form BSM-like formula (with adjusted volatility \(\sigma\sqrt{(2n + 1)/(6(n + 1))}\) and adjusted drift). Using the geometric price as \(Y'\) in the control-variate estimator for the arithmetic price gives variance reduction factor \(\sim 50\)–\(100\).

7.8 Callouts

Monte Carlo pricing is the workhorse of quantitative finance because it scales to problems that no closed-form or PDE method can touch: high-dimensional baskets, complex payoffs, multi-factor rate models.

Always report a Monte Carlo estimate with its standard error. A price of “\(10.45\)” from \(N = 10^4\) paths is meaningless without knowing the CI is \(\pm 0.3\) (say).

For high-dimensional integrands, plain Monte Carlo is unbeatable in scaling with dimension, but variance reduction can beat it by a large constant. In production, always implement at least one variance-reduction technique.

QMC’s superior convergence rate requires smoothness of the integrand — often destroyed by kinked payoffs and by high-dimensional effective dimensions (path-dependent options). Do not assume QMC always beats MC.

Naive finite-difference Greeks ((V(θ + h) - V(θ - h))/(2h) with two independent Monte-Carlo runs) have worse variance than the value itself. Always use pathwise or common-random-number techniques.

Estimating derivatives of $V$ with respect to inputs (Greeks) requires care.

::: {.definition name="Pathwise derivative"}
For a smooth payoff $f$, differentiate inside the expectation:
$$\partial_\theta V = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\bigl[\partial_\theta f(S_T(\theta))\bigr] .$$
Estimated by MC as $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \partial_\theta f(S_T^{(i)}(\theta))$. Fast but requires $f$ smooth (fails at the strike of a vanilla call).

For any payoff $f$, use the identity
$$\partial_\theta V = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\bigl[f(S_T)\cdot \partial_\theta \log p(S_T; \theta)\bigr] .$$
The score function $\partial_\theta \log p$ absorbs the derivative into the density; the payoff is not differentiated. Works for any payoff but has higher variance.
:::
