6  Monte Carlo methods

Risk-neutral valuation expresses every price as an expectation, \(\Pi_0(G) = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q[G(S_T)]\). When the payoff or the model is complicated, the expectation cannot be computed in closed form — but it can be estimated by simulation. The Monte Carlo method is simple, universal, and indifferent to the dimension of the problem; its weakness is slow convergence, of order \(1/\sqrt M\) in the number of simulations. This chapter presents the method, analyses its two sources of error, and develops the three classical variance-reduction techniques: antithetic variates, control variates and importance sampling.

6.1 The basic method

In the BSM model, \(\log S_T \sim \mathcal N\bigl(\log S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)T, \sigma^2T\bigr)\) under \(Q\). To price \(G(S_T)\):

  1. simulate \(M\) i.i.d. random variables \(\xi^k\) with the law of \(\log S_T\) (equivalently \(S^k_T = S_0\exp((r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma\sqrt TZ^k)\) with \(Z^k\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0,1)\));
  2. approximate the price by the discounted sample mean \[\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^MG(S^k_T).\]

The justification is the law of large numbers: \(\frac1M\sum_kG(S^k_T) \to \mathbb E^Q[G(S_T)]\) almost surely as \(M \to \infty\).

6.1.1 Path-dependent payoffs

Monte Carlo handles path-dependence naturally. For an average-strike Asian option, \(\Pi_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q\bigl[\bigl(S_T - \tfrac1T\int_0^TS_u\,du\bigr)^+\bigr]\), which has no closed form in the BSM model:

  1. discretise \([0, T]\) with \(t_j = jT/N\), \(j = 0, \dots, N\);
  2. simulate \(M\) discretised paths \((\hat S^k_{t_j})_{j=0,\dots,N}\);
  3. approximate each payoff by \(\bigl(\hat S^k_T - \tfrac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\hat S^k_{t_j}\bigr)^+\) and average.

In general, with \(g(S_t; t \leq T)\) the exact (possibly path-dependent) payoff, \(\hat S\) the discretised process and \(\hat S^k\) its \(k\)-th simulated path, \[\Pi_0(g) = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q[g(S)] \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^Mg(\hat S^k).\]

6.2 Error analysis

The error splits into two parts: \[\Pi_0(g) - e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k) = \underbrace{e^{-rT}\bigl(\mathbb E^Q[g(S)] - \mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)]\bigr)}_{\text{discretisation error (bias)}} + \underbrace{e^{-rT}\Bigl(\mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)] - \frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k)\Bigr)}_{\text{statistical error}} .\]

Statistical error. By the central limit theorem, \[\frac{\sqrt M\bigl(\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k) - \mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)]\bigr)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}^Q(g(\hat S))}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0, 1),\] which gives the asymptotic confidence interval of level \(1 - \alpha\) \[\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k) \pm \frac{q_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt M}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}^Q(g(\hat S))},\] with \(q_{0.975} = 1.96\) for 95%. In practice the variance is replaced by the sample variance. The error decays like \(1/\sqrt M\): one more correct digit costs 100 times more simulations.

Discretisation error (bias). \(\mathrm{Bias} = \mathbb E^Q[g(S)] - \mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)]\). For geometric Brownian motion compare the Euler scheme \[\hat S_{t+\Delta} = \hat S_t\bigl(1 + r\Delta + \sigma\Delta W^Q_t\bigr)\] with the exact scheme \[\hat S_{t+\Delta} = \hat S_t\exp\bigl((r - \sigma^2/2)\Delta + \sigma\Delta W^Q_t\bigr).\] Already after one step, \(\mathbb E^Q[\hat S_\Delta] = S_0(1 + r\Delta) < S_0e^{r\Delta} = \mathbb E^Q[S_\Delta]\) for Euler (e.g. \(101.25\) against \(101.258\) with \(S_0 = 100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(\Delta = 0.25\)), while the exact scheme has no bias: simulating from the exact distribution, when it is known, eliminates the discretisation error.

Mean square error. Accuracy is measured by \[\mathrm{MSE} = \mathbb E\Bigl[\Bigl(\mathbb E[g(S)] - \frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k)\Bigr)^2\Bigr] = \mathrm{Bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k)\Bigr),\] so a good computation balances bias (a finer time grid, \(N \uparrow\)) against sampling error (\(M \uparrow\)) for a given budget. Monte Carlo parallelises trivially.

6.3 Generating random numbers

Every simulation starts from pseudo-random uniform numbers \(U \sim \mathcal U(0,1)\), produced by deterministic generators (linear congruential generators, the Mersenne twister) that must have a long period and pass statistical tests. Other distributions are obtained by transformation:

  • Inversion. If \(F\) is a continuous distribution function, \(X = F^{-1}(U)\) has distribution \(F\), since \(\mathbb P(F^{-1}(U) \leq x) = \mathbb P(U \leq F(x)) = F(x)\). Example: \(X = -\log(U)/\lambda\) is exponential. For the normal distribution \(\Phi^{-1}\) is computed by accurate rational approximations (Moro’s algorithm).
  • Box–Muller. If \(U_1, U_2\) are independent uniforms, \[Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\sin(2\pi U_2)\] are independent \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). (In polar coordinates the squared radius of a standard 2D Gaussian is exponential with mean \(2\) and the angle is uniform.) Marsaglia’s polar method avoids the trigonometric functions by rejection sampling on the unit disc.
  • Correlated normals. For a covariance matrix \(\Sigma = LL^\top\) (Cholesky factorisation), \(X = LZ\) with \(Z\) standard normal has covariance \(\Sigma\). In two dimensions, \(Z_1\) and \(\rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}Z_2\) — the construction of correlated Brownian motions in Chapter 7.

6.4 Discretisation schemes for SDEs

When the law of the solution is not explicit, the SDE \(dX_t = a(X_t)\,dt + b(X_t)\,dW_t\) is integrated numerically on a grid of step \(h = \Delta t\).

\[\text{Euler–Maruyama:}\quad \hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j + a(\hat X_j)h + b(\hat X_j)\Delta W_j,\] \[\text{Milstein:}\quad \hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j + a(\hat X_j)h + b(\hat X_j)\Delta W_j + \tfrac12b(\hat X_j)b'(\hat X_j)\bigl((\Delta W_j)^2 - h\bigr),\] with \(\Delta W_j \sim \mathcal N(0, h)\) independent.

The Milstein correction comes from the next term of the Itô–Taylor expansion, the double integral \(\int\!\!\int dW\,dW = \tfrac12((\Delta W)^2 - h)\). For GBM (\(b(x) = \sigma x\)) it reads \(\hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j\bigl(1 + rh + \sigma\Delta W_j + \tfrac12\sigma^2((\Delta W_j)^2 - h)\bigr)\).

A scheme converges strongly with order \(\gamma\) if \(\mathbb E|X_T - \hat X_T| = O(h^\gamma)\) (pathwise accuracy), and weakly with order \(\beta\) if \(|\mathbb E[g(X_T)] - \mathbb E[g(\hat X_T)]| = O(h^\beta)\) for smooth \(g\) (accuracy of expectations).

Euler has strong order \(\tfrac12\) and weak order \(1\); Milstein has strong order \(1\) (weak order still \(1\)). For pricing, only weak convergence matters, so Euler is often enough; strong convergence matters for path-dependent quantities and for multilevel Monte Carlo. A simulation of GBM (\(\mu = 6\%\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(T = 1\), \(2\times10^4\) paths) illustrates the orders:

steps \(N\) Euler strong error Milstein strong error
16 0.0133 0.00106
64 0.0068 0.00028
256 0.0034 0.00007

Quadrupling \(N\) halves the Euler error (order \(\tfrac12\)) and divides the Milstein error by four (order \(1\)).

Bias–variance budget. With a weak order \(\beta\) the MSE behaves like \(\mathrm{MSE} \approx c_1h^{2\beta} + c_2/N\), while the cost grows like \(N/h\). Minimising the MSE for a fixed budget \(C\) gives \(\mathrm{MSE} = O\bigl(C^{-2\beta/(1 + 2\beta)}\bigr)\): for Euler (\(\beta = 1\)), \(O(C^{-2/3})\), slower than the \(O(C^{-1})\) of exact simulation.

6.5 Variance reduction

Rather than increasing \(M\), reduce the variance of what is being averaged. The three standard techniques exploit increasing amounts of problem-specific information. All the numbers below are for \(S_0 = 100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(T = 1\) and \(M = 10^4\) (95% half-widths).

6.5.1 Antithetic variates

If \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\), then \(-Z\) has the same law: the sample \(\{-Z^k\}\) is as good as \(\{Z^k\}\), and large values in one are paired with small values in the other, so errors tend to cancel.

Two identically distributed random variables \(X\), \(Y\) are antithetic if \(\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) \leq 0\).

Let \(g\) be monotone. Then \(\mathrm{Cov}(g(X), g(-X)) \leq 0\) for any random variable \(X\), and \(\mathrm{Cov}(g(U), g(1 - U)) \leq 0\) for any \(U\). (The first is useful when \(X\) is symmetric about \(0\), the second for \(U \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\).)

Proof. Let \(f(x) = -g(-x)\); \(f\) and \(g\) are monotone in the same direction. With \(Y\) an independent copy of \(X\), \((g(X) - g(Y))(f(X) - f(Y)) \geq 0\). Taking expectations and using independence, \[0 \leq 2\,\mathbb E[g(X)f(X)] - 2\,\mathbb E[g(X)]\,\mathbb E[f(X)] = 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(g(X), f(X)) = -2\,\mathrm{Cov}(g(X), g(-X)). \qquad\square\]

For symmetric \(X\), the estimator \(\frac{g(X) + g(-X)}{2}\) is unbiased for \(\mathbb E[g(X)]\) and \[\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\frac{g(X) + g(-X)}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(g(X))}{2}(1 + \rho), \qquad \rho = \mathrm{Corr}(g(X), g(-X)) \leq 0 .\]

Generate \(Z^k\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\), set \(S^k_T = S_0e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma\sqrt TZ^k}\) and \(S^{-,k}_T = S_0e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)T - \sigma\sqrt TZ^k}\), and estimate \[\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\frac{G(S^k_T) + G(S^{-,k}_T)}{2}.\] For path-dependent payoffs, each path built from increments \(Z^k_j\) is paired with the path built from \(-Z^k_j\).

The estimator is unbiased and consistent, and confidence intervals follow from the CLT applied to the pairs. Its variance is \(\frac{\mathrm{Var}(G(S_T))}{2M}(1 + \rho)\): compared with \(2M\) independent samples (the same number of payoff evaluations) it wins iff \(\rho < 0\), which is guaranteed when \(G\) is monotone.

6.5.2 Control variates

Use the known error on a quantity we can compute to correct the estimate of one we cannot.

Let \(Y\) be a random variable with known mean, and for \(c \in \mathbb R\) let \(\tilde X = X + c(Y - \mathbb E[Y])\). Then \(\mathbb E[\tilde X] = \mathbb E[X]\) and \[\mathrm{Var}(\tilde X) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + c^2\mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2c\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y),\] minimised by \(c^* = -\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)\), which gives \(\mathrm{Var}(\tilde X) = \mathrm{Var}(X)\bigl(1 - \rho_{XY}^2\bigr)\).

The stronger the correlation between \(X\) and the control \(Y\), the larger the reduction; any \(c\) with \(c^2\mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2c\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) < 0\) still helps. Intuition (for \(\rho > 0\)): if the simulated control came out above its known mean, the target was probably overestimated too, and is corrected downwards.

To price \(G(S_T)\) using a payoff \(H(S_T)\) whose price is known, \[\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\Bigl[G(S^k_T) + c\bigl(H(S^k_T) - \mathbb E^Q[H(S_T)]\bigr)\Bigr].\]

The cost is that of plain Monte Carlo. Since \(c^*\) is unknown, one uses the regression estimate \[\hat c = -\frac{\sum_k(G(S^k_T) - \bar G)(H(S^k_T) - \bar H)}{\sum_k(H(S^k_T) - \bar H)^2},\] at the price of a small bias. Choice of control: the underlying itself is universal (\(\mathbb E^Q[S_T] = S_0e^{rT}\)); for exotic payoffs, a tractable relative — for arithmetic Asian options, the geometric Asian option, whose price is explicit because the geometric average of log-normals is log-normal.

6.5.3 Importance sampling

Put more probability on the outcomes that matter.

Let \(Z > 0\) with \(\mathbb E[Z] = 1\) and define the probability \(\tilde{\mathbb P}\) by \(d\tilde{\mathbb P} = Z\,d\mathbb P\). Then \[\mathbb E[X] = \tilde{\mathbb E}\Bigl[\frac XZ\Bigr], \qquad \mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbb P}}\Bigl(\frac XZ\Bigr) = \mathbb E\Bigl[\frac{X^2}{Z}\Bigr] - \mathbb E[X]^2,\] which can be much smaller than \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb E[X^2] - \mathbb E[X]^2\). Any \(Y > 0\) with finite mean gives a valid \(Z = Y/\mathbb E[Y]\). For \(X > 0\), the choice \(Z = X/\mathbb E[X]\) gives zero variance — useless, since it needs the answer — but it shows that \(Z\) should be roughly proportional to \(X\).

In the BSM model a convenient family is \[Z(\nu) = \exp\Bigl(-\nu W^Q_T - \frac{\nu^2}{2}T\Bigr), \qquad \nu \in \mathbb R,\] which is positive with \(\mathbb E^Q[Z(\nu)] = 1\).

The probability \(d\tilde Q_\nu = Z(\nu)\,dQ\) is equivalent to \(Q\), and \(\tilde W^\nu_t = W^Q_t + \nu t\) is a \(\tilde Q_\nu\)-Brownian motion.

Proof. \(\tilde Q_\nu(\Omega) = \mathbb E^Q[Z(\nu)] = 1\), and equivalence holds because \(Z(\nu) > 0\). For \(s \leq t\), \(u \in \mathbb R\) and \(A \in \mathcal F_s\), using the \(Q\)-martingale property of \(e^{-\nu W_t - \nu^2t/2}\) and the independence of increments, \[\mathbb E^{\tilde Q_\nu}\bigl[e^{u(\tilde W^\nu_t - \tilde W^\nu_s)}\mathbf 1_A\bigr] = \mathbb E^Q\Bigl[e^{(u - \nu)(W_t - W_s)}\Bigr]\,e^{-\frac{\nu^2}{2}(t-s) + u\nu(t-s)}\,\mathbb E^Q\bigl[e^{-\nu W_s - \frac{\nu^2}{2}s}\mathbf 1_A\bigr] = e^{\frac{u^2}{2}(t-s)}\,\tilde Q_\nu(A).\] So under \(\tilde Q_\nu\) the increment \(\tilde W^\nu_t - \tilde W^\nu_s\) is \(\mathcal N(0, t - s)\) and independent of \(\mathcal F_s\). \(\square\)

Under \(\tilde Q_\nu\), since \(dW^Q_t = d\tilde W^\nu_t - \nu\,dt\), \[dS_t = (r - \sigma\nu)S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,d\tilde W^\nu_t :\] choosing \(\nu\) shifts the drift and makes high (or low) values of \(S\) more likely. (The course slides write the new drift as \(r - \nu\); with the density \(Z(\nu)\) above it is \(r - \sigma\nu\).)

Choose \(\nu\); generate \(Z^k\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) and set \[S^k_T = S_0\exp\bigl((r - \sigma\nu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})T + \sigma\sqrt TZ^k\bigr), \qquad Z^k(\nu) = \exp\bigl(-\nu\sqrt TZ^k + \tfrac{\nu^2}{2}T\bigr),\] and estimate \(\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_kG(S^k_T)/Z^k(\nu)\).

(\(Z^k(\nu)\) is \(Z(\nu)\) evaluated on the simulated path, using \(W^Q_T = \tilde W^\nu_T - \nu T\).) The estimator is unbiased and consistent, with variance \[\frac1M\Bigl(\mathbb E^Q\Bigl[\frac{G(S_T)^2}{Z(\nu)}\Bigr] - \mathbb E^Q[G(S_T)]^2\Bigr),\] so \(\nu\) must make \(\mathbb E^Q[G^2/Z(\nu)] < \mathbb E^Q[G^2]\). For a deep out-of-the-money call (\(K \gg S_0\)) almost all plain samples pay zero; choosing \(\nu < 0\) such that \(\mathbb E^{\tilde Q_\nu}[S_T] = K\), i.e. \(\nu = \frac{1}{\sigma}\bigl(r - \frac1T\log\frac{K}{S_0}\bigr)\), centres the simulation on the exercise region. Importance sampling is the tool for rare events: deep out-of-the-money options, credit losses, ruin probabilities in insurance.

Antithetic variates need almost no information but rarely give large gains. Control variates require knowing something about the problem and can be very efficient. Importance sampling is the most delicate: with a good change of measure it gains orders of magnitude, with a bad one it can make things worse. Whenever possible combine techniques, and always validate against a benchmark.

6.6 Exercises

Problems from Seydel (S), Tools for Computational Finance, Chapters 2–3, and Glasserman (G), Chapter 4.

(S 2, Box–Muller) Show that if \(U_1, U_2\) are independent uniforms on \((0,1)\), then \(Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\cos(2\pi U_2)\) and \(Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\sin(2\pi U_2)\) are independent standard normals.

Solution. Set \(R^2 = -2\log U_1\) and \(\Theta = 2\pi U_2\). Then \(R^2\) is exponential with mean \(2\) (density \(\tfrac12e^{-\rho/2}\)) and \(\Theta\) is uniform on \([0, 2\pi)\), independent. The joint density of \((Z_1, Z_2) = (R\cos\Theta, R\sin\Theta)\) is obtained with \(dz_1\,dz_2 = \tfrac12\,d\rho\,d\theta\) (with \(\rho = r^2\)): \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac12e^{-\rho/2}\cdot2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}\), the product of two standard normal densities.

(S 3.13) Implement Monte Carlo for European options in the Black–Scholes model and test it on: (a) a vanilla put with \(S_0 = 5\), \(K = 10\), \(r = 0.06\), \(\sigma = 0.3\), \(T = 1\); (b) an up-and-out call with \(S_0 = 5\), \(K = 6\), \(r = 0.05\), \(\sigma = 0.3\), \(T = 1\) and barrier \(B = 8\). Reference values: \(4.43046\) and \(0.0983\).

Solution.

  1. The exact Black–Scholes put value is \(4.43046\); plain Monte Carlo with \(10^5\) samples of \(S_T\) reproduces it within about \(\pm0.005\). (b) Simulating paths with \(1000\) monitoring dates and killing those that reach \(B\) gives \(\approx 0.102\), above the continuous-barrier value \(0.0983\): with discrete monitoring the path can cross the barrier between dates unnoticed. The bias decays only like \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\); it can be removed by the Brownian-bridge crossing probability (next exercise) or by shifting the barrier by \(e^{-0.5826\,\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\) (Broadie–Glasserman–Kou).

(S 3.11) For a Brownian motion, let \(X_t = W_t - \frac tTW_T\) on \([0, T]\) (the Brownian bridge from \(0\) to \(0\)). Compute \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t)\) and give a way to simulate \(W_t\) conditionally on \(W_T\).

Solution. \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t - 2\frac tT t + \frac{t^2}{T^2}T = t\bigl(1 - \frac tT\bigr)\). \(X_t\) is independent of \(W_T\) (zero covariance, jointly Gaussian), so given \(W_T = w\), \(W_t = \frac tTw + \sqrt{t(1 - t/T)}\,Z\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\). This allows generating paths “from the end” (useful with quasi-Monte Carlo) and computing barrier-crossing probabilities between monitoring dates.

(S 3.5) Write the Milstein scheme for GBM, and show that for \(dX = a\,dt + b\,dW\) with constant \(b\) it coincides with Euler.

Solution. For GBM, \(b(x) = \sigma x\), \(b' = \sigma\): \(\hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j\bigl(1 + \mu h + \sigma\Delta W_j + \tfrac12\sigma^2((\Delta W_j)^2 - h)\bigr)\). If \(b\) is constant, \(b' = 0\) and the correction vanishes: Euler already has strong order \(1\) for additive noise.

(Weak error of Euler) For GBM with drift \(\mu\), compute \(\mathbb E[\hat X_T]\) under the Euler scheme with \(N\) steps and compare with \(\mathbb E[X_T] = X_0e^{\mu T}\).

Solution. Each step multiplies the mean by \(1 + \mu h\), so \(\mathbb E[\hat X_T] = X_0(1 + \mu T/N)^N\). Since \((1 + x/N)^N = e^x(1 - x^2/(2N) + O(N^{-2}))\), the error is \(X_0e^{\mu T}\mu^2T^2/(2N) = O(h)\): weak order one. With \(\mu = 6\%\), \(T = 1\), \(N = 16\): \(1.06172\) against \(1.06184\).

(S 3.14) Assume \(\mathrm{MSE}(h, N) = \alpha_1^2h^{2\beta} + \alpha_2^2/N\) and cost \(C = \alpha_3N/h\). Minimise the MSE for a given budget \(C\).

Solution. Substituting \(N = Ch/\alpha_3\): \(\mathrm{MSE}(h) = \alpha_1^2h^{2\beta} + \frac{\alpha_2^2\alpha_3}{Ch}\). Setting the derivative to zero gives \(h^{2\beta+1} = \frac{\alpha_2^2\alpha_3}{2\beta\alpha_1^2C}\), so \(h^* \propto C^{-1/(1+2\beta)}\) and \(\mathrm{MSE}^* \propto C^{-2\beta/(1+2\beta)}\), i.e. \(\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}} \propto C^{-\beta/(1+2\beta)}\): \(C^{-1/3}\) for Euler, against \(C^{-1/2}\) for unbiased simulation.

(S 3.15) For the control variate \(V_\alpha = \hat V + \alpha(\hat V^* - \mathbb E\hat V^*)\), find the optimal \(\alpha\) and the optimal variance ratio \(q_0 = \mathrm{Var}(V_{\alpha_0})/\mathrm{Var}(\hat V)\) as a function of the correlation \(\rho\). Evaluate for \(\rho = 0.95\) and \(\rho = 0.8\).

Solution. \(\alpha_0 = -\mathrm{Cov}(\hat V, \hat V^*)/\mathrm{Var}(\hat V^*)\) and \(q_0 = 1 - \rho^2\): \(0.0975\) for \(\rho = 0.95\) (a tenfold reduction, worth ten times more samples) and \(0.36\) for \(\rho = 0.8\). The gain is very sensitive to \(\rho\) near \(1\).

(G 4) Derive the closed-form price of the discretely monitored geometric Asian call with payoff \(\bigl((\prod_{j=1}^NS_{t_j})^{1/N} - K\bigr)^+\), \(t_j = jT/N\), in the Black–Scholes model.

Solution. \(\frac1N\sum_j\log S_{t_j}\) is Gaussian with mean \(m = \log S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)\Delta\frac{N+1}{2}\) and variance \(v = \sigma^2\Delta\frac{(N+1)(2N+1)}{6N}\), with \(\Delta = T/N\) (using \(\mathrm{Var}(\sum_jW_{t_j}) = \Delta\sum_{i,j}\min(i,j) = \Delta\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}{6}\)). For a log-normal \(e^Y\), \(Y \sim \mathcal N(m, v)\): \(\mathbb E[(e^Y - K)^+] = e^{m + v/2}\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_1 - \sqrt v)\) with \(d_1 = (m - \log K + v)/\sqrt v\); multiply by \(e^{-rT}\). With the parameters of the comparison table this gives \(5.940\).

Risk-neutral valuation expresses every price as an expectation, $\Pi_0(G) = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q[G(S_T)]$. When the payoff or the model is complicated, the expectation cannot be computed in closed form — but it can be **estimated by simulation**. The Monte Carlo method is simple, universal, and indifferent to the dimension of the problem; its weakness is slow convergence, of order $1/\sqrt M$ in the number of simulations. This chapter presents the method, analyses its two sources of error, and develops the three classical variance-reduction techniques: antithetic variates, control variates and importance sampling.

## The basic method

In the BSM model, $\log S_T \sim \mathcal N\bigl(\log S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)T, \sigma^2T\bigr)$ under $Q$. To price $G(S_T)$:

1. simulate $M$ i.i.d. random variables $\xi^k$ with the law of $\log S_T$ (equivalently $S^k_T = S_0\exp((r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma\sqrt TZ^k)$ with $Z^k$ i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0,1)$);
2. approximate the price by the discounted sample mean
$$\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^MG(S^k_T).$$

The justification is the **law of large numbers**: $\frac1M\sum_kG(S^k_T) \to \mathbb E^Q[G(S_T)]$ almost surely as $M \to \infty$.

### Path-dependent payoffs

Monte Carlo handles path-dependence naturally. For an average-strike Asian option, $\Pi_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q\bigl[\bigl(S_T - \tfrac1T\int_0^TS_u\,du\bigr)^+\bigr]$, which has no closed form in the BSM model:

1. discretise $[0, T]$ with $t_j = jT/N$, $j = 0, \dots, N$;
2. simulate $M$ discretised paths $(\hat S^k_{t_j})_{j=0,\dots,N}$;
3. approximate each payoff by $\bigl(\hat S^k_T - \tfrac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\hat S^k_{t_j}\bigr)^+$ and average.

In general, with $g(S_t; t \leq T)$ the exact (possibly path-dependent) payoff, $\hat S$ the discretised process and $\hat S^k$ its $k$-th simulated path,
$$\Pi_0(g) = e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q[g(S)] \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^Mg(\hat S^k).$$

## Error analysis

The error splits into two parts:
$$\Pi_0(g) - e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k) = \underbrace{e^{-rT}\bigl(\mathbb E^Q[g(S)] - \mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)]\bigr)}_{\text{discretisation error (bias)}} + \underbrace{e^{-rT}\Bigl(\mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)] - \frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k)\Bigr)}_{\text{statistical error}} .$$

**Statistical error.** By the central limit theorem,
$$\frac{\sqrt M\bigl(\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k) - \mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)]\bigr)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}^Q(g(\hat S))}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0, 1),$$
which gives the asymptotic confidence interval of level $1 - \alpha$
$$\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k) \pm \frac{q_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt M}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}^Q(g(\hat S))},$$
with $q_{0.975} = 1.96$ for 95%. In practice the variance is replaced by the sample variance. The error decays like $1/\sqrt M$: **one more correct digit costs 100 times more simulations**.

**Discretisation error (bias).** $\mathrm{Bias} = \mathbb E^Q[g(S)] - \mathbb E^Q[g(\hat S)]$. For geometric Brownian motion compare the **Euler scheme**
$$\hat S_{t+\Delta} = \hat S_t\bigl(1 + r\Delta + \sigma\Delta W^Q_t\bigr)$$
with the **exact scheme**
$$\hat S_{t+\Delta} = \hat S_t\exp\bigl((r - \sigma^2/2)\Delta + \sigma\Delta W^Q_t\bigr).$$
Already after one step, $\mathbb E^Q[\hat S_\Delta] = S_0(1 + r\Delta) < S_0e^{r\Delta} = \mathbb E^Q[S_\Delta]$ for Euler (e.g. $101.25$ against $101.258$ with $S_0 = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $\Delta = 0.25$), while the exact scheme has no bias: **simulating from the exact distribution, when it is known, eliminates the discretisation error**.

**Mean square error.** Accuracy is measured by
$$\mathrm{MSE} = \mathbb E\Bigl[\Bigl(\mathbb E[g(S)] - \frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k)\Bigr)^2\Bigr] = \mathrm{Bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\frac1M\sum_kg(\hat S^k)\Bigr),$$
so a good computation balances bias (a finer time grid, $N \uparrow$) against sampling error ($M \uparrow$) for a given budget. Monte Carlo parallelises trivially.

## Generating random numbers

Every simulation starts from **pseudo-random** uniform numbers $U \sim \mathcal U(0,1)$, produced by deterministic generators (linear congruential generators, the Mersenne twister) that must have a long period and pass statistical tests. Other distributions are obtained by transformation:

- **Inversion.** If $F$ is a continuous distribution function, $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has distribution $F$, since $\mathbb P(F^{-1}(U) \leq x) = \mathbb P(U \leq F(x)) = F(x)$. Example: $X = -\log(U)/\lambda$ is exponential. For the normal distribution $\Phi^{-1}$ is computed by accurate rational approximations (Moro's algorithm).
- **Box–Muller.** If $U_1, U_2$ are independent uniforms,
$$Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\sin(2\pi U_2)$$
are independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$. (In polar coordinates the squared radius of a standard 2D Gaussian is exponential with mean $2$ and the angle is uniform.) Marsaglia's **polar method** avoids the trigonometric functions by rejection sampling on the unit disc.
- **Correlated normals.** For a covariance matrix $\Sigma = LL^\top$ (Cholesky factorisation), $X = LZ$ with $Z$ standard normal has covariance $\Sigma$. In two dimensions, $Z_1$ and $\rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}Z_2$ — the construction of correlated Brownian motions in Chapter 7.

## Discretisation schemes for SDEs

When the law of the solution is not explicit, the SDE $dX_t = a(X_t)\,dt + b(X_t)\,dW_t$ is integrated numerically on a grid of step $h = \Delta t$.

::: {.definition name="Euler and Milstein schemes"}
$$\text{Euler–Maruyama:}\quad \hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j + a(\hat X_j)h + b(\hat X_j)\Delta W_j,$$
$$\text{Milstein:}\quad \hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j + a(\hat X_j)h + b(\hat X_j)\Delta W_j + \tfrac12b(\hat X_j)b'(\hat X_j)\bigl((\Delta W_j)^2 - h\bigr),$$
with $\Delta W_j \sim \mathcal N(0, h)$ independent.
:::

The Milstein correction comes from the next term of the Itô–Taylor expansion, the double integral $\int\!\!\int dW\,dW = \tfrac12((\Delta W)^2 - h)$. For GBM ($b(x) = \sigma x$) it reads $\hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j\bigl(1 + rh + \sigma\Delta W_j + \tfrac12\sigma^2((\Delta W_j)^2 - h)\bigr)$.

::: {.definition name="Strong and weak convergence"}
A scheme converges **strongly** with order $\gamma$ if $\mathbb E|X_T - \hat X_T| = O(h^\gamma)$ (pathwise accuracy), and **weakly** with order $\beta$ if $|\mathbb E[g(X_T)] - \mathbb E[g(\hat X_T)]| = O(h^\beta)$ for smooth $g$ (accuracy of expectations).
:::

Euler has strong order $\tfrac12$ and weak order $1$; Milstein has strong order $1$ (weak order still $1$). For **pricing**, only weak convergence matters, so Euler is often enough; strong convergence matters for path-dependent quantities and for multilevel Monte Carlo. A simulation of GBM ($\mu = 6\%$, $\sigma = 30\%$, $T = 1$, $2\times10^4$ paths) illustrates the orders:

| steps $N$ | Euler strong error | Milstein strong error |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| 16 | 0.0133 | 0.00106 |
| 64 | 0.0068 | 0.00028 |
| 256 | 0.0034 | 0.00007 |

Quadrupling $N$ halves the Euler error (order $\tfrac12$) and divides the Milstein error by four (order $1$).

**Bias–variance budget.** With a weak order $\beta$ the MSE behaves like $\mathrm{MSE} \approx c_1h^{2\beta} + c_2/N$, while the cost grows like $N/h$. Minimising the MSE for a fixed budget $C$ gives $\mathrm{MSE} = O\bigl(C^{-2\beta/(1 + 2\beta)}\bigr)$: for Euler ($\beta = 1$), $O(C^{-2/3})$, slower than the $O(C^{-1})$ of exact simulation.

## Variance reduction

Rather than increasing $M$, reduce the variance of what is being averaged. The three standard techniques exploit increasing amounts of problem-specific information. All the numbers below are for $S_0 = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1$ and $M = 10^4$ (95% half-widths).

### Antithetic variates

If $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$, then $-Z$ has the same law: the sample $\{-Z^k\}$ is as good as $\{Z^k\}$, and large values in one are paired with small values in the other, so errors tend to cancel.

::: {.definition name="Antithetic pair"}
Two identically distributed random variables $X$, $Y$ are **antithetic** if $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) \leq 0$.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Monotone functions give antithetic pairs"}
Let $g$ be monotone. Then $\mathrm{Cov}(g(X), g(-X)) \leq 0$ for any random variable $X$, and $\mathrm{Cov}(g(U), g(1 - U)) \leq 0$ for any $U$. (The first is useful when $X$ is symmetric about $0$, the second for $U \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$.)
:::

*Proof.* Let $f(x) = -g(-x)$; $f$ and $g$ are monotone in the same direction. With $Y$ an independent copy of $X$, $(g(X) - g(Y))(f(X) - f(Y)) \geq 0$. Taking expectations and using independence,
$$0 \leq 2\,\mathbb E[g(X)f(X)] - 2\,\mathbb E[g(X)]\,\mathbb E[f(X)] = 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(g(X), f(X)) = -2\,\mathrm{Cov}(g(X), g(-X)). \qquad\square$$

For symmetric $X$, the estimator $\frac{g(X) + g(-X)}{2}$ is unbiased for $\mathbb E[g(X)]$ and
$$\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\frac{g(X) + g(-X)}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(g(X))}{2}(1 + \rho), \qquad \rho = \mathrm{Corr}(g(X), g(-X)) \leq 0 .$$

::: {.definition name="Antithetic Monte Carlo"}
Generate $Z^k$ i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, set $S^k_T = S_0e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma\sqrt TZ^k}$ and $S^{-,k}_T = S_0e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)T - \sigma\sqrt TZ^k}$, and estimate
$$\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\frac{G(S^k_T) + G(S^{-,k}_T)}{2}.$$
For path-dependent payoffs, each path built from increments $Z^k_j$ is paired with the path built from $-Z^k_j$.
:::

The estimator is unbiased and consistent, and confidence intervals follow from the CLT applied to the pairs. Its variance is $\frac{\mathrm{Var}(G(S_T))}{2M}(1 + \rho)$: compared with $2M$ independent samples (the same number of payoff evaluations) it wins iff $\rho < 0$, which is guaranteed when $G$ is monotone.

### Control variates

Use the known error on a quantity we *can* compute to correct the estimate of one we cannot.

::: {.proposition name="Control variate"}
Let $Y$ be a random variable with known mean, and for $c \in \mathbb R$ let $\tilde X = X + c(Y - \mathbb E[Y])$. Then $\mathbb E[\tilde X] = \mathbb E[X]$ and
$$\mathrm{Var}(\tilde X) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + c^2\mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2c\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y),$$
minimised by $c^* = -\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$, which gives $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde X) = \mathrm{Var}(X)\bigl(1 - \rho_{XY}^2\bigr)$.
:::

The stronger the correlation between $X$ and the control $Y$, the larger the reduction; any $c$ with $c^2\mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2c\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) < 0$ still helps. Intuition (for $\rho > 0$): if the simulated control came out above its known mean, the target was probably overestimated too, and is corrected downwards.

::: {.definition name="Control-variate Monte Carlo"}
To price $G(S_T)$ using a payoff $H(S_T)$ whose price is known,
$$\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\Bigl[G(S^k_T) + c\bigl(H(S^k_T) - \mathbb E^Q[H(S_T)]\bigr)\Bigr].$$
:::

The cost is that of plain Monte Carlo. Since $c^*$ is unknown, one uses the regression estimate
$$\hat c = -\frac{\sum_k(G(S^k_T) - \bar G)(H(S^k_T) - \bar H)}{\sum_k(H(S^k_T) - \bar H)^2},$$
at the price of a small bias. Choice of control: the underlying itself is universal ($\mathbb E^Q[S_T] = S_0e^{rT}$); for exotic payoffs, a tractable relative — for **arithmetic Asian options, the geometric Asian option**, whose price is explicit because the geometric average of log-normals is log-normal.

### Importance sampling

Put more probability on the outcomes that matter.

Let $Z > 0$ with $\mathbb E[Z] = 1$ and define the probability $\tilde{\mathbb P}$ by $d\tilde{\mathbb P} = Z\,d\mathbb P$. Then
$$\mathbb E[X] = \tilde{\mathbb E}\Bigl[\frac XZ\Bigr], \qquad \mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbb P}}\Bigl(\frac XZ\Bigr) = \mathbb E\Bigl[\frac{X^2}{Z}\Bigr] - \mathbb E[X]^2,$$
which can be much smaller than $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb E[X^2] - \mathbb E[X]^2$. Any $Y > 0$ with finite mean gives a valid $Z = Y/\mathbb E[Y]$. For $X > 0$, the choice $Z = X/\mathbb E[X]$ gives *zero* variance — useless, since it needs the answer — but it shows that $Z$ should be roughly proportional to $X$.

In the BSM model a convenient family is
$$Z(\nu) = \exp\Bigl(-\nu W^Q_T - \frac{\nu^2}{2}T\Bigr), \qquad \nu \in \mathbb R,$$
which is positive with $\mathbb E^Q[Z(\nu)] = 1$.

::: {.theorem name="Girsanov's theorem (Brownian case)"}
The probability $d\tilde Q_\nu = Z(\nu)\,dQ$ is equivalent to $Q$, and $\tilde W^\nu_t = W^Q_t + \nu t$ is a $\tilde Q_\nu$-Brownian motion.
:::

*Proof.* $\tilde Q_\nu(\Omega) = \mathbb E^Q[Z(\nu)] = 1$, and equivalence holds because $Z(\nu) > 0$. For $s \leq t$, $u \in \mathbb R$ and $A \in \mathcal F_s$, using the $Q$-martingale property of $e^{-\nu W_t - \nu^2t/2}$ and the independence of increments,
$$\mathbb E^{\tilde Q_\nu}\bigl[e^{u(\tilde W^\nu_t - \tilde W^\nu_s)}\mathbf 1_A\bigr] = \mathbb E^Q\Bigl[e^{(u - \nu)(W_t - W_s)}\Bigr]\,e^{-\frac{\nu^2}{2}(t-s) + u\nu(t-s)}\,\mathbb E^Q\bigl[e^{-\nu W_s - \frac{\nu^2}{2}s}\mathbf 1_A\bigr] = e^{\frac{u^2}{2}(t-s)}\,\tilde Q_\nu(A).$$
So under $\tilde Q_\nu$ the increment $\tilde W^\nu_t - \tilde W^\nu_s$ is $\mathcal N(0, t - s)$ and independent of $\mathcal F_s$. $\square$

Under $\tilde Q_\nu$, since $dW^Q_t = d\tilde W^\nu_t - \nu\,dt$,
$$dS_t = (r - \sigma\nu)S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,d\tilde W^\nu_t :$$
choosing $\nu$ shifts the drift and makes high (or low) values of $S$ more likely. (The course slides write the new drift as $r - \nu$; with the density $Z(\nu)$ above it is $r - \sigma\nu$.)

::: {.definition name="Importance-sampling Monte Carlo"}
Choose $\nu$; generate $Z^k$ i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0,1)$ and set
$$S^k_T = S_0\exp\bigl((r - \sigma\nu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})T + \sigma\sqrt TZ^k\bigr), \qquad Z^k(\nu) = \exp\bigl(-\nu\sqrt TZ^k + \tfrac{\nu^2}{2}T\bigr),$$
and estimate $\Pi_0(G) \approx e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_kG(S^k_T)/Z^k(\nu)$.
:::

($Z^k(\nu)$ is $Z(\nu)$ evaluated on the simulated path, using $W^Q_T = \tilde W^\nu_T - \nu T$.) The estimator is unbiased and consistent, with variance
$$\frac1M\Bigl(\mathbb E^Q\Bigl[\frac{G(S_T)^2}{Z(\nu)}\Bigr] - \mathbb E^Q[G(S_T)]^2\Bigr),$$
so $\nu$ must make $\mathbb E^Q[G^2/Z(\nu)] < \mathbb E^Q[G^2]$. For a **deep out-of-the-money call** ($K \gg S_0$) almost all plain samples pay zero; choosing $\nu < 0$ such that $\mathbb E^{\tilde Q_\nu}[S_T] = K$, i.e. $\nu = \frac{1}{\sigma}\bigl(r - \frac1T\log\frac{K}{S_0}\bigr)$, centres the simulation on the exercise region. Importance sampling is the tool for **rare events**: deep out-of-the-money options, credit losses, ruin probabilities in insurance.

### Comparison

| case | method | estimate | 95% half-width |
|:--|:--|:--:|:--:|
| call $K = 100$ (exact $10.451$) | plain | 10.304 | 0.286 |
| | antithetic | 10.444 | 0.144 |
| | control variate $S_T$ ($\rho = 0.92$) | 10.455 | 0.110 |
| call $K = 160$ (exact $0.159$) | plain | 0.167 | 0.040 |
| | importance sampling ($\nu = -2.10$) | 0.159 | 0.004 |
| arithmetic Asian, $K = 100$, 12 dates | plain | 6.181 | 0.167 |
| | geometric Asian control ($\rho = 0.9996$) | 6.150 | 0.004 |

| method | complexity | effectiveness | best for |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| antithetic variates | low | moderate | symmetric distributions, monotone payoffs |
| control variates | average | high with a good control | payoffs with a correlated, priceable relative |
| importance sampling | high | very high with a good $Z$ | rare events |

Antithetic variates need almost no information but rarely give large gains. Control variates require knowing something about the problem and can be very efficient. Importance sampling is the most delicate: with a good change of measure it gains orders of magnitude, with a bad one it can make things worse. Whenever possible combine techniques, and always validate against a benchmark.

## Exercises

Problems from Seydel (S), *Tools for Computational Finance*, Chapters 2–3, and Glasserman (G), Chapter 4.

::: {.exercise}
**(S 2, Box–Muller)** Show that if $U_1, U_2$ are independent uniforms on $(0,1)$, then $Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\cos(2\pi U_2)$ and $Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1}\sin(2\pi U_2)$ are independent standard normals.
:::

::: {.solution}
Set $R^2 = -2\log U_1$ and $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$. Then $R^2$ is exponential with mean $2$ (density $\tfrac12e^{-\rho/2}$) and $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$, independent. The joint density of $(Z_1, Z_2) = (R\cos\Theta, R\sin\Theta)$ is obtained with $dz_1\,dz_2 = \tfrac12\,d\rho\,d\theta$ (with $\rho = r^2$): $\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac12e^{-\rho/2}\cdot2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$, the product of two standard normal densities.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(S 3.13)** Implement Monte Carlo for European options in the Black–Scholes model and test it on: (a) a vanilla put with $S_0 = 5$, $K = 10$, $r = 0.06$, $\sigma = 0.3$, $T = 1$; (b) an up-and-out call with $S_0 = 5$, $K = 6$, $r = 0.05$, $\sigma = 0.3$, $T = 1$ and barrier $B = 8$. Reference values: $4.43046$ and $0.0983$.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) The exact Black–Scholes put value is $4.43046$; plain Monte Carlo with $10^5$ samples of $S_T$ reproduces it within about $\pm0.005$. (b) Simulating paths with $1000$ monitoring dates and killing those that reach $B$ gives $\approx 0.102$, *above* the continuous-barrier value $0.0983$: with discrete monitoring the path can cross the barrier between dates unnoticed. The bias decays only like $\sqrt{\Delta t}$; it can be removed by the Brownian-bridge crossing probability (next exercise) or by shifting the barrier by $e^{-0.5826\,\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ (Broadie–Glasserman–Kou).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(S 3.11)** For a Brownian motion, let $X_t = W_t - \frac tTW_T$ on $[0, T]$ (the **Brownian bridge** from $0$ to $0$). Compute $\mathrm{Var}(X_t)$ and give a way to simulate $W_t$ conditionally on $W_T$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t - 2\frac tT t + \frac{t^2}{T^2}T = t\bigl(1 - \frac tT\bigr)$. $X_t$ is independent of $W_T$ (zero covariance, jointly Gaussian), so given $W_T = w$, $W_t = \frac tTw + \sqrt{t(1 - t/T)}\,Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$. This allows generating paths "from the end" (useful with quasi-Monte Carlo) and computing barrier-crossing probabilities between monitoring dates.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(S 3.5)** Write the Milstein scheme for GBM, and show that for $dX = a\,dt + b\,dW$ with constant $b$ it coincides with Euler.
:::

::: {.solution}
For GBM, $b(x) = \sigma x$, $b' = \sigma$: $\hat X_{j+1} = \hat X_j\bigl(1 + \mu h + \sigma\Delta W_j + \tfrac12\sigma^2((\Delta W_j)^2 - h)\bigr)$. If $b$ is constant, $b' = 0$ and the correction vanishes: Euler already has strong order $1$ for additive noise.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(Weak error of Euler)** For GBM with drift $\mu$, compute $\mathbb E[\hat X_T]$ under the Euler scheme with $N$ steps and compare with $\mathbb E[X_T] = X_0e^{\mu T}$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Each step multiplies the mean by $1 + \mu h$, so $\mathbb E[\hat X_T] = X_0(1 + \mu T/N)^N$. Since $(1 + x/N)^N = e^x(1 - x^2/(2N) + O(N^{-2}))$, the error is $X_0e^{\mu T}\mu^2T^2/(2N) = O(h)$: weak order one. With $\mu = 6\%$, $T = 1$, $N = 16$: $1.06172$ against $1.06184$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(S 3.14)** Assume $\mathrm{MSE}(h, N) = \alpha_1^2h^{2\beta} + \alpha_2^2/N$ and cost $C = \alpha_3N/h$. Minimise the MSE for a given budget $C$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Substituting $N = Ch/\alpha_3$: $\mathrm{MSE}(h) = \alpha_1^2h^{2\beta} + \frac{\alpha_2^2\alpha_3}{Ch}$. Setting the derivative to zero gives $h^{2\beta+1} = \frac{\alpha_2^2\alpha_3}{2\beta\alpha_1^2C}$, so $h^* \propto C^{-1/(1+2\beta)}$ and $\mathrm{MSE}^* \propto C^{-2\beta/(1+2\beta)}$, i.e. $\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}} \propto C^{-\beta/(1+2\beta)}$: $C^{-1/3}$ for Euler, against $C^{-1/2}$ for unbiased simulation.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(S 3.15)** For the control variate $V_\alpha = \hat V + \alpha(\hat V^* - \mathbb E\hat V^*)$, find the optimal $\alpha$ and the optimal variance ratio $q_0 = \mathrm{Var}(V_{\alpha_0})/\mathrm{Var}(\hat V)$ as a function of the correlation $\rho$. Evaluate for $\rho = 0.95$ and $\rho = 0.8$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\alpha_0 = -\mathrm{Cov}(\hat V, \hat V^*)/\mathrm{Var}(\hat V^*)$ and $q_0 = 1 - \rho^2$: $0.0975$ for $\rho = 0.95$ (a tenfold reduction, worth ten times more samples) and $0.36$ for $\rho = 0.8$. The gain is very sensitive to $\rho$ near $1$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(G 4)** Derive the closed-form price of the discretely monitored geometric Asian call with payoff $\bigl((\prod_{j=1}^NS_{t_j})^{1/N} - K\bigr)^+$, $t_j = jT/N$, in the Black–Scholes model.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\frac1N\sum_j\log S_{t_j}$ is Gaussian with mean $m = \log S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)\Delta\frac{N+1}{2}$ and variance $v = \sigma^2\Delta\frac{(N+1)(2N+1)}{6N}$, with $\Delta = T/N$ (using $\mathrm{Var}(\sum_jW_{t_j}) = \Delta\sum_{i,j}\min(i,j) = \Delta\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}{6}$). For a log-normal $e^Y$, $Y \sim \mathcal N(m, v)$: $\mathbb E[(e^Y - K)^+] = e^{m + v/2}\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_1 - \sqrt v)$ with $d_1 = (m - \log K + v)/\sqrt v$; multiply by $e^{-rT}$. With the parameters of the comparison table this gives $5.940$.
:::
