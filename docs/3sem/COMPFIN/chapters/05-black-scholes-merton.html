6  The Black–Scholes–Merton model

With Itô calculus in hand we can now derive the Black–Scholes–Merton (BSM) pricing formula — the most influential single result in modern finance. This chapter derives it two ways (PDE via delta-hedging; risk-neutral via Girsanov), computes the Greeks, and discusses the model’s spectacular successes and equally spectacular failures.

6.1 The BSM assumptions

The Black–Scholes–Merton market consists of:

  • One risky asset with dynamics \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\) under \(\mathbb P\) (geometric Brownian motion), constant drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma > 0\).
  • One risk-free money-market account \(B_t = e^{rt}\) with constant \(r\).
  • No dividends, no transaction costs, no taxes, unlimited borrowing and lending at rate \(r\), and continuous trading.

6.2 Delta-hedging derivation

Let \(V(t, S)\) be the value of a self-financing portfolio that replicates a derivative with terminal payoff \(V(T, S_T) = f(S_T)\).

The value \(V(t, S)\) of a European derivative in the BSM market satisfies the parabolic PDE \[\partial_t V + r S \partial_S V + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 S^2 \partial_S^2 V - r V = 0, \qquad V(T, S) = f(S).\]

Proof. Consider the portfolio \(\Pi = V - \Delta S\) with \(\Delta = \partial_S V\). Applying Itô’s lemma to \(V(t, S_t)\) and using \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\), \[dV = (\partial_t V + \mu S \partial_S V + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 S^2 \partial_S^2 V)dt + \sigma S \partial_S V\,dW .\] Choosing \(\Delta = \partial_S V\) kills the \(dW\) term in \(d\Pi\). The remaining portfolio must earn the risk-free rate (no-arbitrage), \(d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt\). Rearranging gives the PDE.

6.3 Black–Scholes formula (call)

The value at time \(t\) of a European call with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) in the BSM market is \[C(t, S) = S\,\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r(T - t)}\,\Phi(d_2),\] where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF and \[d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \tfrac12 \sigma^2)(T - t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T - t}}.\]

Proof. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb Q\), \(dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,d\tilde W\), so \(S_T = S_t \exp((r - \sigma^2/2)(T - t) + \sigma(\tilde W_T - \tilde W_t))\). The pricing formula \(C = e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[(S_T - K)^+ \mid \mathcal F_t]\) evaluates by a Gaussian integral to the stated form.

By put–call parity (Chapter 2): \[P(t, S) = K e^{-r(T-t)}\Phi(-d_2) - S\Phi(-d_1) .\]

6.4 The Greeks

For the BSM call price above, the sensitivities are:

  • \(\Delta = \partial_S C = \Phi(d_1)\)
  • \(\Gamma = \partial_S^2 C = \phi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt{T - t})\)
  • \(\Theta = \partial_t C = -\dfrac{S\phi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{T - t}} - r K e^{-r(T-t)}\Phi(d_2)\)
  • \(\mathcal V = \partial_\sigma C = S\phi(d_1)\sqrt{T - t}\)
  • \(\rho = \partial_r C = K(T-t)e^{-r(T-t)}\Phi(d_2)\)

where \(\phi = \Phi'\) is the standard normal PDF.

Remark (Greeks in practice).

  • Delta is between \(0\) and \(1\) for a call; a trader who is long 100 shares hedges by selling \(100/\Delta\) calls.
  • Gamma peaks at the strike near maturity and is what makes delta-hedging expensive: rebalancing costs scale as \(\int_0^T \tfrac12 \sigma^2 S_t^2 \Gamma\,dt\) in expectation.
  • Vega measures exposure to volatility misspecification; sign is always positive for vanilla options.
  • Theta is the “rent” the option holder pays for the time value of the position.

6.5 Implied volatility and the smile

The BSM formula is invertible in \(\sigma\): given a market price \(C^{mkt}\), there is a unique \(\sigma^{IV}\) such that \(C^{BSM}(\sigma^{IV}) = C^{mkt}\).

The implied volatility \(\sigma^{IV}(K, T)\) of an option with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) is the \(\sigma\) that makes the BSM formula match the market price.

If the BSM model were correct, \(\sigma^{IV}\) would be constant across strikes and maturities. In practice it is not:

For equity index options (SPX, EURO STOXX 50), the implied volatility surface shows a skew: OTM puts have higher IV than ATM options, which have higher IV than OTM calls. The shape is roughly a smile for FX and a skew for equities. This is direct evidence that the BSM assumptions — constant \(\sigma\), log-normal \(S\) — are wrong.

The volatility surface is the central object of modern derivative markets. Chapter 7 studies models that reproduce it.

6.6 Model successes and failures

Remark (What BSM got right). BSM’s contribution is not the formula per se but the methodology: relative pricing by no-arbitrage replication. Every derivative-pricing model in the last 50 years is built on this foundation. The Black–Scholes formula itself remains the universal quotation mechanism (via implied volatility), even though no professional trader believes the model.

Remark (What BSM got wrong).

  • Fat tails. Equity log-returns have kurtosis \(> 3\): extreme moves happen more often than log-normality predicts.
  • Stochastic volatility. \(\sigma\) itself is a random variable that clusters in time (GARCH effects, ARCH regressions).
  • Jumps. Real prices jump — corporate news, macro announcements — and continuous diffusion cannot capture that.
  • Correlation risk. BSM prices individual options; the correlation between options of different strikes matters for structured products and is not in the model.

The 1987 crash, the 1998 LTCM collapse, and the 2008 crisis all involved losses that the standard BSM framework significantly underestimated.

6.7 Feynman–Kac and pricing PDEs

The link between PDE and probabilistic pricing is the Feynman–Kac theorem:

Let \(u(t, x)\) solve the parabolic PDE \[\partial_t u + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 x^2 \partial_x^2 u + r x\partial_x u - r u = 0, \qquad u(T, x) = f(x).\] Then \[u(t, x) = e^{-r(T - t)}\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\bigl[f(X_T) \mid X_t = x\bigr],\] where \(dX_s = r X_s\,ds + \sigma X_s\,d\tilde W_s\) under \(\mathbb Q\).

Feynman–Kac is the two-way street between the PDE and the risk-neutral expectation: you can solve either representation, whichever is more convenient.

6.8 Exercises

Show that the BSM call price satisfies \(C \to \max(S - K e^{-r(T-t)}, 0)\) as \(\sigma \to 0\), and \(C \to S\) as \(\sigma \to \infty\). Interpret physically.

Solution. \(\sigma \to 0\): \(d_1, d_2 \to \pm\infty\) depending on the sign of \(\ln(S/K) + r(T - t)\). If \(S > K e^{-r(T-t)}\), \(\Phi(d_1)\to 1\), \(\Phi(d_2) \to 1\): \(C \to S - Ke^{-r(T-t)}\). If \(S < K e^{-r(T-t)}\), both \(\to 0\): \(C \to 0\). Union: intrinsic value.

\(\sigma \to \infty\): \(d_1 \to \infty\) and \(d_2 \to -\infty\): \(\Phi(d_1) \to 1\), \(\Phi(d_2) \to 0\): \(C \to S\). Physical interpretation: infinite volatility makes every strike equally likely, so the call is worth its underlying (the strike is worth nothing in expectation).

Derive the vega \(\mathcal V = \partial_\sigma C\) by differentiating the BSM formula directly, and verify \(\mathcal V = S\phi(d_1)\sqrt{T - t}\).

Solution. \(\partial_\sigma d_1 = \sqrt{T-t} - d_1/\sigma\), \(\partial_\sigma d_2 = -\sqrt{T - t} - d_2/\sigma\). Chain rule: \(\partial_\sigma C = S\phi(d_1)\partial_\sigma d_1 - Ke^{-r(T-t)}\phi(d_2)\partial_\sigma d_2\). Using the identity \(S\phi(d_1) = Ke^{-r(T-t)}\phi(d_2)\) (from the Gaussian densities and the definition of \(d_1, d_2\)), the two terms combine to \(S\phi(d_1)\sqrt{T - t}\).

A trader is long a call with \(\Delta = 0.7\), \(\Gamma = 0.02\). She wants to be delta-neutral. How many shares does she need to short? If the stock moves by \(\Delta S = 1\), what is her P&L (ignoring theta)?

Solution. Short 0.7 shares per option. After a move of \(\Delta S = 1\): the call gains approximately \(\Delta \cdot \Delta S + \tfrac12 \Gamma (\Delta S)^2 = 0.7 + 0.01 = 0.71\). The short-stock position loses \(0.7 \cdot 1 = 0.7\). Net P&L \(= 0.71 - 0.7 = 0.01\): positive because \(\Gamma > 0\). Gamma is the source of the positive convexity of a long-option position.

6.9 Callouts

The Black–Scholes formula is one of the most-tested equations in finance. Any implementation that produces a positive price, positive vega, and reproduces put–call parity is likely correct; anything else is buggy.

Implied volatility is the language of options trading. Prices vary with spot; IVs vary with fear. Learning to think in IV terms is the single biggest step from student to trader.

Delta-hedging is a dynamic strategy: the option seller must trade continuously to remain hedged. Discrete rebalancing introduces a P&L variance proportional to \(\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma^2 \Delta t\) — an unavoidable cost.

Every BSM Greek assumes the model is right. In a stochastic-volatility world (Chapter 7), the “true” vega differs from the BSM vega, and the extra Greeks (vanna, volga, vomma) matter for FX and structured products.
