6  Continuous random variables

Shannon’s theory extends naturally from discrete to continuous alphabets, but with a twist: continuous entropies can be negative, and the operational meaning of differential entropy is more subtle than that of Shannon entropy. This chapter introduces the differential entropy, the mutual information of continuous variables, and the Gaussian channel — the paradigm of a continuous-alphabet system.

6.1 Differential entropy

For a random variable \(X\) with density \(f_X(x)\), the differential entropy is \[h(X) = -\int f_X(x)\,\log_2 f_X(x)\,dx,\] provided the integral exists.

The formula is the natural continuous analogue of the discrete Shannon entropy, but its interpretation is different:

Remark. \(h(X)\) is not the average number of bits to describe \(X\): continuous variables need infinitely many bits to describe with arbitrary precision. Instead, \(h(X)\) measures the entropy of \(X\) relative to a uniform reference on unit intervals: quantising \(X\) to precision \(\Delta\) gives a discrete variable with entropy \(h(X) - \log_2 \Delta\). The negative differential entropy of a very concentrated density corresponds to a discrete variable that is easier to encode than a uniform bin of size \(\Delta\).

For \(X \sim \text{Unif}[0, a]\): \(h(X) = \log_2 a\). Note \(h < 0\) if \(a < 1\).

For \(X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\): \(h(X) = \tfrac12 \log_2(2\pi e\,\sigma^2)\), independent of \(\mu\).

6.2 Basic properties

Many properties of Shannon entropy carry over unchanged, but with two important exceptions:

  1. \(h(X)\) can be negative (see uniform example).
  2. Scaling: \(h(aX) = h(X) + \log_2 |a|\); translation: \(h(X + c) = h(X)\).
  3. Sub-additivity: \(h(X, Y) \leq h(X) + h(Y)\), equality iff \(X, Y\) independent.
  4. Chain rule: \(h(X, Y) = h(X) + h(Y \mid X)\).
  5. Conditioning reduces entropy: \(h(Y \mid X) \leq h(Y)\).

6.3 Maximum-entropy distributions

Discrete entropies are maximised by the uniform distribution. Continuous ones are maximised by distributions determined by the given constraints:

Among all real random variables with variance \(\sigma^2\), the Gaussian \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) has the largest differential entropy \(h = \tfrac12 \log_2(2\pi e \sigma^2)\).

Proof. Use Gibbs’ inequality: for any density \(f\) with the given variance, \[D(f \| f_{\mathcal N}) = h(f_{\mathcal N}) - h(f) \geq 0,\] where the identity uses only \(\int f x^2 dx = \sigma^2\) and \(\int f = 1\).

  • Bounded support \([a, b]\): uniform maximises \(h\) with \(h = \log_2(b - a)\).
  • Non-negative support, fixed mean \(\mu\): exponential maximises \(h\) with \(h = \log_2(e\,\mu)\).
  • Fixed first and second moments: Gaussian maximises.

6.4 Mutual information for continuous variables

For jointly-distributed \((X, Y)\) with joint density \(f(x, y)\), \[I(X; Y) = \int\!\int f(x, y)\log_2\frac{f(x, y)}{f(x)\,f(y)}\,dx\,dy .\]

Unlike differential entropy, \(I(X; Y) \geq 0\) for continuous variables as well — the KL-divergence structure survives the limit.

\(I(X; Y)\) is invariant under invertible smooth transformations of \(X\) and \(Y\) separately: for diffeomorphisms \(\phi, \psi\), \[I(\phi(X); \psi(Y)) = I(X; Y).\] This is what makes \(I\) a reparameterisation-invariant measure of dependence.

6.5 The Gaussian channel

The additive Gaussian noise channel with power constraint \(P\) is \[Y = X + Z, \qquad Z \sim \mathcal N(0, N),\quad \mathbb E[X^2] \leq P .\] The average power of the transmitted signal is bounded by \(P\); the noise has variance \(N\); both are per channel use.

The capacity of the Gaussian channel is \[C = \tfrac12 \log_2\bigl(1 + P/N\bigr) \quad \text{bits/channel use.}\] The maximising input distribution is \(X \sim \mathcal N(0, P)\).

Proof. Bound \(I(X; Y) = h(Y) - h(Y \mid X) = h(Y) - h(Z)\). Since \(\text{Var}(Y) = \text{Var}(X) + N \leq P + N\) and Gaussian maximises entropy for a given variance, \(h(Y) \leq \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e (P + N))\), with equality iff \(Y\) Gaussian, iff \(X\) Gaussian. Then \(C = \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e(P+N)) - \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e N) = \tfrac12 \log_2(1 + P/N)\).

For a channel of bandwidth \(B\) Hz and noise spectral density \(N_0 / 2\), the (Shannon–Hartley) capacity is \[C = B\,\log_2\bigl(1 + P/(N_0 B)\bigr) \quad \text{bits/second}.\] This is the celebrated formula relating bandwidth, SNR, and channel capacity that governs everything from modem design to DSL to Wi-Fi.

6.6 Parallel Gaussian channels and water-filling

For a bank of \(K\) parallel independent Gaussian channels with individual noise variances \(N_i\) and a total power budget \(\sum P_i \leq P\):

The capacity is \[C = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \tfrac12 \log_2\bigl(1 + P_i/N_i\bigr),\] maximised by allocating power \(P_i = \max(0, \mu - N_i)\), where \(\mu\) is the water level chosen so that \(\sum_i P_i = P\).

The intuition: pour a total volume \(P\) of “water” over containers of depths \(N_i\); deeper containers (nosier channels) receive less water.

Remark (Water-filling in modern communications). Water-filling is what OFDM, DSL, LTE, and 5G do at the physical layer: split the frequency band into sub-carriers with individual SNRs, allocate transmit power to maximise total throughput.

6.7 Exercises

Compute the differential entropy of a Laplace\((\mu, b)\) distribution \(f(x) = (2b)^{-1}\exp(-|x - \mu|/b)\).

Solution. \(h(X) = -\int f \log_2 f = -\int f \log_2\frac{1}{2b} + \log_2 e \int f\,|x - \mu|/b\, dx = \log_2(2b) + \log_2 e\). So \(h = \log_2(2b\,e)\) bits. Interesting comparison: for the same variance \(2b^2\), the Gaussian has entropy \(\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e \cdot 2b^2) = \tfrac12\log_2(4\pi e b^2)\); Laplace has \(h = \log_2(2be) = \tfrac12 \log_2(4b^2 e^2)\). Ratio: Gaussian is larger by factor \(\log_2(\pi/e) \approx 0.20\) bits — Gaussian is the max-ent distribution.

For the Gaussian channel with SNR = 10 dB, compute the capacity in bits/s/Hz.

Solution. \(C = \log_2(1 + 10) = \log_2 11 \approx 3.46\) bits/s/Hz. Modern LTE aims to reach \(\sim 90\%\) of this in the physical layer with efficient turbo/LDPC coding.

Verify that among Gaussian variables the Fisher information \(J(f) = \mathbb E[(\partial_x \log f)^2]\) satisfies \(J(f) = 1/\sigma^2\) and that the entropy-Fisher information identity \(J(f) \geq 2\pi e \cdot 2^{-2h(f)}\) holds with equality iff Gaussian.

Solution. Direct: \(\partial_x \log \mathcal N(x; 0, \sigma^2) = -x/\sigma^2\), so \(J = 1/\sigma^2\). Substituting the Gaussian \(h = \tfrac12 \log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)\): \(2\pi e \cdot 2^{-2h} = 2\pi e / (2\pi e \sigma^2) = 1/\sigma^2 = J\). This is the Stam / Blachman inequality — a Cramér–Rao-like bound in disguise.

6.8 Callouts

Differential entropy is a coordinate-dependent quantity: change of variable \(Y = g(X)\) gives \(h(Y) = h(X) + \mathbb E[\log |g'(X)|]\). Only quantities like \(I(X; Y)\) and KL are coordinate-free.

When you need entropy for a continuous variable, first check whether it fits a maximum-entropy family (Gaussian, exponential, uniform); those admit closed-form answers. Otherwise, quantise and use the discrete entropy formula.

Negative differential entropy is not a sign of an error: it just means the distribution is more concentrated than a unit-support uniform. It becomes a positive quantity again when converted to a discrete entropy at any finite resolution.

The Gaussian capacity \(\tfrac12\log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\) is the practical benchmark of digital communication systems. Anything above it violates Shannon; anything close to it is state-of-the-art coding.

## Differential entropy

::: {.definition name="Differential entropy"}
For a random variable $X$ with density $f_X(x)$, the **differential entropy** is
$$h(X) = -\int f_X(x)\,\log_2 f_X(x)\,dx,$$
provided the integral exists.
:::

::: {.remark}

:::

For $X \sim \text{Unif}[0, a]$: $h(X) = \log_2 a$. Note $h < 0$ if $a < 1$.

$$I(\phi(X); \psi(Y)) = I(X; Y).$$
This is what makes $I$ a *reparameterisation-invariant* measure of dependence.
:::

## The Gaussian channel

::: {.definition name="Gaussian channel"}
The **additive Gaussian noise channel** with power constraint $P$ is
$$Y = X + Z, \qquad Z \sim \mathcal N(0, N),\quad \mathbb E[X^2] \leq P .$$
The average power of the transmitted signal is bounded by $P$; the noise has variance $N$; both are per channel use.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Gaussian channel capacity"}
The capacity of the Gaussian channel is
$$C = \tfrac12 \log_2\bigl(1 + P/N\bigr) \quad \text{bits/channel use.}$$
The maximising input distribution is $X \sim \mathcal N(0, P)$.
:::

*Proof.* Bound $I(X; Y) = h(Y) - h(Y \mid X) = h(Y) - h(Z)$. Since $\text{Var}(Y) = \text{Var}(X) + N \leq P + N$ and Gaussian maximises entropy for a given variance,
$h(Y) \leq \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e (P + N))$, with equality iff $Y$ Gaussian, iff $X$ Gaussian. Then $C = \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e(P+N)) - \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e N) = \tfrac12 \log_2(1 + P/N)$.

::: {.example name="Bandwidth-limited channel"}
For a channel of bandwidth $B$ Hz and noise spectral density $N_0 / 2$, the (Shannon–Hartley) capacity is
$$C = B\,\log_2\bigl(1 + P/(N_0 B)\bigr) \quad \text{bits/second}.$$
This is the celebrated formula relating bandwidth, SNR, and channel capacity that governs everything from modem design to DSL to Wi-Fi.
:::

For a bank of $K$ parallel independent Gaussian channels with individual noise variances $N_i$ and a total power budget $\sum P_i \leq P$:

::: {.theorem name="Water-filling"}
The capacity is
$$C = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \tfrac12 \log_2\bigl(1 + P_i/N_i\bigr),$$
maximised by allocating power $P_i = \max(0, \mu - N_i)$, where $\mu$ is the water level chosen so that $\sum_i P_i = P$.
