6  Continuous random variables

Shannon’s theory extends from discrete to continuous variables with minor changes in formalism — probabilities become densities, sums become integrals — but with an important caveat: the “entropy” of a continuous variable is infinite, and what survives is a finite part, the differential entropy, which can be negative and depends on the choice of coordinates. Mutual information and KL divergence, on the other hand, carry over unchanged. This chapter makes these statements precise and then studies the paradigm of a continuous system, the Gaussian channel.

6.1 Differential entropy

For a random variable \(X\) with probability density \(f_X(x)\), the differential entropy is \[h[X] = -\int f_X(x)\,\log f_X(x)\,dx,\] provided the integral exists.

It is tempting to call this “the Shannon entropy of a continuous variable”. That identification is not correct, as the simplest example shows.

6.1.1 Discretisation: what \(h\) really measures

Let \(X \sim \mathcal U(-L/2, L/2)\). In practice we can only measure \(X\) to some resolution \(\epsilon \ll 1\). Take for simplicity \(L = 2^{m_1}\), \(\epsilon = 2^{-m_2}\). The range splits into \(n_c = L/\epsilon = 2^{m_1 + m_2}\) cells \(c_i\) of width \(\epsilon\), each with probability \(1/n_c\), so at resolution \(\epsilon\) the variable is effectively a discrete \(X_\epsilon \in \{1, \ldots, n_c\}\) with \[H[X_\epsilon] = \log n_c = \log\frac{L}{\epsilon} = \log L + \log\frac1\epsilon .\] The differential entropy is instead \[h[X] = -\int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac1L \log\frac1L\,dx = \log L = \log \operatorname{Vol}(\operatorname{Supp} X).\] Hence \[H[X_\epsilon] = h[X] + \log\frac1\epsilon .\] As \(\epsilon \to 0\), \(H[X_\epsilon] \to \infty\): specifying a real number to infinite precision requires infinite information.

For a (sufficiently regular) density \(f_X\) on \(\mathbb R^k\) and a partition into cells of volume \(\epsilon^k\), \[H[X_\epsilon] = h[X] + \log\frac{1}{\epsilon^k} + O(\epsilon).\]

Proof. Take \(k = 1\). Choose a region \(A\) containing the support, or, for unbounded support, excluding only tails of probability \(O(\epsilon^2)\). Split \(A\) into cells \(c_i\) of width \(\epsilon\) with centres \(x_i\), and let \(X_\epsilon\) be the index of the cell (plus one extra value for \(\bar A\)). For small \(\epsilon\), \[P(X \in c_i) = \int_{c_i} f_X(x)\,dx = f_X(x_i)\,\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2).\] Then \[H[X_\epsilon] = -\sum_i f_X(x_i)\epsilon \log\bigl(f_X(x_i)\epsilon\bigr) + O(\epsilon) = -\sum_i f_X(x_i)\log f_X(x_i)\,\epsilon \;-\; \log\epsilon \sum_i f_X(x_i)\,\epsilon \;+\; O(\epsilon).\] Both sums are Riemann sums: the first converges to \(h[X]\), the second to \(\int f_X = 1\). Thus \(H[X_\epsilon] = h[X] - \log\epsilon + O(\epsilon)\). In \(k\) dimensions, cells have volume \(\epsilon^k\) and \(-\log\epsilon\) becomes \(-\log\epsilon^k\).

So \(h[X]\) is the finite part of the entropy, after subtracting the divergent, resolution-dependent term \(\log(1/\epsilon^k)\). The term \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) is what keeps \(H[X_\epsilon] \geq 0\) even when \(h[X] < 0\).

Remark. \(h[X]\) is not the average number of bits needed to describe \(X\). It measures entropy relative to a uniform reference on unit cells: a negative \(h\) means that \(X\) is more concentrated than a uniform variable on a unit interval, so at any fixed resolution it is cheaper to describe.

Uniform on an interval of length \(L\): \(h = \log L\), negative if \(L < 1\).

Gaussian \(X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\): \(\log f(x) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \tfrac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\log e\), and \(\mathbb E[(X - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2\), so \[h[X] = \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \tfrac12\log e = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\,\sigma^2), \qquad H[X_\epsilon] \simeq \tfrac12 \log\frac{2\pi e\,\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2}.\] Independent of \(\mu\); negative if \(\sigma^2 < 1/(2\pi e)\).

Multivariate Gaussian \(\mathbf X \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)\) in \(\mathbb R^k\): \[h[\mathbf X] = \tfrac12\log\bigl((2\pi e)^k \det\Sigma\bigr), \qquad H[\mathbf X_\epsilon] \simeq \tfrac12\log\bigl((2\pi e)^k\det\Sigma\bigr) + \log\frac1{\epsilon^k}.\] The entropy grows with the volume of the “error ellipsoid”, \(\sqrt{\det \Sigma}\).

Exponential with mean \(\mu\): \(h = \log(e\mu)\).

6.2 Joint, conditional, mutual, relative

The other quantities generalise in the same way. Consider \(\mathbf X \in \mathbb R^k\), \(\mathbf Y \in \mathbb R^l\).

Conditional entropy. \(H_\epsilon[X \mid Y] = H_\epsilon[X, Y] - H_\epsilon[Y] = h[X, Y] - h[Y] + \log\frac{1}{\epsilon^k}\), i.e. \[H_\epsilon[X \mid Y] = h[X \mid Y] + \log\frac1{\epsilon^k}, \qquad h[X \mid Y] = -\int dx\,dy\,f_{XY}(x, y)\log\frac{f_{XY}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} .\] Like \(h[X]\), it is resolution-dependent and can be negative.

Mutual information. The divergent terms cancel: \[I_\epsilon[X : Y] = h[X] + \log\tfrac1{\epsilon^k} + h[Y] + \log\tfrac1{\epsilon^l} - h[X, Y] - \log\tfrac1{\epsilon^{k+l}} = \int dx\,dy\,f_{XY}\log\frac{f_{XY}(x,y)}{f_X(x)f_Y(y)} \equiv i[X : Y].\] Mutual information does not depend on the resolution and coincides with its differential expression. It remains a KL divergence, hence \(\geq 0\).

KL divergence. \[D_{KL}(f \| g) = \int dx\,f(x)\log\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \geq 0,\] again resolution-independent (the \(\epsilon\)’s cancel in the ratio).

If \((\mathbf X, \mathbf Y)\) is jointly Gaussian with marginal covariances \(\Sigma_X, \Sigma_Y\) and joint covariance \(\Sigma_{XY}\), then \[i[\mathbf X : \mathbf Y] = h[\mathbf X] + h[\mathbf Y] - h[\mathbf X, \mathbf Y] = \tfrac12\log\frac{\det\Sigma_X\,\det\Sigma_Y}{\det\Sigma_{XY}}.\] For two scalars with variances \(a, b\) and correlation coefficient \(c\), \[\Sigma_{XY} = \begin{pmatrix} a & c\sqrt{ab} \\ c\sqrt{ab} & b\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det\Sigma_{XY} = ab(1 - c^2),\] so \[i[X : Y] = -\tfrac12\log(1 - c^2).\] It vanishes for \(c = 0\) (for Gaussians, uncorrelated = independent) and diverges as \(|c| \to 1\) (one variable determines the other exactly, and a real number carries infinite information). For \(c = 0.5\), \(i \approx 0.21\) bits; for \(c = 0.9\), \(i \approx 1.2\) bits.

6.3 Properties of differential entropy

  1. \(h[X]\) can be negative.
  2. Translation: \(h[X + c] = h[X]\). Scaling: \(h[aX] = h[X] + \log|a|\); for vectors, \(h[A\mathbf X] = h[\mathbf X] + \log|\det A|\).
  3. More generally, for an invertible smooth \(\mathbf Y = g(\mathbf X)\), \(h[\mathbf Y] = h[\mathbf X] + \mathbb E\bigl[\log|\det J_g(\mathbf X)|\bigr]\) — \(h\) depends on the coordinates.
  4. Chain rule: \(h[X, Y] = h[X] + h[Y \mid X]\).
  5. Conditioning reduces entropy: \(h[Y \mid X] \leq h[Y]\); sub-additivity \(h[X,Y] \leq h[X] + h[Y]\), with equality iff independent.

The scaling rule is just the discretisation formula in disguise: stretching \(X\) by \(a\) is equivalent to measuring it with resolution \(\epsilon/a\).

For diffeomorphisms \(\phi, \psi\) acting separately on \(X\) and \(Y\), \[i[\phi(X) : \psi(Y)] = i[X : Y].\] The Jacobian terms cancel between \(h[X] + h[Y]\) and \(h[X, Y]\). This makes \(i\) a reparameterisation-invariant measure of dependence.

6.4 Maximum-entropy distributions

Discrete entropies are maximised by the uniform distribution. Continuous ones need a constraint (otherwise \(h\) is unbounded), and the maximiser depends on the constraint.

Among all real random variables with variance \(\sigma^2\), the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy, \(h = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)\). In \(\mathbb R^k\), among all vectors with covariance \(\Sigma\), \(\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)\) is the maximiser.

Proof. Let \(f\) have mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), and \(\phi\) be the Gaussian with the same moments. Since \(\log \phi(x)\) is a quadratic polynomial in \(x\), \(\int f\log\phi = \int\phi\log\phi\) (only the first two moments enter). Then \[0 \leq D(f \| \phi) = \int f\log f - \int f\log\phi = -h(f) + h(\phi).\]

  • Support \([a, b]\), no other constraint: uniform, \(h = \log(b - a)\).
  • Support \([0, \infty)\), fixed mean \(\mu\): exponential, \(h = \log(e\mu)\).
  • In general, fixing \(\mathbb E[T_j(X)]\) gives an exponential family \(f(x) \propto \exp(\sum_j \lambda_j T_j(x))\) — the continuous analogue of the Boltzmann distribution.

6.5 The Gaussian channel

The noisy-channel framework extends to continuous inputs and outputs. Let \(Z\) be the input and \(Y\) the output.

\[Y = Z + \eta, \qquad \eta \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2), \qquad f_{Y|Z}(y \mid z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-\frac{(y - z)^2}{2\sigma^2}} .\] The noise is additive, and white if independent across uses. The input must be constrained — otherwise a single real number could carry infinite information. The standard constraint is a power (variance) constraint \(\operatorname{Var}[Z] \leq v^2\).

Under \(\operatorname{Var}[Z] \leq v^2\), the capacity is achieved by a Gaussian input \(Z \sim \mathcal N(\xi, v^2)\) and equals \[C = \frac12 \log\Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr) = \frac12\log(1 + \text{SNR}), \qquad \text{SNR} = \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}.\]

Proof (Proof via maximum entropy). \(I[Z:Y] = h[Y] - h[Y \mid Z] = h[Y] - h[\eta]\), since given \(Z = z\), \(Y\) is just \(\eta\) shifted by \(z\). Since \(Z\) and \(\eta\) are independent, \(\operatorname{Var}[Y] = \operatorname{Var}[Z] + \sigma^2 \leq v^2 + \sigma^2\), so by the max-ent theorem \(h[Y] \leq \tfrac12\log\bigl(2\pi e(v^2 + \sigma^2)\bigr)\), with equality iff \(Y\) is Gaussian — which happens iff \(Z\) is Gaussian (sum of independent variables is Gaussian only if both are). Hence \[C = \tfrac12\log\bigl(2\pi e(v^2 + \sigma^2)\bigr) - \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2) = \tfrac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr).\] Equivalently, using the Gaussian MI formula with \(\Sigma_{ZY} = \begin{pmatrix} v^2 & v^2 \\ v^2 & v^2 + \sigma^2\end{pmatrix}\): \(i = \tfrac12\log\frac{v^2(v^2 + \sigma^2)}{v^2(v^2 + \sigma^2) - v^4}\).

Proof (Proof via the calculus of variations). Maximise \(I[Z:Y]\) over \(f_Z\) with Lagrange multipliers for normalisation and variance: \[\mathcal F[f_Z] = \int dz\,f_Z(z)\Bigl[\int dy\,f(y \mid z)\log\frac{f(y \mid z)}{f_Y(y)} - \lambda - \mu z^2\Bigr], \qquad f_Y(y) = \int dz\,f_Z(z) f(y\mid z).\] Setting \(\delta\mathcal F/\delta f_Z(z^*) = 0\), and noting that the variation of \(f_Y\) inside the logarithm contributes \(-\int dy\,f(y\mid z^*) = -1\) (absorbed into \(\lambda\)), gives \[\int dy\,f(y\mid z^*)\log f(y \mid z^*) - \int dy\,f(y\mid z^*)\log f_Y(y) = \lambda' + \mu z^{*2}.\] The first term is \(-h[\eta]\), a constant. So the Gaussian smoothing of \(\log f_Y\) must be a quadratic function of \(z^*\), which forces \(\log f_Y(y)\) to be quadratic in \(y\): \(f_Y \propto e^{a + cy^2}\), i.e. \(Y\) is Gaussian, hence so is \(Z\).

6.5.1 Decoding a Gaussian channel

With prior \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0, v^2)\) (take \(\xi = 0\)) and likelihood \(Y \mid Z \sim \mathcal N(Z, \sigma^2)\), Bayes’ theorem gives a Gaussian posterior: \[Z \mid Y = y \;\sim\; \mathcal N\Bigl(\frac{v^2}{v^2 + \sigma^2}\,y,\ \Bigl(\frac1{v^2} + \frac1{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigr).\] Precisions add; the posterior mean is a shrinkage of the observation towards the prior mean. The MAP and posterior-mean estimators coincide (the posterior is symmetric and unimodal): \[z' = D(y) = \frac{v^2}{v^2 + \sigma^2}\,y .\] The mean squared error of this decoder is the posterior variance, \[\mathbb E[(z' - Z)^2] = \frac{v^2\sigma^2}{v^2 + \sigma^2} = \sigma^2\,\frac{\text{SNR}}{1 + \text{SNR}},\] which is less than both the prior variance \(v^2\) (ignoring the data) and the noise variance \(\sigma^2\) (trusting the data blindly).

Remark (Two limits). If \(\text{SNR} \to 0\) the posterior reduces to the prior \(\mathcal N(0, v^2)\): the data are useless. If \(\text{SNR} \to \infty\) the posterior reduces to the likelihood \(\mathcal N(y, \sigma^2)\), centred on the observation. Bayesian inference interpolates between “believe the prior” and “believe the data” according to their relative precisions.

6.5.2 Continuous-time signals and bandwidth

A Gaussian channel arises naturally when a time-continuous signal \(z(t)\), \(t \in [0, T]\), is sent through a medium adding white noise. Encode \(N\) numbers \(z_k\) as coefficients of \(N\) orthonormal functions \(\phi_k\): \[z(t) = \sum_{k=1}^N z_k\,\phi_k(t), \qquad z_k = \int_0^T \phi_k(t)\,z(t)\,dt,\] e.g. sines and cosines of frequencies up to \(W\), with \(N = 2WT\) (the Nyquist count). A power constraint \(\frac1T\int_0^T z(t)^2\,dt \leq P\) becomes \(\sum_k z_k^2 \leq TP\), i.e. \(\langle z_k^2\rangle \leq TP/N = P/(2W)\).

The receiver gets \(y(t) = z(t) + \eta(t)\), so \(y_k = z_k + \eta_k\) with \(\eta_k = \int_0^T \phi_k(t)\eta(t)\,dt\). For white noise, \(\mathbb E[\eta(t)] = 0\) and \(\mathbb E[\eta(t)\eta(t')] = \sigma^2\delta(t - t')\): \[\mathbb E[\eta_k] = 0, \qquad \mathbb E[\eta_k\eta_l] = \sigma^2\!\int_0^T\!\!\int_0^T dt\,dt'\,\phi_k(t)\phi_l(t')\delta(t - t') = \sigma^2\!\int_0^T \phi_k\phi_l\,dt = \sigma^2\delta_{kl}.\] The noise components are independent \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\): sending the signal is equivalent to \(N = 2WT\) uses of a Gaussian channel. Writing the (two-sided) noise spectral density as \(\sigma^2 = N_0/2\), the capacity per second is \[C = \frac{N}{T}\cdot\frac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{P/(2W)}{N_0/2}\Bigr) = W\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{P}{N_0 W}\Bigr) \ \text{bits/s},\] the Shannon–Hartley formula, which governs everything from modems to Wi-Fi.

A telephone line with \(W = 3\) kHz and SNR \(= 30\) dB (\(P/(N_0W) = 1000\)) has capacity \(C = 3000\log_2 1001 \approx 30\) kbit/s — the ceiling that analogue modems approached in the 1990s.

6.5.3 Sphere packing: the coding theorem for the Gaussian channel

The noisy-channel coding theorem has a beautiful geometric picture in the Gaussian case. Consider \(n\) uses of the channel.

  • The noise vector \(\boldsymbol\eta \in \mathbb R^n\) has \(\sum_i\eta_i^2 \approx n\sigma^2\) by the law of large numbers: it lies, with overwhelming probability, close to the sphere of radius \(\sqrt{n\sigma^2}\).
  • Given the codeword \(\mathbf z\), the output \(\mathbf y = \mathbf z + \boldsymbol\eta\) lies near a sphere of radius \(\sqrt{n\sigma^2}\) centred on \(\mathbf z\).
  • The inputs have power \(v^2\), so all outputs lie within a sphere of radius \(\sqrt{n(v^2 + \sigma^2)}\).

For codewords to be non-confusable, their noise spheres must not overlap. The number of codewords is at most the ratio of volumes: \[|S| \leq \Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{n(v^2 + \sigma^2)}}{\sqrt{n\sigma^2}}\Bigr)^n = \Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{n/2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\log|S|}{n} \leq \frac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr) = C .\] Random coding shows that this packing bound is (asymptotically) achievable.

6.6 Parallel Gaussian channels and water-filling

For \(K\) independent Gaussian channels with noise variances \(\sigma_i^2\) and a total power budget \(\sum_i P_i \leq P\):

\[C = \max_{\sum P_i \leq P}\sum_{i=1}^{K} \tfrac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{P_i}{\sigma_i^2}\Bigr),\] achieved by \(P_i = \max(0, \mu - \sigma_i^2)\), with the “water level” \(\mu\) fixed by \(\sum_i P_i = P\).

Picture pouring a volume \(P\) of water over a vessel whose floor has heights \(\sigma_i^2\): noisier channels (higher floor) get less power, and very noisy ones none at all. This is what OFDM, DSL and LTE do at the physical layer, allocating power across frequency sub-carriers.

6.7 Exercises

Compute the differential entropy of a Laplace distribution \(f(x) = (2b)^{-1}e^{-|x - \mu|/b}\) and compare it with a Gaussian of the same variance.

Solution. \(h = -\int f\log f = \log(2b) + \log e\,\mathbb E|X - \mu|/b = \log(2b) + \log e = \log(2be)\). The variance is \(2b^2\); the Gaussian with that variance has \(h = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\cdot 2b^2) = \tfrac12\log(4\pi e b^2)\), while Laplace has \(\tfrac12\log(4e^2b^2)\). The difference is \(\tfrac12\log(\pi/e) \approx 0.104\) bits in favour of the Gaussian, as the max-ent theorem requires.

A variable \(X \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\) is measured with resolution \(\epsilon = 10^{-3}\). How many bits does a measurement carry? How many if the unit of measurement is changed so that \(\sigma = 1000\)?

Solution. \(H[X_\epsilon] \approx \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e) + \log_2 10^3 \approx 2.05 + 9.97 = 12.0\) bits. Changing units so that \(\sigma = 1000\) (and keeping \(\epsilon = 10^{-3}\) in the new units) adds \(\log_2 1000 \approx 9.97\) bits: \(h\) grows by \(\log|a|\) and the resolution is effectively finer. Only the ratio \(\sigma/\epsilon\) matters.

Two jointly Gaussian variables have correlation \(c = 0.8\). Compute \(i[X:Y]\). What is \(i[X^3 : e^Y]\)?

Solution. \(i = -\tfrac12\log_2(1 - 0.64) = -\tfrac12\log_2 0.36 \approx 0.737\) bits. Since \(x \mapsto x^3\) and \(y \mapsto e^y\) are diffeomorphisms of their domains, \(i[X^3 : e^Y] = 0.737\) bits as well — even though the transformed variables are no longer Gaussian and their correlation coefficient is different.

For a Gaussian channel with SNR = 10 dB, compute the capacity in bits per channel use, and the MSE of the optimal decoder relative to \(\sigma^2\).

Solution. SNR \(= 10\), so \(C = \tfrac12\log_2 11 \approx 1.73\) bits per use (or \(\log_2 11 \approx 3.46\) bits/s/Hz with \(N = 2WT\) uses). MSE \(= \sigma^2\cdot 10/11 \approx 0.91\,\sigma^2\).

6.8 Callouts

Differential entropy is coordinate-dependent: \(h(g(X)) = h(X) + \mathbb E\log|g'(X)|\). Only \(i[X:Y]\) and \(D_{KL}\) are coordinate-free, because the divergent \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) terms cancel in them.

When you need the entropy of a continuous variable, first check whether it belongs to a maximum-entropy family (Gaussian, exponential, uniform), which admits closed forms. Otherwise quantise and use the discrete formula, remembering the \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) offset.

Negative differential entropy is not a mistake: it means the distribution is more concentrated than a uniform on a unit cell. Any finite-resolution discrete entropy is still positive.

The Gaussian capacity \(\tfrac12\log(1 + \text{SNR})\) per use, or \(W\log(1 + P/N_0W)\) per second, is the benchmark of digital communication.

Shannon's theory extends from discrete to continuous variables with minor changes in formalism — probabilities become densities, sums become integrals — but with an important caveat: the "entropy" of a continuous variable is *infinite*, and what survives is a finite part, the **differential entropy**, which can be negative and depends on the choice of coordinates. Mutual information and KL divergence, on the other hand, carry over unchanged. This chapter makes these statements precise and then studies the paradigm of a continuous system, the Gaussian channel.

## Differential entropy

::: {.definition name="Differential entropy"}
For a random variable $X$ with probability density $f_X(x)$, the **differential entropy** is
$$h[X] = -\int f_X(x)\,\log f_X(x)\,dx,$$
provided the integral exists.
:::

It is tempting to call this "the Shannon entropy of a continuous variable". That identification is not correct, as the simplest example shows.

### Discretisation: what $h$ really measures

::: {.example name="Uniform variable measured with finite resolution"}
Let $X \sim \mathcal U(-L/2, L/2)$. In practice we can only measure $X$ to some resolution $\epsilon \ll 1$. Take for simplicity $L = 2^{m_1}$, $\epsilon = 2^{-m_2}$. The range splits into $n_c = L/\epsilon = 2^{m_1 + m_2}$ cells $c_i$ of width $\epsilon$, each with probability $1/n_c$, so at resolution $\epsilon$ the variable is effectively a discrete $X_\epsilon \in \{1, \ldots, n_c\}$ with
$$H[X_\epsilon] = \log n_c = \log\frac{L}{\epsilon} = \log L + \log\frac1\epsilon .$$
The differential entropy is instead
$$h[X] = -\int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac1L \log\frac1L\,dx = \log L = \log \operatorname{Vol}(\operatorname{Supp} X).$$
Hence
$$H[X_\epsilon] = h[X] + \log\frac1\epsilon .$$
As $\epsilon \to 0$, $H[X_\epsilon] \to \infty$: specifying a real number to infinite precision requires infinite information.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Differential entropy as a limit"}
For a (sufficiently regular) density $f_X$ on $\mathbb R^k$ and a partition into cells of volume $\epsilon^k$,
$$H[X_\epsilon] = h[X] + \log\frac{1}{\epsilon^k} + O(\epsilon).$$
:::

::: {.proof}
Take $k = 1$. Choose a region $A$ containing the support, or, for unbounded support, excluding only tails of probability $O(\epsilon^2)$. Split $A$ into cells $c_i$ of width $\epsilon$ with centres $x_i$, and let $X_\epsilon$ be the index of the cell (plus one extra value for $\bar A$). For small $\epsilon$,
$$P(X \in c_i) = \int_{c_i} f_X(x)\,dx = f_X(x_i)\,\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2).$$
Then
$$H[X_\epsilon] = -\sum_i f_X(x_i)\epsilon \log\bigl(f_X(x_i)\epsilon\bigr) + O(\epsilon) = -\sum_i f_X(x_i)\log f_X(x_i)\,\epsilon \;-\; \log\epsilon \sum_i f_X(x_i)\,\epsilon \;+\; O(\epsilon).$$
Both sums are Riemann sums: the first converges to $h[X]$, the second to $\int f_X = 1$. Thus $H[X_\epsilon] = h[X] - \log\epsilon + O(\epsilon)$. In $k$ dimensions, cells have volume $\epsilon^k$ and $-\log\epsilon$ becomes $-\log\epsilon^k$.
:::

So $h[X]$ is the **finite part** of the entropy, after subtracting the divergent, resolution-dependent term $\log(1/\epsilon^k)$. The term $\log(1/\epsilon)$ is what keeps $H[X_\epsilon] \geq 0$ even when $h[X] < 0$.

::: {.remark}
$h[X]$ is *not* the average number of bits needed to describe $X$. It measures entropy *relative to a uniform reference on unit cells*: a negative $h$ means that $X$ is more concentrated than a uniform variable on a unit interval, so at any fixed resolution it is cheaper to describe.
:::

::: {.example name="Uniform and Gaussian differential entropies"}
*Uniform* on an interval of length $L$: $h = \log L$, negative if $L < 1$.

*Gaussian* $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$: $\log f(x) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \tfrac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\log e$, and $\mathbb E[(X - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$, so
$$h[X] = \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \tfrac12\log e = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\,\sigma^2), \qquad H[X_\epsilon] \simeq \tfrac12 \log\frac{2\pi e\,\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2}.$$
Independent of $\mu$; negative if $\sigma^2 < 1/(2\pi e)$.

*Multivariate Gaussian* $\mathbf X \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$ in $\mathbb R^k$:
$$h[\mathbf X] = \tfrac12\log\bigl((2\pi e)^k \det\Sigma\bigr), \qquad H[\mathbf X_\epsilon] \simeq \tfrac12\log\bigl((2\pi e)^k\det\Sigma\bigr) + \log\frac1{\epsilon^k}.$$
The entropy grows with the volume of the "error ellipsoid", $\sqrt{\det \Sigma}$.

*Exponential* with mean $\mu$: $h = \log(e\mu)$.
:::

## Joint, conditional, mutual, relative

The other quantities generalise in the same way. Consider $\mathbf X \in \mathbb R^k$, $\mathbf Y \in \mathbb R^l$.

**Conditional entropy.** $H_\epsilon[X \mid Y] = H_\epsilon[X, Y] - H_\epsilon[Y] = h[X, Y] - h[Y] + \log\frac{1}{\epsilon^k}$, i.e.
$$H_\epsilon[X \mid Y] = h[X \mid Y] + \log\frac1{\epsilon^k}, \qquad h[X \mid Y] = -\int dx\,dy\,f_{XY}(x, y)\log\frac{f_{XY}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} .$$
Like $h[X]$, it is resolution-dependent and can be negative.

**Mutual information.** The divergent terms cancel:
$$I_\epsilon[X : Y] = h[X] + \log\tfrac1{\epsilon^k} + h[Y] + \log\tfrac1{\epsilon^l} - h[X, Y] - \log\tfrac1{\epsilon^{k+l}} = \int dx\,dy\,f_{XY}\log\frac{f_{XY}(x,y)}{f_X(x)f_Y(y)} \equiv i[X : Y].$$
**Mutual information does not depend on the resolution** and coincides with its differential expression. It remains a KL divergence, hence $\geq 0$.

**KL divergence.**
$$D_{KL}(f \| g) = \int dx\,f(x)\log\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \geq 0,$$
again resolution-independent (the $\epsilon$'s cancel in the ratio).

::: {.example name="Mutual information of jointly Gaussian variables"}
If $(\mathbf X, \mathbf Y)$ is jointly Gaussian with marginal covariances $\Sigma_X, \Sigma_Y$ and joint covariance $\Sigma_{XY}$, then
$$i[\mathbf X : \mathbf Y] = h[\mathbf X] + h[\mathbf Y] - h[\mathbf X, \mathbf Y] = \tfrac12\log\frac{\det\Sigma_X\,\det\Sigma_Y}{\det\Sigma_{XY}}.$$
For two scalars with variances $a, b$ and correlation coefficient $c$,
$$\Sigma_{XY} = \begin{pmatrix} a & c\sqrt{ab} \\ c\sqrt{ab} & b\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det\Sigma_{XY} = ab(1 - c^2),$$
so
$$i[X : Y] = -\tfrac12\log(1 - c^2).$$
It vanishes for $c = 0$ (for Gaussians, uncorrelated = independent) and diverges as $|c| \to 1$ (one variable determines the other exactly, and a real number carries infinite information). For $c = 0.5$, $i \approx 0.21$ bits; for $c = 0.9$, $i \approx 1.2$ bits.
:::

## Properties of differential entropy

::: {.proposition name="Properties of differential entropy"}
1. $h[X]$ can be negative.
2. Translation: $h[X + c] = h[X]$. Scaling: $h[aX] = h[X] + \log|a|$; for vectors, $h[A\mathbf X] = h[\mathbf X] + \log|\det A|$.
3. More generally, for an invertible smooth $\mathbf Y = g(\mathbf X)$, $h[\mathbf Y] = h[\mathbf X] + \mathbb E\bigl[\log|\det J_g(\mathbf X)|\bigr]$ — **$h$ depends on the coordinates**.
4. Chain rule: $h[X, Y] = h[X] + h[Y \mid X]$.
5. Conditioning reduces entropy: $h[Y \mid X] \leq h[Y]$; sub-additivity $h[X,Y] \leq h[X] + h[Y]$, with equality iff independent.
:::

The scaling rule is just the discretisation formula in disguise: stretching $X$ by $a$ is equivalent to measuring it with resolution $\epsilon/a$.

::: {.proposition name="Invariance of mutual information"}
For diffeomorphisms $\phi, \psi$ acting separately on $X$ and $Y$,
$$i[\phi(X) : \psi(Y)] = i[X : Y].$$
The Jacobian terms cancel between $h[X] + h[Y]$ and $h[X, Y]$. This makes $i$ a *reparameterisation-invariant* measure of dependence.
:::

## Maximum-entropy distributions

Discrete entropies are maximised by the uniform distribution. Continuous ones need a constraint (otherwise $h$ is unbounded), and the maximiser depends on the constraint.

::: {.theorem name="The Gaussian maximises entropy at fixed variance"}
Among all real random variables with variance $\sigma^2$, the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy, $h = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$. In $\mathbb R^k$, among all vectors with covariance $\Sigma$, $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$ is the maximiser.
:::

::: {.proof}
Let $f$ have mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and $\phi$ be the Gaussian with the same moments. Since $\log \phi(x)$ is a quadratic polynomial in $x$, $\int f\log\phi = \int\phi\log\phi$ (only the first two moments enter). Then
$$0 \leq D(f \| \phi) = \int f\log f - \int f\log\phi = -h(f) + h(\phi).$$
:::

::: {.corollary name="Other max-ent distributions"}
- Support $[a, b]$, no other constraint: uniform, $h = \log(b - a)$.
- Support $[0, \infty)$, fixed mean $\mu$: exponential, $h = \log(e\mu)$.
- In general, fixing $\mathbb E[T_j(X)]$ gives an exponential family $f(x) \propto \exp(\sum_j \lambda_j T_j(x))$ — the continuous analogue of the Boltzmann distribution.
:::

## The Gaussian channel

The noisy-channel framework extends to continuous inputs and outputs. Let $Z$ be the input and $Y$ the output.

::: {.definition name="Additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) channel"}
$$Y = Z + \eta, \qquad \eta \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2), \qquad f_{Y|Z}(y \mid z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-\frac{(y - z)^2}{2\sigma^2}} .$$
The noise is *additive*, and *white* if independent across uses. The input must be constrained — otherwise a single real number could carry infinite information. The standard constraint is a power (variance) constraint $\operatorname{Var}[Z] \leq v^2$.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Gaussian channel capacity"}
Under $\operatorname{Var}[Z] \leq v^2$, the capacity is achieved by a Gaussian input $Z \sim \mathcal N(\xi, v^2)$ and equals
$$C = \frac12 \log\Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr) = \frac12\log(1 + \text{SNR}), \qquad \text{SNR} = \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}.$$
:::

::: {.proof name="Proof via maximum entropy"}
$I[Z:Y] = h[Y] - h[Y \mid Z] = h[Y] - h[\eta]$, since given $Z = z$, $Y$ is just $\eta$ shifted by $z$. Since $Z$ and $\eta$ are independent, $\operatorname{Var}[Y] = \operatorname{Var}[Z] + \sigma^2 \leq v^2 + \sigma^2$, so by the max-ent theorem $h[Y] \leq \tfrac12\log\bigl(2\pi e(v^2 + \sigma^2)\bigr)$, with equality iff $Y$ is Gaussian — which happens iff $Z$ is Gaussian (sum of independent variables is Gaussian only if both are). Hence
$$C = \tfrac12\log\bigl(2\pi e(v^2 + \sigma^2)\bigr) - \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2) = \tfrac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr).$$
Equivalently, using the Gaussian MI formula with $\Sigma_{ZY} = \begin{pmatrix} v^2 & v^2 \\ v^2 & v^2 + \sigma^2\end{pmatrix}$: $i = \tfrac12\log\frac{v^2(v^2 + \sigma^2)}{v^2(v^2 + \sigma^2) - v^4}$.
:::

::: {.proof name="Proof via the calculus of variations"}
Maximise $I[Z:Y]$ over $f_Z$ with Lagrange multipliers for normalisation and variance:
$$\mathcal F[f_Z] = \int dz\,f_Z(z)\Bigl[\int dy\,f(y \mid z)\log\frac{f(y \mid z)}{f_Y(y)} - \lambda - \mu z^2\Bigr], \qquad f_Y(y) = \int dz\,f_Z(z) f(y\mid z).$$
Setting $\delta\mathcal F/\delta f_Z(z^*) = 0$, and noting that the variation of $f_Y$ inside the logarithm contributes $-\int dy\,f(y\mid z^*) = -1$ (absorbed into $\lambda$), gives
$$\int dy\,f(y\mid z^*)\log f(y \mid z^*) - \int dy\,f(y\mid z^*)\log f_Y(y) = \lambda' + \mu z^{*2}.$$
The first term is $-h[\eta]$, a constant. So the Gaussian smoothing of $\log f_Y$ must be a quadratic function of $z^*$, which forces $\log f_Y(y)$ to be quadratic in $y$: $f_Y \propto e^{a + cy^2}$, i.e. $Y$ is Gaussian, hence so is $Z$.
:::

### Decoding a Gaussian channel

With prior $Z \sim \mathcal N(0, v^2)$ (take $\xi = 0$) and likelihood $Y \mid Z \sim \mathcal N(Z, \sigma^2)$, Bayes' theorem gives a Gaussian posterior:
$$Z \mid Y = y \;\sim\; \mathcal N\Bigl(\frac{v^2}{v^2 + \sigma^2}\,y,\ \Bigl(\frac1{v^2} + \frac1{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigr).$$
Precisions add; the posterior mean is a **shrinkage** of the observation towards the prior mean. The MAP and posterior-mean estimators coincide (the posterior is symmetric and unimodal):
$$z' = D(y) = \frac{v^2}{v^2 + \sigma^2}\,y .$$
The mean squared error of this decoder is the posterior variance,
$$\mathbb E[(z' - Z)^2] = \frac{v^2\sigma^2}{v^2 + \sigma^2} = \sigma^2\,\frac{\text{SNR}}{1 + \text{SNR}},$$
which is less than both the prior variance $v^2$ (ignoring the data) and the noise variance $\sigma^2$ (trusting the data blindly).

::: {.remark name="Two limits"}
If $\text{SNR} \to 0$ the posterior reduces to the prior $\mathcal N(0, v^2)$: the data are useless. If $\text{SNR} \to \infty$ the posterior reduces to the likelihood $\mathcal N(y, \sigma^2)$, centred on the observation. Bayesian inference interpolates between "believe the prior" and "believe the data" according to their relative precisions.
:::

### Continuous-time signals and bandwidth

A Gaussian channel arises naturally when a time-continuous signal $z(t)$, $t \in [0, T]$, is sent through a medium adding white noise. Encode $N$ numbers $z_k$ as coefficients of $N$ orthonormal functions $\phi_k$:
$$z(t) = \sum_{k=1}^N z_k\,\phi_k(t), \qquad z_k = \int_0^T \phi_k(t)\,z(t)\,dt,$$
e.g. sines and cosines of frequencies up to $W$, with $N = 2WT$ (the Nyquist count). A power constraint $\frac1T\int_0^T z(t)^2\,dt \leq P$ becomes $\sum_k z_k^2 \leq TP$, i.e. $\langle z_k^2\rangle \leq TP/N = P/(2W)$.

The receiver gets $y(t) = z(t) + \eta(t)$, so $y_k = z_k + \eta_k$ with $\eta_k = \int_0^T \phi_k(t)\eta(t)\,dt$. For white noise, $\mathbb E[\eta(t)] = 0$ and $\mathbb E[\eta(t)\eta(t')] = \sigma^2\delta(t - t')$:
$$\mathbb E[\eta_k] = 0, \qquad \mathbb E[\eta_k\eta_l] = \sigma^2\!\int_0^T\!\!\int_0^T dt\,dt'\,\phi_k(t)\phi_l(t')\delta(t - t') = \sigma^2\!\int_0^T \phi_k\phi_l\,dt = \sigma^2\delta_{kl}.$$
The noise components are independent $\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$: **sending the signal is equivalent to $N = 2WT$ uses of a Gaussian channel.** Writing the (two-sided) noise spectral density as $\sigma^2 = N_0/2$, the capacity per second is
$$C = \frac{N}{T}\cdot\frac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{P/(2W)}{N_0/2}\Bigr) = W\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{P}{N_0 W}\Bigr) \ \text{bits/s},$$
the **Shannon–Hartley formula**, which governs everything from modems to Wi-Fi.

::: {.example name="Telephone line"}
A telephone line with $W = 3$ kHz and SNR $= 30$ dB ($P/(N_0W) = 1000$) has capacity $C = 3000\log_2 1001 \approx 30$ kbit/s — the ceiling that analogue modems approached in the 1990s.
:::

### Sphere packing: the coding theorem for the Gaussian channel

The noisy-channel coding theorem has a beautiful geometric picture in the Gaussian case. Consider $n$ uses of the channel.

- The noise vector $\boldsymbol\eta \in \mathbb R^n$ has $\sum_i\eta_i^2 \approx n\sigma^2$ by the law of large numbers: it lies, with overwhelming probability, close to the **sphere of radius $\sqrt{n\sigma^2}$**.
- Given the codeword $\mathbf z$, the output $\mathbf y = \mathbf z + \boldsymbol\eta$ lies near a sphere of radius $\sqrt{n\sigma^2}$ centred on $\mathbf z$.
- The inputs have power $v^2$, so all outputs lie within a sphere of radius $\sqrt{n(v^2 + \sigma^2)}$.

For codewords to be non-confusable, their noise spheres must not overlap. The number of codewords is at most the ratio of volumes:
$$|S| \leq \Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{n(v^2 + \sigma^2)}}{\sqrt{n\sigma^2}}\Bigr)^n = \Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{n/2}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\log|S|}{n} \leq \frac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{v^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr) = C .$$
Random coding shows that this packing bound is (asymptotically) achievable.

## Parallel Gaussian channels and water-filling

For $K$ independent Gaussian channels with noise variances $\sigma_i^2$ and a total power budget $\sum_i P_i \leq P$:

::: {.theorem name="Water-filling"}
$$C = \max_{\sum P_i \leq P}\sum_{i=1}^{K} \tfrac12\log\Bigl(1 + \frac{P_i}{\sigma_i^2}\Bigr),$$
achieved by $P_i = \max(0, \mu - \sigma_i^2)$, with the "water level" $\mu$ fixed by $\sum_i P_i = P$.
:::

Picture pouring a volume $P$ of water over a vessel whose floor has heights $\sigma_i^2$: noisier channels (higher floor) get less power, and very noisy ones none at all. This is what OFDM, DSL and LTE do at the physical layer, allocating power across frequency sub-carriers.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Compute the differential entropy of a Laplace distribution $f(x) = (2b)^{-1}e^{-|x - \mu|/b}$ and compare it with a Gaussian of the same variance.
:::

::: {.solution}
$h = -\int f\log f = \log(2b) + \log e\,\mathbb E|X - \mu|/b = \log(2b) + \log e = \log(2be)$. The variance is $2b^2$; the Gaussian with that variance has $h = \tfrac12\log(2\pi e\cdot 2b^2) = \tfrac12\log(4\pi e b^2)$, while Laplace has $\tfrac12\log(4e^2b^2)$. The difference is $\tfrac12\log(\pi/e) \approx 0.104$ bits in favour of the Gaussian, as the max-ent theorem requires.
:::

::: {.exercise}
A variable $X \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ is measured with resolution $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$. How many bits does a measurement carry? How many if the unit of measurement is changed so that $\sigma = 1000$?
:::

::: {.solution}
$H[X_\epsilon] \approx \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e) + \log_2 10^3 \approx 2.05 + 9.97 = 12.0$ bits. Changing units so that $\sigma = 1000$ (and keeping $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ in the new units) adds $\log_2 1000 \approx 9.97$ bits: $h$ grows by $\log|a|$ and the resolution is effectively finer. Only the ratio $\sigma/\epsilon$ matters.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Two jointly Gaussian variables have correlation $c = 0.8$. Compute $i[X:Y]$. What is $i[X^3 : e^Y]$?
:::

::: {.solution}
$i = -\tfrac12\log_2(1 - 0.64) = -\tfrac12\log_2 0.36 \approx 0.737$ bits. Since $x \mapsto x^3$ and $y \mapsto e^y$ are diffeomorphisms of their domains, $i[X^3 : e^Y] = 0.737$ bits as well — even though the transformed variables are no longer Gaussian and their *correlation coefficient* is different.
:::

::: {.exercise}
For a Gaussian channel with SNR = 10 dB, compute the capacity in bits per channel use, and the MSE of the optimal decoder relative to $\sigma^2$.
:::

::: {.solution}
SNR $= 10$, so $C = \tfrac12\log_2 11 \approx 1.73$ bits per use (or $\log_2 11 \approx 3.46$ bits/s/Hz with $N = 2WT$ uses). MSE $= \sigma^2\cdot 10/11 \approx 0.91\,\sigma^2$.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
Differential entropy is *coordinate-dependent*: $h(g(X)) = h(X) + \mathbb E\log|g'(X)|$. Only $i[X:Y]$ and $D_{KL}$ are coordinate-free, because the divergent $\log(1/\epsilon)$ terms cancel in them.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
When you need the entropy of a continuous variable, first check whether it belongs to a maximum-entropy family (Gaussian, exponential, uniform), which admits closed forms. Otherwise quantise and use the discrete formula, remembering the $\log(1/\epsilon)$ offset.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
Negative differential entropy is not a mistake: it means the distribution is more concentrated than a uniform on a unit cell. Any finite-resolution discrete entropy is still positive.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
The Gaussian capacity $\tfrac12\log(1 + \text{SNR})$ per use, or $W\log(1 + P/N_0W)$ per second, is *the* benchmark of digital communication.
:::
