12  Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Random-walk Metropolis (Chapter 7) mixes slowly in high dimensions. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) uses ideas from classical mechanics to propose distant, high-acceptance moves. This chapter mirrors Prof. Allegra’s Chapter 13.

12.1 11.1 Hamiltonian dynamics

Augment the state \(\mathbf q \in \mathbb R^d\) with an auxiliary momentum \(\mathbf p \in \mathbb R^d\), and define the Hamiltonian \[H(\mathbf q, \mathbf p) = U(\mathbf q) + K(\mathbf p) = -\log \pi(\mathbf q) + \tfrac12 \mathbf p^\top M^{-1}\mathbf p,\] where \(\pi\) is the target distribution, \(U = -\log\pi\) is the potential energy, and \(K\) is a Gaussian kinetic energy with mass matrix \(M\).

The Hamiltonian dynamics \[\dot{\mathbf q} = \partial_{\mathbf p} H = M^{-1}\mathbf p, \qquad \dot{\mathbf p} = -\partial_{\mathbf q} H = -\nabla U(\mathbf q)\] preserve the joint density \(e^{-H(\mathbf q, \mathbf p)}\) (Liouville’s theorem) and conserve energy (\(dH/dt = 0\)).

12.2 11.2 Numerical integration: leapfrog

The continuous Hamiltonian flow cannot be integrated exactly. The leapfrog scheme is a symplectic integrator that preserves the volume of phase space and conserves \(H\) up to \(O(\Delta t^2)\) error:

Given step size \(\varepsilon\):

  1. \(\mathbf p \leftarrow \mathbf p - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(\mathbf q)\) (half-step in momentum).
  2. \(\mathbf q \leftarrow \mathbf q + \varepsilon M^{-1}\mathbf p\) (full step in position).
  3. \(\mathbf p \leftarrow \mathbf p - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(\mathbf q)\) (half-step in momentum).

Composition of \(L\) leapfrog steps gives a proposal \((\mathbf q, \mathbf p) \to (\mathbf q', \mathbf p')\) with symplectic Jacobian equal to 1.

12.3 11.3 The HMC algorithm

Given current \(\mathbf q\):

  1. Sample momentum \(\mathbf p \sim \mathcal N(0, M)\).
  2. Run \(L\) leapfrog steps of size \(\varepsilon\) starting from \((\mathbf q, \mathbf p)\), giving \((\mathbf q', \mathbf p')\).
  3. Accept the proposal \(\mathbf q'\) with probability \[A = \min\bigl(1,\ \exp(H(\mathbf q, \mathbf p) - H(\mathbf q', \mathbf p'))\bigr) .\] Otherwise return \(\mathbf q\).

The acceptance probability depends only on the (small) integration error of the leapfrog scheme, not on the distance travelled. This is why HMC can propose moves of \(O(1)\) magnitude in high dimension while keeping acceptance \(\sim 0.7\).

Detailed balance holds: the HMC kernel leaves the joint distribution \(\pi(\mathbf q) \cdot \mathcal N(\mathbf p; 0, M)\) invariant. Consequently the marginal on \(\mathbf q\) is \(\pi\).

12.4 11.4 Advantages of HMC

The essential reason HMC dominates in modern Bayesian inference is its dimension scaling:

For a target with sufficient regularity, the optimal number of leapfrog steps scales as \(L \sim d^{1/4}\) and the optimal step size as \(\varepsilon \sim d^{-1/4}\), giving an effective sample size per gradient evaluation of \(O(d^{-1/4})\). Random-walk Metropolis, by contrast, has ESS/eval \(\sim O(d^{-1})\).

The one-order-of-magnitude improvement in scaling is what made HMC the default sampler for continuous parameters.

Remark (NUTS). The No-U-Turn Sampler (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014) automates HMC by dynamically choosing \(L\) so the trajectory stops just before it starts curving back. NUTS is the algorithm underlying Stan, PyMC, and TensorFlow Probability.

12.5 11.5 Practical considerations

12.5.1 Mass matrix

The mass matrix \(M\) acts as a preconditioner. For Gaussian-like targets, \(M = \text{diag}(\text{Var}(q_j)^{-1})\) or \(M = \Sigma_{\pi}^{-1}\) (the inverse target covariance) makes the Hamiltonian isotropic and improves mixing dramatically. Modern implementations estimate \(M\) from an initial warm-up phase.

12.5.2 Step size adaptation

If \(\varepsilon\) is too large the leapfrog error blows up and acceptance goes to zero; too small and \(L\) blows up. Dual averaging adapts \(\varepsilon\) during warm-up to hit a target acceptance \(\sim 0.8\).

12.5.3 Non-continuous targets

HMC requires a differentiable log-density. For discrete parameters or heavy-tailed distributions, HMC fails; Gibbs sampling or ensemble MCMC methods remain the tools of choice.

12.6 Exercises

For a Gaussian target \(\pi(\mathbf q) = \mathcal N(0, \Sigma)\) show that Hamiltonian dynamics with \(M = \Sigma^{-1}\) decouples into independent harmonic oscillators along the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\).

Solution. The Hamiltonian is \(H = \tfrac12 \mathbf q^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf q + \tfrac12 \mathbf p^\top \Sigma\,\mathbf p\). Diagonalise \(\Sigma^{-1} = O \Lambda O^\top\) and transform \(\tilde{\mathbf q} = O^\top \mathbf q\), \(\tilde{\mathbf p} = O^\top \mathbf p\). Then \(H = \tfrac12 \tilde{\mathbf q}^\top \Lambda\,\tilde{\mathbf q} + \tfrac12 \tilde{\mathbf p}^\top \Lambda^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf p}\), a sum of independent harmonic oscillators. Each mode is analytically integrable — HMC is exact in this special case.

Show that the leapfrog integrator is reversible: swapping the sign of momentum and integrating backwards returns to the starting point.

Solution. Direct verification of the three-step definition: applying it with \(\varepsilon \to -\varepsilon\) inverts each step, and by symmetry of the composition the whole trajectory reverses exactly.

12.7 Callouts

HMC is the modern MCMC sampler for continuous parameters. If your model is smooth and differentiable, use HMC/NUTS; use random-walk Metropolis only for discrete parameters or as a baseline.

When HMC has trouble mixing (low ESS, divergent transitions), reparameterise the model to make its geometry more isotropic. Non-centred parameterisations of hierarchical models are the canonical example.

The energy conservation of leapfrog holds up to \(O(\varepsilon^2)\) per step but without drift: energy oscillates but does not systematically increase. This is what makes long trajectories possible without a Metropolis correction blowing up.

HMC requires evaluating \(\nabla \log \pi(\mathbf q)\) at every leapfrog step. If your log-density is expensive (e.g. an ODE solve inside the likelihood), HMC can be prohibitively slow.
