12  Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

We now have several ways to obtain posterior estimates. Gibbs sampling and variational Bayes are very efficient, but both need strong analytical control: closed-form conditionals, typically requiring simple likelihoods and conjugate priors. When these are unavailable we can always fall back on Metropolis–Hastings, but in high dimension its random-walk proposals must be tiny to be accepted (Chapter 7), and the chain diffuses slowly. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) overcomes this by borrowing classical mechanics: it uses the gradient of the log-posterior to build distant proposals that are nevertheless accepted with probability close to 1. Like Metropolis it needs the unnormalised posterior (likelihood × prior), and in addition its derivatives with respect to the parameters.

12.1 Hamiltonian dynamics

Consider coordinates \(\mathbf x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) and conjugate momenta \(\mathbf p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n)\), with Hamiltonian \[H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \sum_i\frac{p_i^2}{2m_i} + U(\mathbf x),\] the sum of kinetic and potential energy. Hamilton’s equations \[\dot x_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = \frac{p_i}{m_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i}\] define a flow \(\phi_t : \mathbb R^{2n} \to \mathbb R^{2n}\), \((\mathbf x_0, \mathbf p_0) \mapsto (\mathbf x_t, \mathbf p_t)\). Three properties of this flow make it useful for Monte Carlo.

12.1.1 Energy conservation

\[\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}\dot x_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\Bigr) = \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}\Bigr) = 0 .\]

12.1.2 Volume preservation (Liouville’s theorem)

For every region \(A \subset \mathbb R^{2n}\), \(\operatorname{Vol}(\phi_t(A)) = \operatorname{Vol}(A)\); equivalently, the Jacobian determinant of the flow is \(|J_{\phi_t}| = 1\) everywhere.

Proof. Changing variables \((\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p)\), \(\int_{\phi_t(A)}d\mathbf x'\,d\mathbf p' = \int_A |J_{\phi_t}|\,d\mathbf x\,d\mathbf p\), so volume preservation for all \(A\) is equivalent to \(|J_{\phi_t}| = 1\). For an infinitesimal time step, \[\phi_{\delta t}(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf x\\ \mathbf p\end{pmatrix} + \delta t\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\mathbf p}H \\ -\partial_{\mathbf x}H\end{pmatrix} + O(\delta t^2), \qquad J_{\phi_{\delta t}} = \begin{pmatrix}1 + \delta t\,\partial_{\mathbf x}\partial_{\mathbf p}H & \delta t\,\partial^2_{\mathbf p}H \\ -\delta t\,\partial^2_{\mathbf x}H & 1 - \delta t\,\partial_{\mathbf p}\partial_{\mathbf x}H\end{pmatrix} + O(\delta t^2).\] The determinant is \(1 + \delta t\,(\partial_{\mathbf x}\partial_{\mathbf p}H - \partial_{\mathbf p}\partial_{\mathbf x}H) + O(\delta t^2) = 1 + O(\delta t^2)\): the trace of the first-order term vanishes because mixed partial derivatives commute. For finite \(t = n\,\delta t\), \(\phi_t\) is the \(n\)-fold composition, so \(|J_{\phi_t}| = (1 + O(\delta t^2))^n = 1 + O(n\,\delta t^2) = 1 + O(t^2/n) \to 1\) as \(n \to \infty\).

12.1.3 Time reversibility

The flow is invertible, and its inverse is obtained by running time backwards: \(\phi_t^{-1} = \phi_{-t}\), i.e. integrating \(\dot x_i = -p_i/m_i\), \(\dot p_i = +\partial_{x_i}U\). These are the original equations with \(\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p\). Introducing the momentum flip \(N_p(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = (\mathbf x, -\mathbf p)\), \[\phi_t^{-1} = \phi_{-t} = N_p\circ\phi_t\circ N_p .\] To run a trajectory backwards: flip the momentum, run forwards, flip again. For every forward trajectory \((\mathbf x, \mathbf p) \to (\mathbf x', \mathbf p')\) there is a backward one \((\mathbf x', -\mathbf p') \to (\mathbf x, -\mathbf p)\).

12.2 Numerical integration of Hamilton’s equations

In practice the flow must be approximated numerically, and how matters: we need an integrator that retains volume preservation and reversibility.

12.2.1 Euler’s method

\[p_i(t + \epsilon) = p_i(t) - \epsilon\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)), \qquad x_i(t + \epsilon) = x_i(t) + \epsilon\,p_i(t)/m_i .\] Its Jacobian determinant is \(\det\begin{pmatrix}1 & \epsilon/m \\ -\epsilon\,\partial^2_xU & 1\end{pmatrix} = 1 + \epsilon^2\partial_x^2U/m \neq 1\): Euler does not preserve volume. For the harmonic oscillator (\(m = 1\), \(U = x^2/2\)) each step multiplies the phase-space area by \(1 + \epsilon^2\) and the radius by \(\sqrt{1 + \epsilon^2}\): the exact circular orbit becomes an outward spiral. With \(\epsilon = 0.3\), twenty steps already inflate the radius by \((1.09)^{10} \approx 2.4\). Smaller steps (\(\epsilon = 0.01\)) delay the divergence, but over many steps (thousands) the trajectory still spirals away.

12.2.2 Modified (symplectic) Euler

Update the momentum first, then use the new momentum to update the position: \[p_i(t + \epsilon) = p_i(t) - \epsilon\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)), \qquad x_i(t + \epsilon) = x_i(t) + \epsilon\,p_i(t + \epsilon)/m_i .\] This is the composition of a map that changes only momenta and a map that changes only positions, with Jacobians \[J^{(p)} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -\epsilon\,\partial^2_{\mathbf x}U & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J^{(x)} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \epsilon\,\operatorname{diag}(m^{-1}) \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},\] both triangular with unit diagonal, hence determinant 1. The modified Euler method preserves volume exactly, and trajectories no longer diverge. But it is not reversible: flipping the momentum, running it, and flipping again does not retrace the trajectory, because the order “momentum then position” is not symmetric in time.

12.2.3 Leapfrog

The fix is to make the scheme symmetric: half a step in momentum, a full step in position, another half step in momentum.

\[\begin{aligned} p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2) &= p_i(t) - \tfrac\epsilon2\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)), \\ x_i(t + \epsilon) &= x_i(t) + \epsilon\,p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2)/m_i, \\ p_i(t + \epsilon) &= p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2) - \tfrac\epsilon2\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t + \epsilon)). \end{aligned}\]

The leapfrog map \(\phi^{\text{lf}}_\epsilon\) is

  1. volume-preserving: it is a composition of three shear maps (momentum, position, momentum), each with unit Jacobian determinant;
  2. time-reversible: \((\phi^{\text{lf}}_\epsilon)^{-1} = \phi^{\text{lf}}_{-\epsilon} = N_p\circ\phi^{\text{lf}}_\epsilon\circ N_p\);
  3. second-order accurate: the energy error is \(O(\epsilon^2)\) and, for stable step sizes, does not drift over long trajectories.

Proof (Proof of reversibility). Apply \(\phi^{\text{lf}}_{-\epsilon}\) to the endpoint \((\mathbf x(t + \epsilon), \mathbf p(t + \epsilon))\): \[\begin{aligned} p_i' &= p_i(t + \epsilon) + \tfrac\epsilon2\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t + \epsilon)) = p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2), \\ x_i' &= x_i(t + \epsilon) - \epsilon\,p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2)/m_i = x_i(t), \\ p_i'' &= p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2) + \tfrac\epsilon2\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)) = p_i(t), \end{aligned}\] recovering the starting point exactly (each line undoes the corresponding line of the forward step, in reverse order). And running \(\phi_{-\epsilon}\) is the same as flipping \(\mathbf p\), running \(\phi_{+\epsilon}\), and flipping again.

Take \(m = 1\), \(U(x) = x^2/(2\sigma^2)\) (a Gaussian target of width \(\sigma\)). One leapfrog step is the linear map \[\begin{pmatrix}x(t + \epsilon)\\ p(t + \epsilon)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2} & \epsilon \\[3pt] -\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\epsilon^3}{4\sigma^4} & 1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\ p(t)\end{pmatrix},\] with determinant \(\bigl(1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\bigr)^2 + \frac{\epsilon^2}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\epsilon^4}{4\sigma^4} = 1\) (volume preservation). With \(a = 1 - \epsilon^2/(2\sigma^2)\) the eigenvalues are \(\lambda_\pm = a \pm\sqrt{a^2 - 1}\), with \(\lambda_+\lambda_- = 1\).

  • If \(\epsilon < 2\sigma\), then \(|a| < 1\), the eigenvalues are complex conjugates with \(|\lambda_\pm| = 1\), and the trajectory is a stable (slightly distorted) ellipse.
  • If \(\epsilon > 2\sigma\), the eigenvalues are real with one of modulus \(> 1\): the trajectory diverges exponentially.

Rule of thumb: the step size must be smaller than the narrowest width of the target, \(\epsilon \lesssim \sigma_{\min}\).

12.3 The HMC algorithm

12.3.1 The augmented distribution

To sample \(\pi(\mathbf x)\), \(\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n\), add \(n\) auxiliary momenta, independent of \(\mathbf x\) and of each other, \(p_i \sim \mathcal N(0, m_i)\): \[\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \pi(\mathbf x)\cdot\prod_i(2\pi m_i)^{-1/2}e^{-p_i^2/2m_i} .\] If we can sample \(\Pi\), the \(\mathbf x\)-components of the samples are distributed according to \(\pi\) (just discard \(\mathbf p\)). Define the potential \(U(\mathbf x) = -\log\pi(\mathbf x)\); then \[\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \frac{1}{Z}e^{-H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p)}, \qquad H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = U(\mathbf x) + \sum_i\frac{p_i^2}{2m_i},\] a Boltzmann distribution at unit temperature, with the momentum variances playing the role of masses. Below we take \(m_i = 1\), so \(H = U(\mathbf x) + \tfrac12|\mathbf p|^2\).

For Bayesian inference, \(\pi(\mathbf x) = f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf x)/f(\mathbf y)\); since the evidence does not depend on \(\mathbf x\) it is absorbed into \(Z\), and \[U(\mathbf x) = -\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf x) - \log f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf x).\]

12.3.2 The proposal

Each HMC iteration is a composition of two Metropolis–Hastings moves on \(\Pi\):

  1. Momentum refresh: draw \(\mathbf p_r \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\), independently of the current \(\mathbf p\).
  2. Hamiltonian move: evolve \((\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\) under the Hamiltonian flow for a time \(t\) and flip the momentum: \((\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = N_p\circ\phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\).

With the exact Hamiltonian flow, both moves are accepted with probability 1.

Proof. Move 1 is a Gibbs update: it samples \(\mathbf p\) from its exact conditional \(\Pi(\mathbf p\mid\mathbf x) = \mathcal N(0, I)\). Explicitly, the proposal is \(T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \delta(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x)(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|\mathbf p_r|^2/2}\) and the Hastings ratio is \[\frac{\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\,T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)}{\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p)\,T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p)} = \frac{\pi(\mathbf x)e^{-|\mathbf p_r|^2/2}\cdot e^{-|\mathbf p|^2/2}}{\pi(\mathbf x)e^{-|\mathbf p|^2/2}\cdot e^{-|\mathbf p_r|^2/2}} = 1 .\]

Move 2 is deterministic: \(T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p'\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r) = \delta\bigl((\mathbf x', -\mathbf p') - \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\bigr)\). Energy conservation gives \(\Pi(\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = \Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\) (the flip does not change \(|\mathbf p|^2\)), so the acceptance probability reduces to \(\min\{1, T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x', \mathbf p')/T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p'\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\}\). For the reverse move, \[T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = \delta\bigl((\mathbf x, -\mathbf p_r) - \phi_t(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')\bigr) = \delta\bigl((\mathbf x', \mathbf p') - \phi_t^{-1}(\mathbf x, -\mathbf p_r)\bigr)\,|J_{\phi_t}|^{-1},\] and \(|J_{\phi_t}| = 1\) by volume preservation. By reversibility \(\phi_t^{-1}\circ N_p = N_p\circ\phi_t\), so \(\phi_t^{-1}(\mathbf x, -\mathbf p_r) = N_p\phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\) and the delta function equals \(\delta\bigl((\mathbf x', -\mathbf p') - \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\bigr) = T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p'\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\). The ratio is 1.

The proof shows exactly which properties are needed: volume preservation (no Jacobian factor), reversibility with the momentum flip (the reverse proposal exists and has the same density), and energy conservation (\(\Pi\) is unchanged). The momentum flip is required for the proof; in practice it can be skipped, because the momentum is immediately resampled at the next iteration.

12.3.3 With a numerical integrator

The exact flow is replaced by \(L = t/\epsilon\) leapfrog steps. Leapfrog is exactly volume-preserving and reversible, so the proposal densities still cancel; but energy is conserved only approximately, \(\Pi(\mathbf x', \mathbf p') \approx \Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\). The Metropolis correction takes care of this:

From the current state \(\mathbf x\):

  1. draw \(\mathbf p_r \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\);
  2. apply \(L\) leapfrog steps of size \(\epsilon\) to \((\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\), obtaining \((\mathbf x', \mathbf p')\) (and flip \(\mathbf p'\));
  3. accept \(\mathbf x'\) with probability \[\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\Pi(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')}{\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)}\Bigr\} = \min\bigl\{1, e^{H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r) - H(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')}\bigr\};\] otherwise keep \(\mathbf x\).

The acceptance probability depends only on the integration error, not on the distance travelled. With a stable step size the energy error stays small along arbitrarily long trajectories, so HMC can make large moves with high acceptance. The price is one gradient evaluation \(\nabla U(\mathbf x) = -\nabla\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x) - \nabla\log f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf x)\) per leapfrog step.

Remark (HMC as gradient descent with momentum). The update \(\mathbf p \leftarrow \mathbf p - \epsilon\nabla U\), \(\mathbf x \leftarrow \mathbf x + \epsilon\mathbf p\) is “heavy-ball” gradient descent on \(U = -\log\pi\): the direction of motion is not just the current gradient but has inertia. HMC balances exploration (the random momentum draw, which sets the direction and energy of each trajectory) and exploitation (the deterministic flow, which follows the geometry of the posterior). Unlike gradient descent, energy conservation prevents the trajectory from simply sliding to the mode: it converts potential into kinetic energy and back, travelling along the level sets of the posterior.

12.4 Advantages of HMC

The key advantage of HMC is that it replaces the random walk of Metropolis by directed motion. A random walk with step \(\epsilon\) moves a distance \(\sim\epsilon\sqrt k\) in \(k\) steps; a Hamiltonian trajectory moves a distance \(\sim\epsilon k\).

Sample \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Sigma)\) with \[\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.98 \\ 0.98 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{eigenvalues } 1.98 \text{ and } 0.02 .\] The standard deviations along the principal axes are \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{1.98} \approx 1.41\) and \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt{0.02} \approx 0.14\). Both samplers must use steps of size \(\approx\sigma_2\) to stay stable/accepted: HMC with \(\epsilon = \sigma_2\) and \(L = 20\) leapfrog steps per iteration; Metropolis with Gaussian jumps of size \(\sigma_2\).

To reach an effectively independent point we must cross the long axis, a distance \(\approx\sigma_1 \approx 10\,\sigma_2\). HMC’s momentum along the long direction is stable, so about 10 leapfrog steps suffice. Metropolis cannot move consistently in one direction: after \(k\) steps it has moved \(\sim\sigma_2\sqrt k\), so it needs \(\sim 100\) steps.

With the same budget — 20 HMC iterations (\(400\) leapfrog steps) versus \(400\) Metropolis steps — the estimated covariances are (Allegra, Fig. 13.2) \[\hat\Sigma_{\text{HMC}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.96 & 0.90 \\ 0.90 & 0.88\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat\Sigma_{\text{Met}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.26 & 0.26 \\ 0.26 & 0.30\end{pmatrix}.\] HMC is already close to the truth; Metropolis has explored only a small part of the long axis and badly underestimates the variances.

Take \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(0.01^2, 0.02^2, \ldots, 1.00^2)\): 100 independent coordinates with widths from \(0.01\) to \(1\). Both step sizes must be of the order of the smallest width: HMC uses \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal U[0.0104, 0.0156]\) with \(L = 150\) leapfrog steps and \(1000\) iterations; Metropolis uses jumps \(\sim\mathcal U[0.0176, 0.0264]\) and \(150{,}000\) steps — the same number of gradient/likelihood evaluations. (Randomising \(\epsilon\) avoids accidental periodicities in which a trajectory returns exactly to its start.) HMC estimates the means and standard deviations of all coordinates well, including the widest ones; Metropolis gets the narrow coordinates right but grossly underestimates the spread of the wide ones, which it has barely explored.

For targets with \(d\) roughly independent components, keeping the acceptance rate fixed requires a step size \(\epsilon \propto d^{-1/2}\) for random-walk Metropolis but only \(\epsilon \propto d^{-1/4}\) for HMC. Reaching an effectively independent point (moving a distance of order the target’s width) therefore takes \(O(d)\) Metropolis steps but only \(O(d^{1/4})\) leapfrog steps. If each density or gradient evaluation costs \(O(d)\), the total cost per independent sample is \(O(d^2)\) for Metropolis versus \(O(d^{5/4})\) for HMC (Neal 2011, §5.4.4). The optimal HMC acceptance rate in this limit is about \(0.65\).

12.5 Practical considerations

  • Step size. Too large: leapfrog becomes unstable and acceptance collapses (“divergent transitions”). Too small: wasted gradient evaluations. Modern implementations tune \(\epsilon\) during warm-up to reach a target acceptance (\(\approx 0.65\)–\(0.8\)).
  • Trajectory length. Too short: HMC degenerates into a random walk. Too long: trajectories loop back (for a Gaussian, after half a period). The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS, Hoffman & Gelman 2014) chooses \(L\) adaptively, stopping when the trajectory starts to turn back; it underlies Stan and PyMC.
  • Mass matrix. Using \(\mathbf p \sim \mathcal N(0, M)\) with \(M \approx \Sigma_\pi^{-1}\) (estimated during warm-up) makes all directions equally “stiff”: it is equivalent to rescaling the target to unit covariance, which removes the \(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) penalty seen in the examples.
  • Limitations. HMC requires a differentiable log-density on a continuous space: discrete parameters need Gibbs steps (or marginalisation). Multimodality remains hard. Each step costs a gradient, which can be expensive (automatic differentiation helps).

12.6 Exercises

Show that the modified Euler map \(p \leftarrow p - \epsilon x\), \(x \leftarrow x + \epsilon p\) for the harmonic oscillator has unit Jacobian, and find the condition on \(\epsilon\) for stability.

Solution. The map is \(\begin{pmatrix}x'\\p'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \epsilon^2 & \epsilon \\ -\epsilon & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\p\end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(1 - \epsilon^2 + \epsilon^2 = 1\). Trace \(2 - \epsilon^2\): eigenvalues are complex with unit modulus iff \(|2 - \epsilon^2| < 2\), i.e. \(\epsilon < 2\) — the same stability limit as leapfrog with \(\sigma = 1\).

For a Gaussian target \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Sigma)\) show that with mass matrix \(M = \Sigma^{-1}\) the Hamiltonian dynamics reduce to independent oscillators all with the same frequency.

Solution. \(H = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x + \tfrac12\mathbf p^\top M^{-1}\mathbf p = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x + \tfrac12\mathbf p^\top\Sigma\mathbf p\). Then \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \Sigma\mathbf p\), \(\dot{\mathbf p} = -\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x\), so \(\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf x\): every direction oscillates with unit angular frequency. With \(M = I\) instead, the mode along eigenvector \(k\) has frequency \(1/\sigma_k\), and a single step size must resolve the fastest mode while the trajectory must be long enough for the slowest: the ratio \(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) is the cost of a poor mass matrix.

Write the HMC potential \(U\) and its gradient for the posterior of \(\mu\) in the model \(x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, 1)\), \(\mu \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\).

Solution. \(U(\mu) = \tfrac12\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \mu^2/(2\tau^2)\) + const, \(U'(\mu) = -\sum_i(x_i - \mu) + \mu/\tau^2 = (n + \tau^{-2})\mu - n\bar x\). The target is Gaussian with \(\sigma^2 = (n + \tau^{-2})^{-1}\), so stable leapfrog needs \(\epsilon < 2\sigma\).

Explain why the momentum flip is needed in the proof of detailed balance but can be omitted in practice.

Solution. Without the flip, the reverse of the proposal \((\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r) \to \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\) would require running the flow backwards, which is not the same proposal mechanism, so \(T(\text{reverse})\) would vanish and detailed balance would fail. With the flip, the proposal is an involution: applying it twice returns the start. In practice the momentum is discarded and redrawn from the symmetric \(\mathcal N(0, I)\) at the next iteration, so its sign never matters for the \(\mathbf x\)-chain.

12.7 Callouts

HMC is the default sampler for smooth posteriors over continuous parameters (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro). Use Gibbs for discrete latent variables and random-walk Metropolis only as a baseline or when gradients are unavailable.

Poor HMC mixing (low ESS, divergences) usually signals bad geometry. Reparameterise to make the posterior more isotropic, or adapt the mass matrix.

Leapfrog energy errors oscillate but do not drift for stable step sizes. This is why long trajectories remain acceptable — an integrator with energy drift (like Euler) would make acceptance decay with trajectory length.

We now have several ways to obtain posterior estimates. Gibbs sampling and variational Bayes are very efficient, but both need strong analytical control: closed-form conditionals, typically requiring simple likelihoods and conjugate priors. When these are unavailable we can always fall back on Metropolis–Hastings, but in high dimension its random-walk proposals must be tiny to be accepted (Chapter 7), and the chain diffuses slowly. **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (HMC) overcomes this by borrowing classical mechanics: it uses the gradient of the log-posterior to build *distant* proposals that are nevertheless accepted with probability close to 1. Like Metropolis it needs the unnormalised posterior (likelihood × prior), and in addition **its derivatives with respect to the parameters**.

## Hamiltonian dynamics

Consider coordinates $\mathbf x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ and conjugate momenta $\mathbf p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n)$, with Hamiltonian
$$H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \sum_i\frac{p_i^2}{2m_i} + U(\mathbf x),$$
the sum of kinetic and potential energy. Hamilton's equations
$$\dot x_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = \frac{p_i}{m_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i}$$
define a **flow** $\phi_t : \mathbb R^{2n} \to \mathbb R^{2n}$, $(\mathbf x_0, \mathbf p_0) \mapsto (\mathbf x_t, \mathbf p_t)$. Three properties of this flow make it useful for Monte Carlo.

### Energy conservation

$$\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}\dot x_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\Bigr) = \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i}\Bigr) = 0 .$$

### Volume preservation (Liouville's theorem)

::: {.proposition name="Volume preservation"}
For every region $A \subset \mathbb R^{2n}$, $\operatorname{Vol}(\phi_t(A)) = \operatorname{Vol}(A)$; equivalently, the Jacobian determinant of the flow is $|J_{\phi_t}| = 1$ everywhere.
:::

::: {.proof}
Changing variables $(\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p)$, $\int_{\phi_t(A)}d\mathbf x'\,d\mathbf p' = \int_A |J_{\phi_t}|\,d\mathbf x\,d\mathbf p$, so volume preservation for all $A$ is equivalent to $|J_{\phi_t}| = 1$. For an infinitesimal time step,
$$\phi_{\delta t}(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf x\\ \mathbf p\end{pmatrix} + \delta t\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\mathbf p}H \\ -\partial_{\mathbf x}H\end{pmatrix} + O(\delta t^2), \qquad J_{\phi_{\delta t}} = \begin{pmatrix}1 + \delta t\,\partial_{\mathbf x}\partial_{\mathbf p}H & \delta t\,\partial^2_{\mathbf p}H \\ -\delta t\,\partial^2_{\mathbf x}H & 1 - \delta t\,\partial_{\mathbf p}\partial_{\mathbf x}H\end{pmatrix} + O(\delta t^2).$$
The determinant is $1 + \delta t\,(\partial_{\mathbf x}\partial_{\mathbf p}H - \partial_{\mathbf p}\partial_{\mathbf x}H) + O(\delta t^2) = 1 + O(\delta t^2)$: the trace of the first-order term vanishes because mixed partial derivatives commute. For finite $t = n\,\delta t$, $\phi_t$ is the $n$-fold composition, so $|J_{\phi_t}| = (1 + O(\delta t^2))^n = 1 + O(n\,\delta t^2) = 1 + O(t^2/n) \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$.
:::

### Time reversibility

The flow is invertible, and its inverse is obtained by running time backwards: $\phi_t^{-1} = \phi_{-t}$, i.e. integrating $\dot x_i = -p_i/m_i$, $\dot p_i = +\partial_{x_i}U$. These are the original equations with $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$. Introducing the **momentum flip** $N_p(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = (\mathbf x, -\mathbf p)$,
$$\phi_t^{-1} = \phi_{-t} = N_p\circ\phi_t\circ N_p .$$
**To run a trajectory backwards: flip the momentum, run forwards, flip again.** For every forward trajectory $(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) \to (\mathbf x', \mathbf p')$ there is a backward one $(\mathbf x', -\mathbf p') \to (\mathbf x, -\mathbf p)$.

## Numerical integration of Hamilton's equations

In practice the flow must be approximated numerically, and *how* matters: we need an integrator that retains volume preservation and reversibility.

### Euler's method

$$p_i(t + \epsilon) = p_i(t) - \epsilon\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)), \qquad x_i(t + \epsilon) = x_i(t) + \epsilon\,p_i(t)/m_i .$$
Its Jacobian determinant is $\det\begin{pmatrix}1 & \epsilon/m \\ -\epsilon\,\partial^2_xU & 1\end{pmatrix} = 1 + \epsilon^2\partial_x^2U/m \neq 1$: **Euler does not preserve volume.** For the harmonic oscillator ($m = 1$, $U = x^2/2$) each step multiplies the phase-space area by $1 + \epsilon^2$ and the radius by $\sqrt{1 + \epsilon^2}$: the exact circular orbit becomes an outward spiral. With $\epsilon = 0.3$, twenty steps already inflate the radius by $(1.09)^{10} \approx 2.4$. Smaller steps ($\epsilon = 0.01$) delay the divergence, but over many steps (thousands) the trajectory still spirals away.

### Modified (symplectic) Euler

Update the momentum first, then use the *new* momentum to update the position:
$$p_i(t + \epsilon) = p_i(t) - \epsilon\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)), \qquad x_i(t + \epsilon) = x_i(t) + \epsilon\,p_i(t + \epsilon)/m_i .$$
This is the composition of a map that changes only momenta and a map that changes only positions, with Jacobians
$$J^{(p)} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -\epsilon\,\partial^2_{\mathbf x}U & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J^{(x)} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \epsilon\,\operatorname{diag}(m^{-1}) \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},$$
both triangular with unit diagonal, hence determinant 1. **The modified Euler method preserves volume exactly**, and trajectories no longer diverge. But it is not reversible: flipping the momentum, running it, and flipping again does not retrace the trajectory, because the order "momentum then position" is not symmetric in time.

### Leapfrog

The fix is to make the scheme symmetric: half a step in momentum, a full step in position, another half step in momentum.

::: {.definition name="Leapfrog step"}
$$\begin{aligned}
p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2) &= p_i(t) - \tfrac\epsilon2\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)), \\
x_i(t + \epsilon) &= x_i(t) + \epsilon\,p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2)/m_i, \\
p_i(t + \epsilon) &= p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2) - \tfrac\epsilon2\,\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t + \epsilon)).
\end{aligned}$$
:::

::: {.proposition name="Properties of leapfrog"}
The leapfrog map $\phi^{\text{lf}}_\epsilon$ is

1. **volume-preserving**: it is a composition of three shear maps (momentum, position, momentum), each with unit Jacobian determinant;
2. **time-reversible**: $(\phi^{\text{lf}}_\epsilon)^{-1} = \phi^{\text{lf}}_{-\epsilon} = N_p\circ\phi^{\text{lf}}_\epsilon\circ N_p$;
3. second-order accurate: the energy error is $O(\epsilon^2)$ and, for stable step sizes, **does not drift** over long trajectories.
:::

::: {.proof name="Proof of reversibility"}
Apply $\phi^{\text{lf}}_{-\epsilon}$ to the endpoint $(\mathbf x(t + \epsilon), \mathbf p(t + \epsilon))$:
$$\begin{aligned}
p_i' &= p_i(t + \epsilon) + \tfrac\epsilon2\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t + \epsilon)) = p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2), \\
x_i' &= x_i(t + \epsilon) - \epsilon\,p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2)/m_i = x_i(t), \\
p_i'' &= p_i(t + \tfrac\epsilon2) + \tfrac\epsilon2\partial_{x_i}U(\mathbf x(t)) = p_i(t),
\end{aligned}$$
recovering the starting point exactly (each line undoes the corresponding line of the forward step, in reverse order). And running $\phi_{-\epsilon}$ is the same as flipping $\mathbf p$, running $\phi_{+\epsilon}$, and flipping again.
:::

::: {.example name="Stability of leapfrog for a harmonic oscillator"}
Take $m = 1$, $U(x) = x^2/(2\sigma^2)$ (a Gaussian target of width $\sigma$). One leapfrog step is the linear map
$$\begin{pmatrix}x(t + \epsilon)\\ p(t + \epsilon)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2} & \epsilon \\[3pt] -\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\epsilon^3}{4\sigma^4} & 1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\ p(t)\end{pmatrix},$$
with determinant $\bigl(1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\bigr)^2 + \frac{\epsilon^2}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\epsilon^4}{4\sigma^4} = 1$ (volume preservation). With $a = 1 - \epsilon^2/(2\sigma^2)$ the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = a \pm\sqrt{a^2 - 1}$, with $\lambda_+\lambda_- = 1$.

- If $\epsilon < 2\sigma$, then $|a| < 1$, the eigenvalues are complex conjugates with $|\lambda_\pm| = 1$, and the trajectory is a stable (slightly distorted) ellipse.
- If $\epsilon > 2\sigma$, the eigenvalues are real with one of modulus $> 1$: the trajectory diverges exponentially.

**Rule of thumb: the step size must be smaller than the narrowest width of the target**, $\epsilon \lesssim \sigma_{\min}$.
:::

## The HMC algorithm

### The augmented distribution

To sample $\pi(\mathbf x)$, $\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n$, add $n$ auxiliary momenta, independent of $\mathbf x$ and of each other, $p_i \sim \mathcal N(0, m_i)$:
$$\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \pi(\mathbf x)\cdot\prod_i(2\pi m_i)^{-1/2}e^{-p_i^2/2m_i} .$$
If we can sample $\Pi$, the $\mathbf x$-components of the samples are distributed according to $\pi$ (just discard $\mathbf p$). Define the **potential** $U(\mathbf x) = -\log\pi(\mathbf x)$; then
$$\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \frac{1}{Z}e^{-H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p)}, \qquad H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = U(\mathbf x) + \sum_i\frac{p_i^2}{2m_i},$$
a Boltzmann distribution at unit temperature, with the momentum variances playing the role of masses. Below we take $m_i = 1$, so $H = U(\mathbf x) + \tfrac12|\mathbf p|^2$.

For Bayesian inference, $\pi(\mathbf x) = f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf x)/f(\mathbf y)$; since the evidence does not depend on $\mathbf x$ it is absorbed into $Z$, and
$$U(\mathbf x) = -\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf x) - \log f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf x).$$

### The proposal

Each HMC iteration is a composition of two Metropolis–Hastings moves on $\Pi$:

1. **Momentum refresh:** draw $\mathbf p_r \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, independently of the current $\mathbf p$.
2. **Hamiltonian move:** evolve $(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$ under the Hamiltonian flow for a time $t$ and flip the momentum: $(\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = N_p\circ\phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$.

::: {.proposition}
With the exact Hamiltonian flow, both moves are accepted with probability 1.
:::

::: {.proof}
*Move 1* is a Gibbs update: it samples $\mathbf p$ from its exact conditional $\Pi(\mathbf p\mid\mathbf x) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. Explicitly, the proposal is $T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p) = \delta(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x)(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|\mathbf p_r|^2/2}$ and the Hastings ratio is
$$\frac{\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\,T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)}{\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p)\,T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p)} = \frac{\pi(\mathbf x)e^{-|\mathbf p_r|^2/2}\cdot e^{-|\mathbf p|^2/2}}{\pi(\mathbf x)e^{-|\mathbf p|^2/2}\cdot e^{-|\mathbf p_r|^2/2}} = 1 .$$

*Move 2* is deterministic: $T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p'\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r) = \delta\bigl((\mathbf x', -\mathbf p') - \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\bigr)$. Energy conservation gives $\Pi(\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = \Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$ (the flip does not change $|\mathbf p|^2$), so the acceptance probability reduces to $\min\{1, T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x', \mathbf p')/T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p'\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\}$. For the reverse move,
$$T(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r\mid\mathbf x', \mathbf p') = \delta\bigl((\mathbf x, -\mathbf p_r) - \phi_t(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')\bigr) = \delta\bigl((\mathbf x', \mathbf p') - \phi_t^{-1}(\mathbf x, -\mathbf p_r)\bigr)\,|J_{\phi_t}|^{-1},$$
and $|J_{\phi_t}| = 1$ by volume preservation. By reversibility $\phi_t^{-1}\circ N_p = N_p\circ\phi_t$, so $\phi_t^{-1}(\mathbf x, -\mathbf p_r) = N_p\phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$ and the delta function equals $\delta\bigl((\mathbf x', -\mathbf p') - \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)\bigr) = T(\mathbf x', \mathbf p'\mid\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$. The ratio is 1.
:::

The proof shows exactly which properties are needed: **volume preservation** (no Jacobian factor), **reversibility with the momentum flip** (the reverse proposal exists and has the same density), and **energy conservation** ($\Pi$ is unchanged). The momentum flip is required for the proof; in practice it can be skipped, because the momentum is immediately resampled at the next iteration.

### With a numerical integrator

The exact flow is replaced by $L = t/\epsilon$ leapfrog steps. Leapfrog is exactly volume-preserving and reversible, so the proposal densities still cancel; but energy is conserved only approximately, $\Pi(\mathbf x', \mathbf p') \approx \Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$. The Metropolis correction takes care of this:

::: {.definition name="Hamiltonian Monte Carlo"}
From the current state $\mathbf x$:

1. draw $\mathbf p_r \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$;
2. apply $L$ leapfrog steps of size $\epsilon$ to $(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$, obtaining $(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')$ (and flip $\mathbf p'$);
3. accept $\mathbf x'$ with probability
$$\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\Pi(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')}{\Pi(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)}\Bigr\} = \min\bigl\{1, e^{H(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r) - H(\mathbf x', \mathbf p')}\bigr\};$$
otherwise keep $\mathbf x$.
:::

**The acceptance probability depends only on the integration error, not on the distance travelled.** With a stable step size the energy error stays small along arbitrarily long trajectories, so HMC can make large moves with high acceptance. The price is one gradient evaluation $\nabla U(\mathbf x) = -\nabla\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x) - \nabla\log f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf x)$ per leapfrog step.

::: {.remark name="HMC as gradient descent with momentum"}
The update $\mathbf p \leftarrow \mathbf p - \epsilon\nabla U$, $\mathbf x \leftarrow \mathbf x + \epsilon\mathbf p$ is "heavy-ball" gradient descent on $U = -\log\pi$: the direction of motion is not just the current gradient but has inertia. HMC balances **exploration** (the random momentum draw, which sets the direction and energy of each trajectory) and **exploitation** (the deterministic flow, which follows the geometry of the posterior). Unlike gradient descent, energy conservation prevents the trajectory from simply sliding to the mode: it converts potential into kinetic energy and back, travelling along the level sets of the posterior.
:::

## Advantages of HMC

The key advantage of HMC is that it **replaces the random walk of Metropolis by directed motion**. A random walk with step $\epsilon$ moves a distance $\sim\epsilon\sqrt k$ in $k$ steps; a Hamiltonian trajectory moves a distance $\sim\epsilon k$.

::: {.example name="A strongly correlated 2-D Gaussian"}
Sample $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Sigma)$ with
$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.98 \\ 0.98 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{eigenvalues } 1.98 \text{ and } 0.02 .$$
The standard deviations along the principal axes are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{1.98} \approx 1.41$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{0.02} \approx 0.14$. Both samplers must use steps of size $\approx\sigma_2$ to stay stable/accepted: HMC with $\epsilon = \sigma_2$ and $L = 20$ leapfrog steps per iteration; Metropolis with Gaussian jumps of size $\sigma_2$.

To reach an effectively independent point we must cross the long axis, a distance $\approx\sigma_1 \approx 10\,\sigma_2$. HMC's momentum along the long direction is stable, so about **10 leapfrog steps** suffice. Metropolis cannot move consistently in one direction: after $k$ steps it has moved $\sim\sigma_2\sqrt k$, so it needs **$\sim 100$ steps**.

With the same budget — 20 HMC iterations ($400$ leapfrog steps) versus $400$ Metropolis steps — the estimated covariances are (Allegra, Fig. 13.2)
$$\hat\Sigma_{\text{HMC}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.96 & 0.90 \\ 0.90 & 0.88\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat\Sigma_{\text{Met}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.26 & 0.26 \\ 0.26 & 0.30\end{pmatrix}.$$
HMC is already close to the truth; Metropolis has explored only a small part of the long axis and badly underestimates the variances.
:::

::: {.example name="A 100-dimensional Gaussian"}
Take $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(0.01^2, 0.02^2, \ldots, 1.00^2)$: 100 independent coordinates with widths from $0.01$ to $1$. Both step sizes must be of the order of the smallest width: HMC uses $\epsilon \sim \mathcal U[0.0104, 0.0156]$ with $L = 150$ leapfrog steps and $1000$ iterations; Metropolis uses jumps $\sim\mathcal U[0.0176, 0.0264]$ and $150{,}000$ steps — the same number of gradient/likelihood evaluations. (Randomising $\epsilon$ avoids accidental periodicities in which a trajectory returns exactly to its start.) HMC estimates the means and standard deviations of *all* coordinates well, including the widest ones; Metropolis gets the narrow coordinates right but grossly underestimates the spread of the wide ones, which it has barely explored.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Dimension scaling"}
For targets with $d$ roughly independent components, keeping the acceptance rate fixed requires a step size $\epsilon \propto d^{-1/2}$ for random-walk Metropolis but only $\epsilon \propto d^{-1/4}$ for HMC. Reaching an effectively independent point (moving a distance of order the target's width) therefore takes $O(d)$ Metropolis steps but only $O(d^{1/4})$ leapfrog steps. If each density or gradient evaluation costs $O(d)$, the total cost per independent sample is $O(d^2)$ for Metropolis versus $O(d^{5/4})$ for HMC (Neal 2011, §5.4.4). The optimal HMC acceptance rate in this limit is about $0.65$.
:::

## Practical considerations

- **Step size.** Too large: leapfrog becomes unstable and acceptance collapses ("divergent transitions"). Too small: wasted gradient evaluations. Modern implementations tune $\epsilon$ during warm-up to reach a target acceptance ($\approx 0.65$–$0.8$).
- **Trajectory length.** Too short: HMC degenerates into a random walk. Too long: trajectories loop back (for a Gaussian, after half a period). The **No-U-Turn Sampler** (NUTS, Hoffman & Gelman 2014) chooses $L$ adaptively, stopping when the trajectory starts to turn back; it underlies Stan and PyMC.
- **Mass matrix.** Using $\mathbf p \sim \mathcal N(0, M)$ with $M \approx \Sigma_\pi^{-1}$ (estimated during warm-up) makes all directions equally "stiff": it is equivalent to rescaling the target to unit covariance, which removes the $\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ penalty seen in the examples.
- **Limitations.** HMC requires a differentiable log-density on a continuous space: discrete parameters need Gibbs steps (or marginalisation). Multimodality remains hard. Each step costs a gradient, which can be expensive (automatic differentiation helps).

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Show that the modified Euler map $p \leftarrow p - \epsilon x$, $x \leftarrow x + \epsilon p$ for the harmonic oscillator has unit Jacobian, and find the condition on $\epsilon$ for stability.
:::

::: {.solution}
The map is $\begin{pmatrix}x'\\p'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \epsilon^2 & \epsilon \\ -\epsilon & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\p\end{pmatrix}$, determinant $1 - \epsilon^2 + \epsilon^2 = 1$. Trace $2 - \epsilon^2$: eigenvalues are complex with unit modulus iff $|2 - \epsilon^2| < 2$, i.e. $\epsilon < 2$ — the same stability limit as leapfrog with $\sigma = 1$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
For a Gaussian target $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Sigma)$ show that with mass matrix $M = \Sigma^{-1}$ the Hamiltonian dynamics reduce to independent oscillators *all with the same frequency*.
:::

::: {.solution}
$H = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x + \tfrac12\mathbf p^\top M^{-1}\mathbf p = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x + \tfrac12\mathbf p^\top\Sigma\mathbf p$. Then $\dot{\mathbf x} = \Sigma\mathbf p$, $\dot{\mathbf p} = -\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x$, so $\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf x$: every direction oscillates with unit angular frequency. With $M = I$ instead, the mode along eigenvector $k$ has frequency $1/\sigma_k$, and a single step size must resolve the fastest mode while the trajectory must be long enough for the slowest: the ratio $\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ is the cost of a poor mass matrix.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Write the HMC potential $U$ and its gradient for the posterior of $\mu$ in the model $x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, 1)$, $\mu \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$U(\mu) = \tfrac12\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \mu^2/(2\tau^2)$ + const, $U'(\mu) = -\sum_i(x_i - \mu) + \mu/\tau^2 = (n + \tau^{-2})\mu - n\bar x$. The target is Gaussian with $\sigma^2 = (n + \tau^{-2})^{-1}$, so stable leapfrog needs $\epsilon < 2\sigma$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Explain why the momentum flip is needed in the proof of detailed balance but can be omitted in practice.
:::

::: {.solution}
Without the flip, the reverse of the proposal $(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r) \to \phi_t(\mathbf x, \mathbf p_r)$ would require running the flow *backwards*, which is not the same proposal mechanism, so $T(\text{reverse})$ would vanish and detailed balance would fail. With the flip, the proposal is an involution: applying it twice returns the start. In practice the momentum is discarded and redrawn from the symmetric $\mathcal N(0, I)$ at the next iteration, so its sign never matters for the $\mathbf x$-chain.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
HMC is the default sampler for smooth posteriors over continuous parameters (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro). Use Gibbs for discrete latent variables and random-walk Metropolis only as a baseline or when gradients are unavailable.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
Poor HMC mixing (low ESS, divergences) usually signals bad geometry. Reparameterise to make the posterior more isotropic, or adapt the mass matrix.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
Leapfrog energy errors oscillate but do not drift for stable step sizes. This is why long trajectories remain acceptable — an integrator with energy drift (like Euler) would make acceptance decay with trajectory length.
:::
