2  Defining information

Information theory begins with a puzzle: what does it mean, quantitatively, that one message is more informative than another? Shannon’s answer — that the information content of an event is the logarithm of the reciprocal of its probability — turns out to have a striking number of good properties. This chapter fixes the vocabulary and derives the first quantity of interest, the entropy of a probability space.

2.1 Events and probability spaces

We assume the reader is familiar with probability. Recall the standard structure:

A probability space is a triple \((\Omega, \mathcal F, P)\) where \(\Omega\) is the sample space, \(\mathcal F\) a \(\sigma\)-algebra of events (subsets of \(\Omega\)), and \(P : \mathcal F \to [0, 1]\) a probability measure obeying the Kolmogorov axioms.

For most of what follows we work with a finite alphabet \(\mathcal X\) and take \(\Omega = \mathcal X\), \(\mathcal F = 2^{\mathcal X}\). The atomic events are singletons \(\{x\}\) with probabilities \(p_X(x) = P(\{X = x\})\).

2.2 The surprisal

The right quantitative notion of “how much information does the event \(\{X = x\}\) carry” turns out to be the surprisal.

The surprisal (or self-information) of an event \(A\) with probability \(P(A)\) is \[I(A) = -\log_2 P(A) \quad \text{bits}.\] For a random variable \(X\) taking value \(x\) we write \(I(x) = -\log_2 p_X(x)\).

The function \(I(A) = f(P(A))\) is uniquely determined (up to the choice of logarithm base) by three properties:

  1. Monotonicity. \(I\) is a decreasing function of \(P\): rarer events are more surprising.
  2. Non-negativity. \(I \geq 0\), with \(I = 0\) iff \(P = 1\).
  3. Additivity for independent events. If \(A, B\) are independent, \(I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B)\).

Cauchy’s functional equation forces \(f(p) = -\log_c p\) for some base \(c > 1\); the choice \(c = 2\) measures information in bits.

Remark. The base of the logarithm is a matter of units, not physics. \(c = 2\) gives bits (Shannon), \(c = e\) gives nats (Kolmogorov), \(c = 10\) gives dits/hartleys. All results in the notes can be rewritten in any base by multiplying entropy by the appropriate constant.

For a fair coin, \(p(H) = p(T) = 1/2\): each outcome carries \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit of information. For a biased coin with \(p(H) = 0.99\), seeing heads carries only \(-\log_2 0.99 \approx 0.014\) bits — the outcome was almost certain. Seeing tails carries \(-\log_2 0.01 \approx 6.6\) bits — a much more informative event.

2.3 Probability trees

A useful visualisation for compound events is the probability tree: a rooted tree with edges labelled by conditional probabilities, whose leaves are the possible outcomes. Multiplying probabilities along a path gives the probability of the corresponding sequence.

For any sequence of events \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\), \[I(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} I(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}),\] where \(I(x_i \mid \cdot) = -\log_2 p(x_i \mid \cdot)\) is the conditional surprisal.

This is a direct consequence of the chain rule \(p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1})\) and additivity of the logarithm.

2.4 Information for a probability space

The surprisal is the information of a single outcome. To characterise the information content of the whole distribution, we average over all outcomes.

The Shannon entropy of a discrete random variable \(X\) with distribution \(p_X\) is \[H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal X} p_X(x)\,\log_2 p_X(x) = \mathbb E[I(X)] .\] By convention \(0\log 0 = 0\).

The entropy is the expected number of bits of information carried by a random outcome. This is Shannon’s operational reading, and it will be justified rigorously by the source-coding theorem in Chapter 3.

For \(X \sim \text{Bern}(p)\), \(H(X) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \equiv h_2(p)\). The binary entropy function \(h_2\) is symmetric around \(p = 1/2\), where it takes its maximum value \(h_2(1/2) = 1\) bit. It vanishes at \(p = 0\) or \(p = 1\) — no information from a certain outcome.

For a variable \(X\) taking \(M\) values, the entropy is maximised when \(p_X\) is uniform, in which case \(H(X) = \log_2 M\). Non-uniform distributions give strictly less entropy — a preview of the general theorem that entropy is concave in \(p\).

2.5 Elementary properties

The entropy is well-behaved as a function of the distribution.

For any discrete random variable \(X\) with distribution on \(\mathcal X\):

  1. \(H(X) \geq 0\), with equality iff \(X\) is deterministic.
  2. \(H(X) \leq \log_2 |\mathcal X|\), with equality iff \(X\) is uniform.
  3. \(H\) is a concave function of \(p_X\).
  4. Grouping property. If two outcomes \(x_1, x_2\) are merged into one with probability \(p_1 + p_2\), then \[H(X) = H(X_{merged}) + (p_1 + p_2)\,h_2\bigl(p_1/(p_1 + p_2)\bigr).\]

For a pair \((X, Y)\) with joint distribution \(p(x, y)\), \[H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log_2 p(x, y) \leq H(X) + H(Y),\] with equality iff \(X, Y\) are independent (this is a special case of the sub-additivity property proved in Chapter 2).

2.6 Exercises

A fair die is rolled. Compute (i) the surprisal of the event “the outcome is 6”, (ii) the entropy of the rolling process, (iii) the surprisal of the event “the outcome is even”.

Solution.

  1. \(I(\{6\}) = -\log_2(1/6) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585\) bits.
  2. \(H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585\) bits (uniform on 6 outcomes).
  3. \(I(\{2, 4, 6\}) = -\log_2(3/6) = 1\) bit — exactly one bit of information (equivalent to a coin toss).

Show that for a Bernoulli\((p)\) variable the entropy is maximised at \(p = 1/2\) by direct differentiation. What is the second derivative at the maximum?

Solution. \(H'(p) = -\log_2 p + \log_2(1 - p) = \log_2\bigl((1-p)/p\bigr)\). Setting \(H' = 0\) gives \(p = 1/2\). The second derivative is \(H''(p) = -[1/(p\ln 2) + 1/((1 - p)\ln 2)]\), which at \(p = 1/2\) equals \(-4/\ln 2 \approx -5.77\). Negative \(H''\) confirms concavity — a global maximum.

Verify the grouping property for a three-outcome variable with probabilities \((1/2, 1/4, 1/4)\).

Solution. Direct: \(H = -\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 = 1.5\) bits. Grouping the last two into a merged outcome of probability \(1/2\): \(H_{merged} = h_2(1/2) = 1\) bit, plus \(1/2 \cdot h_2(1/2) = 0.5\) bits, total \(1.5\) bits. ✓

2.7 Callouts

Shannon’s definition is operational — it derives its meaning from the coding theorems of Chapter 3. Purely axiomatic derivations (e.g. Khinchin’s) are elegant but do not tell you why the answer is useful.

When computing entropies by hand, remember the identity \(\log_2 n = \log_2 e \cdot \ln n \approx 1.4427 \ln n\): it lets you convert between nats and bits without a calculator.

The convention \(0\log 0 = 0\) is genuine, not a hack: \(p \log(1/p) \to 0\) as \(p \to 0^+\). But if you compute \(I(x)\) for an event that has empirical probability zero from a finite sample, the value is \(+\infty\), and downstream computations will be nonsense. Always regularise.

Information theory begins with a puzzle: what does it mean, quantitatively, that one message is *more informative* than another? Shannon's answer — that the information content of an event is the logarithm of the reciprocal of its probability — turns out to have a striking number of good properties. This chapter fixes the vocabulary and derives the first quantity of interest, the entropy of a probability space.

## Events and probability spaces

We assume the reader is familiar with probability. Recall the standard structure:

::: {.definition name="Probability space"}
A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal F, P)$ where $\Omega$ is the sample space, $\mathcal F$ a $\sigma$-algebra of events (subsets of $\Omega$), and $P : \mathcal F \to [0, 1]$ a probability measure obeying the Kolmogorov axioms.
:::

For most of what follows we work with a finite alphabet $\mathcal X$ and take $\Omega = \mathcal X$, $\mathcal F = 2^{\mathcal X}$. The atomic events are singletons $\{x\}$ with probabilities $p_X(x) = P(\{X = x\})$.

## The surprisal

The right quantitative notion of "how much information does the event $\{X = x\}$ carry" turns out to be the **surprisal**.

::: {.definition name="Surprisal"}
The **surprisal** (or self-information) of an event $A$ with probability $P(A)$ is
$$I(A) = -\log_2 P(A) \quad \text{bits}.$$
For a random variable $X$ taking value $x$ we write $I(x) = -\log_2 p_X(x)$.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Characterising the surprisal"}
The function $I(A) = f(P(A))$ is uniquely determined (up to the choice of logarithm base) by three properties:

1. **Monotonicity.** $I$ is a decreasing function of $P$: rarer events are more surprising.
2. **Non-negativity.** $I \geq 0$, with $I = 0$ iff $P = 1$.
3. **Additivity for independent events.** If $A, B$ are independent, $I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B)$.

Cauchy's functional equation forces $f(p) = -\log_c p$ for some base $c > 1$; the choice $c = 2$ measures information in *bits*.
:::

::: {.remark}

:::

::: {.example name="Coin toss"}
For a fair coin, $p(H) = p(T) = 1/2$: each outcome carries $\log_2 2 = 1$ bit of information. For a biased coin with $p(H) = 0.99$, seeing heads carries only $-\log_2 0.99 \approx 0.014$ bits — the outcome was almost certain. Seeing tails carries $-\log_2 0.01 \approx 6.6$ bits — a much more informative event.
:::

## Probability trees

A useful visualisation for compound events is the **probability tree**: a rooted tree with edges labelled by conditional probabilities, whose leaves are the possible outcomes. Multiplying probabilities along a path gives the probability of the corresponding sequence.

::: {.lemma name="Chain rule for surprisal"}
For any sequence of events $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$,
$$I(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} I(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}),$$
where $I(x_i \mid \cdot) = -\log_2 p(x_i \mid \cdot)$ is the *conditional surprisal*.
:::

This is a direct consequence of the chain rule $p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1})$ and additivity of the logarithm.

## Information for a probability space

The surprisal is the information of a *single* outcome. To characterise the information content of the whole distribution, we average over all outcomes.

::: {.definition name="Shannon entropy"}
The **Shannon entropy** of a discrete random variable $X$ with distribution $p_X$ is
$$H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal X} p_X(x)\,\log_2 p_X(x) = \mathbb E[I(X)] .$$
By convention $0\log 0 = 0$.
:::

The entropy is the *expected* number of bits of information carried by a random outcome. This is Shannon's operational reading, and it will be justified rigorously by the source-coding theorem in Chapter 3.

::: {.example name="Entropy of Bernoulli"}
For $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$, $H(X) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \equiv h_2(p)$. The **binary entropy function** $h_2$ is symmetric around $p = 1/2$, where it takes its maximum value $h_2(1/2) = 1$ bit. It vanishes at $p = 0$ or $p = 1$ — no information from a certain outcome.
:::

::: {.example name="Uniform distribution maximises entropy"}
For a variable $X$ taking $M$ values, the entropy is maximised when $p_X$ is uniform, in which case $H(X) = \log_2 M$. Non-uniform distributions give strictly less entropy — a preview of the general theorem that entropy is concave in $p$.
:::

## Elementary properties

The entropy is well-behaved as a function of the distribution.

::: {.proposition name="Basic properties of entropy"}
For any discrete random variable $X$ with distribution on $\mathcal X$:

1. $H(X) \geq 0$, with equality iff $X$ is deterministic.
2. $H(X) \leq \log_2 |\mathcal X|$, with equality iff $X$ is uniform.
3. $H$ is a concave function of $p_X$.
4. **Grouping property.** If two outcomes $x_1, x_2$ are merged into one with probability $p_1 + p_2$, then
$$H(X) = H(X_{merged}) + (p_1 + p_2)\,h_2\bigl(p_1/(p_1 + p_2)\bigr).$$
:::

::: {.corollary name="Entropy of a joint variable"}
For a pair $(X, Y)$ with joint distribution $p(x, y)$,
$$H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log_2 p(x, y) \leq H(X) + H(Y),$$
with equality iff $X, Y$ are independent (this is a special case of the sub-additivity property proved in Chapter 2).
:::
