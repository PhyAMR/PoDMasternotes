2  Defining information

Information theory begins with a puzzle: what does it mean, quantitatively, that one message is more informative than another? The outcome of a random variable \(X\) is, by definition, uncertain — when we toss a coin we do not know whether it will land heads or tails. We can think of the information associated with \(X\) either as the uncertainty we have before the outcome is known, or, equivalently, as the knowledge we gain once the outcome is revealed.

In this chapter we derive a measure of information from first principles, by requiring it to satisfy a few intuitively appealing properties. Shannon’s answer — the information content of an event is the logarithm of the reciprocal of its probability — turns out to be the only answer compatible with those properties. Averaging it over all outcomes gives the first central quantity of the course, the entropy of a probability space.

2.1 Events and probability spaces

We start in the most abstract setting.

A probability space is a triple \(S = (\Omega, \mathcal F, P)\) where

  • \(\Omega\) is the sample space, the set of elementary outcomes;
  • \(\mathcal F \subseteq \mathcal P(\Omega)\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra of events. An event \(E\) is a set of outcomes, \(E \subseteq \Omega\); the family \(\mathcal F\) must be closed under union, intersection and complement;
  • \(P : \mathcal F \to [0, 1]\) is a probability, with (i) \(P(\Omega) = 1\) and (ii) \(\sigma\)-additivity: for any countable family of pairwise disjoint events \(\{E_i\}_{i \in I}\), \(E_i \cap E_j = \emptyset\) for \(i \neq j\), \[P\Bigl(\bigcup_{i \in I} E_i\Bigr) = \sum_{i \in I} P(E_i).\]

For most of what follows we work with a finite alphabet \(\mathcal X\) and take \(\Omega = \mathcal X\), \(\mathcal F = 2^{\mathcal X}\) (the power set). The atomic events are singletons \(\{x\}\) with probabilities \(p_X(x) = P(\{X = x\})\).

Toss two independent fair coins. The sample space is \[\Omega = \{hh, ht, th, tt\},\] where \(hh\) means “heads in the first toss and heads in the second toss”. The set of events is the whole power set, \(2^4 = 16\) events: \[\begin{aligned} \mathcal F = \bigl\{&\emptyset,\ \{hh\}, \{ht\}, \{th\}, \{tt\},\ \{hh,ht\}, \{hh,th\}, \{hh,tt\}, \{ht,th\}, \{ht,tt\}, \{th,tt\},\\ &\{hh,ht,th\}, \{hh,ht,tt\}, \{hh,th,tt\}, \{ht,th,tt\},\ \{hh,ht,th,tt\}\bigr\}. \end{aligned}\] Since all outcomes are equiprobable, \(P(E) = |E|/4\): one-outcome events have probability \(1/4\), two-outcome events \(1/2\), three-outcome events \(3/4\), and \(P(\Omega) = 1\).

2.1.1 Events as answers

A useful way to think about events — and the one that leads naturally to information — is the following: every event is a possible answer to the question “What was the outcome?”. Answers can be more or less specific depending on how many outcomes they include.

  • The four singletons \(\{hh\}, \{ht\}, \{th\}, \{tt\}\) fully specify the outcome (“the outcome was \(hh\)”).
  • The six two-outcome events are less detailed: \(\{hh, ht\}\) means “the outcome was \(hh\) or \(ht\)” — equivalently, “the first coin gave heads”.
  • The four three-outcome events are even vaguer: \(\{hh, ht, th\}\) means “the outcome was not \(tt\)”; it only excludes one possibility.
  • The event \(\Omega = \{hh, ht, th, tt\}\) is the trivial answer — we already knew that one of these happened.

So \(\mathcal F\) contains every possible answer, from the most detailed to the least detailed, and each answer has its own probability.

2.1.2 Conditional probability and independence

Disjoint events (\(E_1 \cap E_2 = \emptyset\)) are mutually exclusive answers; non-disjoint events are possibly compatible answers. Any event \(E_2\) splits into a part compatible with \(E_1\) and a part incompatible with it, \[E_2 = (E_1 \cap E_2) \cup (\bar E_1 \cap E_2).\] If we know that \(E_2\) occurred, then either \(E_1\) occurred too, or it did not. The relative weight of the first case defines the conditional probability.

For \(P(E_2) > 0\), the conditional probability of \(E_1\) given \(E_2\) is \[P(E_1 \mid E_2) \equiv \frac{P(E_1 \cap E_2)}{P(E_2)} = \frac{P(E_1 \cap E_2)}{P(E_1 \cap E_2) + P(\bar E_1 \cap E_2)}.\] Two events are independent if the probability of the joint event factorises, \[P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1)\,P(E_2),\] or equivalently \(P(E_1 \mid E_2) = P(E_1)\) and \(P(E_2 \mid E_1) = P(E_2)\).

In the “answers” language: independent events are complementary answers — knowing one does not make the other more or less likely.

In the two-coin space:

  • \(E_1 = \{hh, ht\}\) (“first toss gave \(h\)”) and \(E_2 = \{th, tt\}\) (“first toss gave \(t\)”) are mutually exclusive: \(E_1 \cap E_2 = \emptyset\).
  • \(E_1 = \{hh, ht, th\}\) (“not \(tt\)”) and \(E_2 = \{hh, ht, tt\}\) (“not \(th\)”) intersect in \(\{hh, ht\}\). Then \[P(E_1 \mid E_2) = \frac{P(\{hh, ht\})}{P(\{hh, ht, tt\})} = \frac{1/2}{3/4} = \frac23 < P(E_1) = \frac34 .\] Learning \(E_2\) decreases the likelihood of \(E_1\).
  • \(E_1 = \{hh\}\) and \(E_2 = \{hh, ht, tt\}\): \[P(E_1 \mid E_2) = \frac{1/4}{3/4} = \frac13 > P(E_1) = \frac14 .\] Learning \(E_2\) increases the likelihood of \(E_1\).
  • The pairs \[(\{hh,ht\}, \{hh,th\}),\quad (\{hh,ht\}, \{hh,tt\}),\quad (\{hh,ht\}, \{ht,th\})\] are all independent: e.g. for the second pair, “first toss gave \(h\)” and “the two tosses agree”, \(P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(\{hh\}) = 1/4 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12\). Knowing that the coins agree tells us nothing about the first coin. (In fact nearly all pairs of two-outcome events in this space are independent.)

Remark (When does conditioning increase a probability?). Write \(\Delta \equiv P(E_1 \cap \bar E_2)\) and \(\bar\Delta \equiv P(\bar E_1 \cap \bar E_2)\) for the probability that is “removed” from \(E_1\) and from its complement when we learn \(E_2\). Then \[P(E_1) = \frac{P(E_1)}{P(E_1) + P(\bar E_1)}, \qquad P(E_1 \mid E_2) = \frac{P(E_1) - \Delta}{P(E_1) - \Delta + P(\bar E_1) - \bar\Delta}.\] The two expressions have the same structure; conditioning simply subtracts the excluded probability. One checks that \[P(E_1 \mid E_2) > P(E_1) \iff \frac{\Delta}{\bar \Delta} < \frac{P(E_1)}{P(\bar E_1)},\] i.e. \(E_1\) becomes more likely exactly when it loses proportionally less probability than its complement.

2.2 The surprisal

Each event specifies the outcome to a larger or lesser degree, i.e. provides more or less information about it. Let us make this quantitative by constructing a function \[I : \mathcal F \to \mathbb R_+\] that assigns to each event the information it provides about the outcome.

2.2.1 Requirements

Intuitively we should agree that:

  1. Events with the same probability provide the same amount of information. Hence \(I\) depends on \(E\) only through its probability: \(I(E) = f(P(E))\) for some unknown \(f : [0,1] \to \mathbb R_+\).
  2. More probable events (more outcomes, vaguer answers) provide less information: \(f\) is monotonically decreasing. In particular \(I(\Omega) = f(1)\) should be zero — the trivial answer tells us nothing.
  3. Additivity for independent events. Independent events are complementary answers, so the information of the combined answer \(E = E_1 \cap E_2\) should be the sum of the two pieces: \(I(E_1 \cap E_2) = I(E_1) + I(E_2)\). Since \(P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1)P(E_2)\), this means \[f(x_1 x_2) = f(x_1) + f(x_2).\]

We could first say “the first coin gave \(h\)” (\(E_1 = \{hh, ht\}\)) and then “the second coin gave \(h\)” (\(E_2 = \{hh, th\}\)). These events are independent, and together they say exactly “the outcome was \(hh\)” (\(E_1 \cap E_2 = \{hh\}\)). It is natural to demand that the information in the single answer \(\{hh\}\) equal the sum of the information in the two partial answers.

2.2.2 The logarithm is forced

A monotonically decreasing function \(f : (0, 1] \to \mathbb R_+\) satisfying \(f(x_1 x_2) = f(x_1) + f(x_2)\) must be of the form \[f(x) = -\alpha \log x, \qquad \alpha > 0 .\]

Proof. Set \(x = e^y\) and \(g(y) = f(e^y)\). The functional equation becomes \[f(e^{y_1} e^{y_2}) = f(e^{y_1 + y_2}) = f(e^{y_1}) + f(e^{y_2}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(y_1 + y_2) = g(y_1) + g(y_2).\] This is Cauchy’s functional equation; its only monotone solutions are linear, \(g(y) = \alpha' y\). Hence \(f(e^y) = \alpha' y\), i.e. \(f(x) = \alpha' \log x\). Monotonic decrease requires \(\alpha' < 0\); write \(\alpha' = -\alpha\) with \(\alpha > 0\).

The surprisal (or self-information) of an event \(E\) is \[I(E) = -\log_2 P(E) \quad \text{bits}.\] For a random variable \(X\) taking value \(x\) we write \(I(x) = -\log_2 p_X(x)\).

The name reflects that low-probability events are more “surprising”. It is worth stressing what the surprisal measures: the amount of information we obtain when we learn that the outcome of a random process lies in a given subset \(E\) of all possible outcomes.

Remark (Units). The constant \(\alpha\) is arbitrary and only fixes the unit. Choosing \(\alpha\) is the same as choosing a base \(\beta = e^{1/\alpha}\) for the logarithm: \[-\alpha \log P = -\frac{\log P}{\log \beta} = -\log_\beta P .\] With \(\beta = 2\) we measure information in bits (binary digits); with \(\beta = e\) in nats; with \(\beta = 10\) in hartleys. Since \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\), \(1\ \text{bit} \approx 0.693\ \text{nat}\). From now on \(\log \equiv \log_2\) unless stated otherwise.

The bit has a simple interpretation: it is the information revealed when we learn the outcome of a process with two equiprobable outcomes, such as the toss of a fair coin.

For a fair coin each outcome carries \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit. For a biased coin with \(p(H) = 0.99\), seeing heads carries only \(-\log_2 0.99 \approx 0.014\) bits — the outcome was almost certain. Seeing tails carries \(-\log_2 0.01 \approx 6.6\) bits — a much more informative event.

\(I(\{hh\}) = -\log_2(1/4) = 2\) bits; \(I(\{hh, ht\}) = 1\) bit; \(I(\{hh, ht, th\}) = -\log_2(3/4) \approx 0.415\) bits; \(I(\Omega) = 0\). The more specific the answer, the more bits it carries; full specification of two coins costs two bits, one per coin.

2.2.3 Conditional surprisal: redundant and synergistic answers

What if we specify the outcome through answers that are not independent? If we already know that the outcome is in \(E_1\), the natural information carried by \(E_2\) is

\[I(E_2 \mid E_1) = -\log P(E_2 \mid E_1).\]

Since \(P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1) P(E_2 \mid E_1)\), taking logarithms gives the chain rule for surprisal \[I(E_1 \cap E_2) = I(E_1) + I(E_2 \mid E_1).\]

In general \(I(E_2 \mid E_1)\) can be larger or smaller than \(I(E_2)\). Correspondingly the information provided jointly by two non-independent events can be less (redundancy) or more (synergy) than the sum of their separate informations.

The event \(E = \{hh\}\) can be obtained as the intersection of the non-independent events \(E_1 = \{hh, ht, th\}\) (“at least one \(h\)”), \(E_2 = \{hh, th\}\) (“heads in the second toss”), \(E_3 = \{hh\}\). There is redundancy among these answers: \[P(E_2 \mid E_1) = \tfrac23 > P(E_2) = \tfrac12, \qquad P(E_3 \mid E_2) = \tfrac12 > P(E_3) = \tfrac14 .\] The separate surprisals are \[I(E_1) = -\log\tfrac34 \approx 0.42, \quad I(E_2) = 1, \quad I(E_3) = 2,\] so naïvely summing gives \(3.42 > I(E) = 2\) bits: we have “overcounted” the information. Using conditional surprisals instead, \[I(E) = I(E_1) + I(E_2 \mid E_1) + I(E_3 \mid E_2) = -\log\Bigl(\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12\Bigr) = -\log\tfrac14 = 2 \text{ bits}. \checkmark\]

Take \(E = \{hh, ht\}\) as the intersection of \(E_1 = \{hh, ht, th\}\) and \(E_2 = \{hh, ht, tt\}\). Then \[P(E) = \tfrac12 = \tfrac{8}{16} < P(E_1)P(E_2) = \tfrac{9}{16},\] and therefore \[I(E) = 1 > I(E_1) + I(E_2) = 2 \times 0.415 = 0.83 \text{ bits}.\] Each answer alone only excludes one outcome, but together they exclude two: the combination is worth more than the parts.

For any sequence of random variables \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\), \[I(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} I(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}),\] where \(I(x_i \mid \cdot) = -\log_2 p(x_i \mid \cdot)\) is the conditional surprisal.

This follows from the chain rule of probability \(p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1})\) and the additivity of the logarithm.

2.3 Probability trees

Possible answers to “What was the outcome?” can be arranged in a probability tree:

  • each node is a possible answer, i.e. an event \(E \in \mathcal F\), carrying probability \(P(E)\); the root is \(\Omega\);
  • each branching splits an answer \(E\) into more detailed, mutually exclusive answers \(E_1, \ldots, E_k\) with \(\bigcup_k E_k = E\); the \(k\)-th edge carries the conditional probability \(P(E_k \mid E)\);
  • at each level, the nodes form a partition of \(\Omega\).

Conceptually, each branching is a sub-question and each edge an answer to it. The probability of a node is the product of the conditional probabilities along the path from the root; correspondingly, since \(I = -\log P\), the information of a node is the sum of the informations along the edges leading to it. The same probability space can be split in many different ways, giving different trees.

Tree A (one coin at a time):

Omega --1/2--> {hh,ht}  "first coin h?"  --1/2--> {hh}
  |                                     +-1/2--> {ht}
  +---1/2--> {th,tt}                    --1/2--> {th}
                                        +-1/2--> {tt}

Each edge carries 1 bit; each leaf is reached after two edges, i.e. 2 bits, consistent with \(I(\{hh\}) = 2\).

Tree B (an unbalanced split): first ask “was it \(tt\)?”,

Omega --1/4--> {tt}
  +---3/4--> {hh,ht,th}  --2/3--> {hh,ht}  --1/2--> {hh}
                         |                 +-1/2--> {ht}
                         +-1/3--> {th}

Now the leaf \(\{hh\}\) is reached through edges carrying \(-\log\tfrac34 - \log\tfrac23 - \log\tfrac12 = 2\) bits — the same total, distributed differently along the path. The leaf \(\{tt\}\) is reached in one step, also with \(-\log\tfrac14 = 2\) bits.

2.4 Information for a probability space

The surprisal \(I(E)\) measures how much information event \(E\) gives about the outcome. We now ask: how much information is needed, on average, to completely specify the outcome? This will be the information content of the probability space as a whole.

An urn contains red, blue and green balls in proportions \(50\%, 25\%, 25\%\). We draw one ball. To identify its colour we may first ask “is it red?” (\(E_1 = \{r\}\) vs \(\bar E_1 = \{b, g\}\), each answer worth 1 bit). If not red, we ask “is it blue?” — a second bit. Thus red is identified with 1 bit, blue and green with 2 bits. On average \[\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 = 1.5 \text{ bits},\] which is the average number of yes/no questions needed to identify the outcome.

Now red, blue, green, yellow, purple in proportions \(50\%, 12.5\%, 12.5\%, 12.5\%, 12.5\%\). Ask “red?” (1 bit); if not, “blue or green?” (2nd bit); then “blue?” or “yellow?” (3rd bit). Red costs 1 question, every other colour 3. On average \[\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 2 \text{ bits}.\] Note that each question was designed to split the remaining probability in half, so every answer was worth exactly one bit.

Formalising the argument: for each outcome \(a \in \Omega\) of a discrete space there is an elementary event \(\{a\}\) with information \(-\log P(\{a\})\). The average information needed to specify the outcome is \[H(S) = \langle I \rangle = -\sum_{a \in \Omega} P(\{a\}) \log P(\{a\}),\] a single number for the whole space \(S = (\Omega, \mathcal F, P)\). Introducing a random variable \(X : \Omega \to \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}\) with \(p_X(x_i) = P(\{a\})\) when \(X(a) = x_i\) (in the urn, \(r \mapsto x_1\), \(b \mapsto x_2\), \(g \mapsto x_3\)):

The Shannon entropy of a discrete random variable \(X\) with distribution \(p_X\) is \[H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal X} p_X(x)\,\log_2 p_X(x) = \mathbb E[I(X)] .\] By convention \(0\log 0 = 0\).

The entropy is the expected number of bits carried by a random outcome — the average information needed to specify it. This operational reading will be made rigorous by the source-coding theorem in Chapter 3.

For \(X \sim \text{Bern}(p)\), \(H(X) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \equiv h_2(p)\). The binary entropy function \(h_2\) is symmetric around \(p = 1/2\), where it takes its maximum value \(h_2(1/2) = 1\) bit. It vanishes at \(p = 0\) or \(p = 1\) — no information from a certain outcome. A few values worth remembering: \(h_2(0.1) \approx 0.469\), \(h_2(0.11) \approx 0.5\), \(h_2(0.25) \approx 0.811\).

Let \(X\) take values \(a, b, c, d\) with probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\). Then \[H(X) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 1.75 \text{ bits}.\] An efficient questioning strategy is “Is \(X = a\)?”, then “Is \(X = b\)?”, then “Is \(X = c\)?”; the expected number of questions is exactly \(1.75\). We will see in Chapter 3 that no strategy can use fewer than \(H(X)\) questions on average.

An integer \(x\) is chosen uniformly in \(\{0, 1, \ldots, 63\}\). Ask: “is \(x \geq 32\)?”, “is \(x \bmod 32 \geq 16\)?”, “is \(x \bmod 16 \geq 8\)?”, “is \(x \bmod 8 \geq 4\)?”, “is \(x \bmod 4 \geq 2\)?”, “is \(x \bmod 2 = 1\)?”. Each question halves the set of candidates and therefore yields exactly one bit; after six questions \(x\) is known, consistent with \(H = \log_2 64 = 6\) bits. Writing the answers as \(1\) = yes, \(0\) = no gives precisely the binary expansion of \(x\) — the bits of information are the binary digits.

For a variable \(X\) taking \(M\) values, the entropy is maximised when \(p_X\) is uniform, in which case \(H(X) = \log_2 M\). Non-uniform distributions give strictly less entropy — a preview of the general result proved in Chapter 2.

2.5 An axiomatic derivation of entropy

Had we not derived the surprisal first, we could have obtained Shannon’s formula directly from requirements on \(H\) itself, following an argument reported by Bialek. We look for a functional \(H[X]\) measuring the information content of the distribution of \(X\).

Step 1: equiprobable outcomes. If \(p_X(x_i) = 1/N\) for all \(i\), then \(H\) should be a monotonically increasing function of the number of outcomes: \(H[X] = g(N)\).

Step 2: composition. Let \(N = k^m\). We can draw a tree with \(m\) levels and \(k\) equiprobable branches at each level, and map \(X \mapsto (X_1, \ldots, X_m)\) where each \(X_j\) is an independent variable with \(k\) equally likely outcomes — each outcome of \(X\) is identified by answering \(m\) independent sub-questions. (Two coins: \(k = 2\), \(m = 2\).) The total information must be the sum of the parts: \[g(k^m) = \sum_{j=1}^m H[X_j] = m\, g(k).\]

The only monotonic function satisfying \(g(k^m) = m\, g(k)\) for all integers \(k, m\) is \(g(x) = \alpha \log x\).

Proof. Fix integers \(k, \ell \geq 2\) and \(n \geq 1\), and choose \(m\) such that \(k^m \leq \ell^n < k^{m+1}\), i.e. \(m = \lfloor n \log \ell / \log k \rfloor\). (E.g. \(k = 4\), \(\ell = 3\), \(n = 3\): \(m = \lfloor 2.37 \rfloor = 2\).) Taking logarithms and dividing by \(n \log k\): \[\frac mn \leq \frac{\log \ell}{\log k} < \frac mn + \frac1n \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Bigl|\frac{\log \ell}{\log k} - \frac mn\Bigr| \leq \frac1n .\] By monotonicity \(g(k^m) \leq g(\ell^n) < g(k^{m+1})\), i.e. \(m\, g(k) \leq n\, g(\ell) < (m+1) g(k)\); dividing by \(n\, g(k)\), \[\Bigl|\frac{g(\ell)}{g(k)} - \frac mn\Bigr| \leq \frac1n .\] By the triangle inequality, \(|g(\ell)/g(k) - \log \ell/\log k| \leq 2/n\) for every \(n\). Letting \(n \to \infty\) gives \(g(\ell)/g(k) = \log \ell / \log k\) for all \(k, \ell\), i.e. \(g(x) = \alpha \log x\).

Thus for \(N\) equiprobable outcomes \(H[X] = \log N\) (choosing \(\alpha\) so that we work in bits).

Step 3: non-uniform probabilities. Suppose the probabilities \(p_i\) are rational (any real can be approximated arbitrarily well). Write \(p_i = r_i / s_i\) and let \(M = \prod_l s_l\); then \(p_i = k_i / M\) with \(k_i = r_i \prod_{l \neq i} s_l \in \mathbb N\) and \(\sum_i k_i = M\).

Imagine a variable \(Y\) with \(M\) equiprobable outcomes arranged into \(N\) groups, group \(i\) containing \(k_i\) outcomes. Let \(X\) be the group label (so \(P(X = i) = k_i / M = p_i\)) and \(Z_i\) the position within group \(i\) (uniform on \(k_i\) values). Identifying \(Y\) takes two sub-questions — first the group, then the element — so \[H[Y] = H[X] + \sum_i p_i\, H[Z_i],\] where the second term is averaged because the different branches are reached with different probabilities. Since \(H[Y] = \log M\) and \(H[Z_i] = \log k_i\), \[\log M = H[X] + \sum_i p_i \log k_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H[X] = -\sum_i p_i \log\frac{k_i}{M} = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i . \qquad\blacksquare\]

Take \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16)\). With \(M = 6\): \(k = (3, 2, 1)\). Then \(H[Y] = \log 6 \approx 2.585\), and the within-group terms give \(\tfrac12 \log 3 + \tfrac13 \log 2 + \tfrac16 \log 1 \approx 0.792 + 0.333 = 1.126\). Hence \(H[X] \approx 2.585 - 1.126 = 1.459\) bits, which agrees with \(-\tfrac12\log\tfrac12 - \tfrac13\log\tfrac13 - \tfrac16\log\tfrac16 \approx 0.5 + 0.528 + 0.431 = 1.459\). \(\checkmark\)

The composition rule used in Step 3 is the grouping property of entropy, which we state in general below.

2.6 Elementary properties

The entropy is well-behaved as a function of the distribution (proofs in Chapter 2).

For any discrete random variable \(X\) with distribution on \(\mathcal X\):

  1. \(H(X) \geq 0\), with equality iff \(X\) is deterministic.
  2. \(H(X) \leq \log_2 |\mathcal X|\), with equality iff \(X\) is uniform.
  3. \(H\) is a concave function of \(p_X\).
  4. Grouping property. If two outcomes \(x_1, x_2\) are merged into one with probability \(p_1 + p_2\), then \[H(X) = H(X_{merged}) + (p_1 + p_2)\,h_2\bigl(p_1/(p_1 + p_2)\bigr).\]

For a pair \((X, Y)\) with joint distribution \(p(x, y)\), \[H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log_2 p(x, y) \leq H(X) + H(Y),\] with equality iff \(X, Y\) are independent (sub-additivity, proved in Chapter 2).

2.7 Exercises

A fair die is rolled. Compute (i) the surprisal of the event “the outcome is 6”, (ii) the entropy of the rolling process, (iii) the surprisal of the event “the outcome is even”.

Solution.

  1. \(I(\{6\}) = -\log_2(1/6) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585\) bits.
  2. \(H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585\) bits (uniform on 6 outcomes).
  3. \(I(\{2, 4, 6\}) = -\log_2(3/6) = 1\) bit — exactly one bit of information (equivalent to a coin toss).

Show that for a Bernoulli\((p)\) variable the entropy is maximised at \(p = 1/2\) by direct differentiation. What is the second derivative at the maximum?

Solution. \(H'(p) = -\log_2 p + \log_2(1 - p) = \log_2\bigl((1-p)/p\bigr)\). Setting \(H' = 0\) gives \(p = 1/2\). The second derivative is \(H''(p) = -[1/(p\ln 2) + 1/((1 - p)\ln 2)]\), which at \(p = 1/2\) equals \(-4/\ln 2 \approx -5.77\). Negative \(H''\) confirms concavity — a global maximum.

Verify the grouping property for a three-outcome variable with probabilities \((1/2, 1/4, 1/4)\).

Solution. Direct: \(H = -\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 = 1.5\) bits. Grouping the last two into a merged outcome of probability \(1/2\): \(H_{merged} = h_2(1/2) = 1\) bit, plus \(1/2 \cdot h_2(1/2) = 0.5\) bits, total \(1.5\) bits. \(\checkmark\)

In the two-coin space, check that \(E_1 = \{hh, ht\}\) (“first coin \(h\)”) and \(E_2 = \{ht, th\}\) (“coins differ”) are independent, and compute \(I(E_1)\), \(I(E_2)\) and \(I(E_1 \cap E_2)\).

Solution. \(E_1 \cap E_2 = \{ht\}\), \(P = 1/4 = P(E_1)P(E_2) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12\): independent. \(I(E_1) = I(E_2) = 1\) bit and \(I(\{ht\}) = 2 = I(E_1) + I(E_2)\) bits, as additivity requires.

Twelve balls look identical, but one is either heavier or lighter than the others. How much information is needed to identify the odd ball and whether it is heavy or light? A balance has three outcomes (left heavy, right heavy, balanced). What is the minimum number of weighings needed in principle?

Solution. There are \(12 \times 2 = 24\) equiprobable hypotheses, so \(\log_2 24 \approx 4.58\) bits are needed. A weighing has three outcomes and can deliver at most \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits. Hence at least \(\lceil 4.58 / 1.58 \rceil = 3\) weighings are needed — and three indeed suffice if each weighing is designed so that its three outcomes are as close to equiprobable as possible (MacKay §4.1). The lesson: good experiments maximise the entropy of their outcome.

2.8 Callouts

Shannon’s definition is operational — it derives its meaning from the coding theorems of Chapter 3. Purely axiomatic derivations (like the one above) show that the formula is unique, but do not tell you why it is useful.

When computing entropies by hand, remember the identity \(\log_2 n = \log_2 e \cdot \ln n \approx 1.4427 \ln n\): it lets you convert between nats and bits without a calculator.

The convention \(0\log 0 = 0\) is genuine, not a hack: \(p \log(1/p) \to 0\) as \(p \to 0^+\). But if you compute \(I(x)\) for an event that has empirical probability zero from a finite sample, the value is \(+\infty\), and downstream computations will be nonsense. Always regularise.

title: "Defining information"
---

Information theory begins with a puzzle: what does it mean, quantitatively, that one message is *more informative* than another? The outcome of a random variable $X$ is, by definition, uncertain — when we toss a coin we do not know whether it will land heads or tails. We can think of the information associated with $X$ either as the *uncertainty* we have before the outcome is known, or, equivalently, as the *knowledge we gain* once the outcome is revealed.

In this chapter we derive a measure of information from first principles, by requiring it to satisfy a few intuitively appealing properties. Shannon's answer — the information content of an event is the logarithm of the reciprocal of its probability — turns out to be the *only* answer compatible with those properties. Averaging it over all outcomes gives the first central quantity of the course, the entropy of a probability space.

## Events and probability spaces

We start in the most abstract setting.

::: {.definition name="Probability space"}
A **probability space** is a triple $S = (\Omega, \mathcal F, P)$ where

- $\Omega$ is the **sample space**, the set of elementary outcomes;
- $\mathcal F \subseteq \mathcal P(\Omega)$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of **events**. An event $E$ is a set of outcomes, $E \subseteq \Omega$; the family $\mathcal F$ must be closed under union, intersection and complement;
- $P : \mathcal F \to [0, 1]$ is a **probability**, with (i) $P(\Omega) = 1$ and (ii) $\sigma$-additivity: for any countable family of pairwise disjoint events $\{E_i\}_{i \in I}$, $E_i \cap E_j = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$,
$$P\Bigl(\bigcup_{i \in I} E_i\Bigr) = \sum_{i \in I} P(E_i).$$
:::

For most of what follows we work with a finite alphabet $\mathcal X$ and take $\Omega = \mathcal X$, $\mathcal F = 2^{\mathcal X}$ (the power set). The atomic events are singletons $\{x\}$ with probabilities $p_X(x) = P(\{X = x\})$.

::: {.example name="Two fair coins"}
Toss two independent fair coins. The sample space is
$$\Omega = \{hh, ht, th, tt\},$$
where $hh$ means "heads in the first toss and heads in the second toss". The set of events is the whole power set, $2^4 = 16$ events:
$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F = \bigl\{&\emptyset,\ \{hh\}, \{ht\}, \{th\}, \{tt\},\ \{hh,ht\}, \{hh,th\}, \{hh,tt\}, \{ht,th\}, \{ht,tt\}, \{th,tt\},\\
&\{hh,ht,th\}, \{hh,ht,tt\}, \{hh,th,tt\}, \{ht,th,tt\},\ \{hh,ht,th,tt\}\bigr\}.
\end{aligned}$$
Since all outcomes are equiprobable, $P(E) = |E|/4$: one-outcome events have probability $1/4$, two-outcome events $1/2$, three-outcome events $3/4$, and $P(\Omega) = 1$.
:::

### Events as answers

A useful way to think about events — and the one that leads naturally to information — is the following: **every event is a possible answer to the question "What was the outcome?"**. Answers can be more or less specific depending on how many outcomes they include.

- The four singletons $\{hh\}, \{ht\}, \{th\}, \{tt\}$ fully specify the outcome ("the outcome was $hh$").
- The six two-outcome events are less detailed: $\{hh, ht\}$ means "the outcome was $hh$ or $ht$" — equivalently, "the first coin gave heads".
- The four three-outcome events are even vaguer: $\{hh, ht, th\}$ means "the outcome was *not* $tt$"; it only excludes one possibility.
- The event $\Omega = \{hh, ht, th, tt\}$ is the trivial answer — we already knew that one of these happened.

So $\mathcal F$ contains every possible answer, from the most detailed to the least detailed, and each answer has its own probability.

### Conditional probability and independence

Disjoint events ($E_1 \cap E_2 = \emptyset$) are *mutually exclusive* answers; non-disjoint events are *possibly compatible* answers. Any event $E_2$ splits into a part compatible with $E_1$ and a part incompatible with it,
$$E_2 = (E_1 \cap E_2) \cup (\bar E_1 \cap E_2).$$
If we know that $E_2$ occurred, then either $E_1$ occurred too, or it did not. The relative weight of the first case defines the conditional probability.

::: {.definition name="Conditional probability and independence"}
For $P(E_2) > 0$, the **conditional probability** of $E_1$ given $E_2$ is
$$P(E_1 \mid E_2) \equiv \frac{P(E_1 \cap E_2)}{P(E_2)} = \frac{P(E_1 \cap E_2)}{P(E_1 \cap E_2) + P(\bar E_1 \cap E_2)}.$$
Two events are **independent** if the probability of the joint event factorises,
$$P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1)\,P(E_2),$$
or equivalently $P(E_1 \mid E_2) = P(E_1)$ and $P(E_2 \mid E_1) = P(E_2)$.
:::

In the "answers" language: **independent events are complementary answers** — knowing one does not make the other more or less likely.

::: {.example name="Conditioning can raise or lower a probability"}
In the two-coin space:

- $E_1 = \{hh, ht\}$ ("first toss gave $h$") and $E_2 = \{th, tt\}$ ("first toss gave $t$") are mutually exclusive: $E_1 \cap E_2 = \emptyset$.
- $E_1 = \{hh, ht, th\}$ ("not $tt$") and $E_2 = \{hh, ht, tt\}$ ("not $th$") intersect in $\{hh, ht\}$. Then
$$P(E_1 \mid E_2) = \frac{P(\{hh, ht\})}{P(\{hh, ht, tt\})} = \frac{1/2}{3/4} = \frac23 < P(E_1) = \frac34 .$$
Learning $E_2$ *decreases* the likelihood of $E_1$.
- $E_1 = \{hh\}$ and $E_2 = \{hh, ht, tt\}$:
$$P(E_1 \mid E_2) = \frac{1/4}{3/4} = \frac13 > P(E_1) = \frac14 .$$
Learning $E_2$ *increases* the likelihood of $E_1$.
- The pairs
$$(\{hh,ht\}, \{hh,th\}),\quad (\{hh,ht\}, \{hh,tt\}),\quad (\{hh,ht\}, \{ht,th\})$$
are all independent: e.g. for the second pair, "first toss gave $h$" and "the two tosses agree", $P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(\{hh\}) = 1/4 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12$. Knowing that the coins agree tells us nothing about the first coin. (In fact nearly all pairs of two-outcome events in this space are independent.)
:::

::: {.remark name="When does conditioning increase a probability?"}
Write $\Delta \equiv P(E_1 \cap \bar E_2)$ and $\bar\Delta \equiv P(\bar E_1 \cap \bar E_2)$ for the probability that is "removed" from $E_1$ and from its complement when we learn $E_2$. Then
$$P(E_1) = \frac{P(E_1)}{P(E_1) + P(\bar E_1)}, \qquad P(E_1 \mid E_2) = \frac{P(E_1) - \Delta}{P(E_1) - \Delta + P(\bar E_1) - \bar\Delta}.$$
The two expressions have the same structure; conditioning simply subtracts the excluded probability. One checks that
$$P(E_1 \mid E_2) > P(E_1) \iff \frac{\Delta}{\bar \Delta} < \frac{P(E_1)}{P(\bar E_1)},$$
i.e. $E_1$ becomes more likely exactly when it loses *proportionally less* probability than its complement.
:::

## The surprisal

Each event specifies the outcome to a larger or lesser degree, i.e. provides more or less information about it. Let us make this quantitative by constructing a function
$$I : \mathcal F \to \mathbb R_+$$
that assigns to each event the information it provides about the outcome.

### Requirements

Intuitively we should agree that:

1. Events with the same probability provide the same amount of information. Hence $I$ depends on $E$ only through its probability: $I(E) = f(P(E))$ for some unknown $f : [0,1] \to \mathbb R_+$.
2. More probable events (more outcomes, vaguer answers) provide *less* information: $f$ is **monotonically decreasing**. In particular $I(\Omega) = f(1)$ should be zero — the trivial answer tells us nothing.
3. **Additivity for independent events.** Independent events are complementary answers, so the information of the combined answer $E = E_1 \cap E_2$ should be the sum of the two pieces: $I(E_1 \cap E_2) = I(E_1) + I(E_2)$. Since $P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1)P(E_2)$, this means
$$f(x_1 x_2) = f(x_1) + f(x_2).$$

::: {.example name="Additivity in the two-coin space"}
We could first say "the first coin gave $h$" ($E_1 = \{hh, ht\}$) and then "the second coin gave $h$" ($E_2 = \{hh, th\}$). These events are independent, and together they say exactly "the outcome was $hh$" ($E_1 \cap E_2 = \{hh\}$). It is natural to demand that the information in the single answer $\{hh\}$ equal the sum of the information in the two partial answers.
:::

### The logarithm is forced

::: {.theorem name="Characterisation of the surprisal"}
A monotonically decreasing function $f : (0, 1] \to \mathbb R_+$ satisfying $f(x_1 x_2) = f(x_1) + f(x_2)$ must be of the form
$$f(x) = -\alpha \log x, \qquad \alpha > 0 .$$
:::

::: {.proof}
Set $x = e^y$ and $g(y) = f(e^y)$. The functional equation becomes
$$f(e^{y_1} e^{y_2}) = f(e^{y_1 + y_2}) = f(e^{y_1}) + f(e^{y_2}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(y_1 + y_2) = g(y_1) + g(y_2).$$
This is Cauchy's functional equation; its only monotone solutions are linear, $g(y) = \alpha' y$. Hence $f(e^y) = \alpha' y$, i.e. $f(x) = \alpha' \log x$. Monotonic decrease requires $\alpha' < 0$; write $\alpha' = -\alpha$ with $\alpha > 0$.
:::

::: {.definition name="Surprisal"}
The **surprisal** (or self-information) of an event $E$ is
$$I(E) = -\log_2 P(E) \quad \text{bits}.$$
For a random variable $X$ taking value $x$ we write $I(x) = -\log_2 p_X(x)$.
:::

The name reflects that low-probability events are more "surprising". It is worth stressing what the surprisal measures: **the amount of information we obtain when we learn that the outcome of a random process lies in a given subset $E$ of all possible outcomes.**

::: {.remark name="Units"}
The constant $\alpha$ is arbitrary and only fixes the unit. Choosing $\alpha$ is the same as choosing a base $\beta = e^{1/\alpha}$ for the logarithm:
$$-\alpha \log P = -\frac{\log P}{\log \beta} = -\log_\beta P .$$
With $\beta = 2$ we measure information in **bits** (binary digits); with $\beta = e$ in **nats**; with $\beta = 10$ in hartleys. Since $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$, $1\ \text{bit} \approx 0.693\ \text{nat}$. From now on $\log \equiv \log_2$ unless stated otherwise.

The bit has a simple interpretation: **it is the information revealed when we learn the outcome of a process with two equiprobable outcomes**, such as the toss of a fair coin.
:::

::: {.example name="Coin toss"}
For a fair coin each outcome carries $\log_2 2 = 1$ bit. For a biased coin with $p(H) = 0.99$, seeing heads carries only $-\log_2 0.99 \approx 0.014$ bits — the outcome was almost certain. Seeing tails carries $-\log_2 0.01 \approx 6.6$ bits — a much more informative event.
:::

::: {.example name="Surprisal in the two-coin space"}
$I(\{hh\}) = -\log_2(1/4) = 2$ bits; $I(\{hh, ht\}) = 1$ bit; $I(\{hh, ht, th\}) = -\log_2(3/4) \approx 0.415$ bits; $I(\Omega) = 0$. The more specific the answer, the more bits it carries; full specification of two coins costs two bits, one per coin.
:::

### Conditional surprisal: redundant and synergistic answers

What if we specify the outcome through answers that are *not* independent? If we already know that the outcome is in $E_1$, the natural information carried by $E_2$ is

$$I(E_2 \mid E_1) = -\log P(E_2 \mid E_1).$$

Since $P(E_1 \cap E_2) = P(E_1) P(E_2 \mid E_1)$, taking logarithms gives the **chain rule for surprisal**
$$I(E_1 \cap E_2) = I(E_1) + I(E_2 \mid E_1).$$

In general $I(E_2 \mid E_1)$ can be larger or smaller than $I(E_2)$. Correspondingly the information provided jointly by two non-independent events can be **less** (redundancy) or **more** (synergy) than the sum of their separate informations.

::: {.example name="Redundant answers"}
The event $E = \{hh\}$ can be obtained as the intersection of the non-independent events $E_1 = \{hh, ht, th\}$ ("at least one $h$"), $E_2 = \{hh, th\}$ ("heads in the second toss"), $E_3 = \{hh\}$. There is redundancy among these answers:
$$P(E_2 \mid E_1) = \tfrac23 > P(E_2) = \tfrac12, \qquad P(E_3 \mid E_2) = \tfrac12 > P(E_3) = \tfrac14 .$$
The separate surprisals are
$$I(E_1) = -\log\tfrac34 \approx 0.42, \quad I(E_2) = 1, \quad I(E_3) = 2,$$
so naïvely summing gives $3.42 > I(E) = 2$ bits: we have "overcounted" the information. Using conditional surprisals instead,
$$I(E) = I(E_1) + I(E_2 \mid E_1) + I(E_3 \mid E_2) = -\log\Bigl(\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12\Bigr) = -\log\tfrac14 = 2 \text{ bits}. \checkmark$$
:::

::: {.example name="Synergistic answers"}
Take $E = \{hh, ht\}$ as the intersection of $E_1 = \{hh, ht, th\}$ and $E_2 = \{hh, ht, tt\}$. Then
$$P(E) = \tfrac12 = \tfrac{8}{16} < P(E_1)P(E_2) = \tfrac{9}{16},$$
and therefore
$$I(E) = 1 > I(E_1) + I(E_2) = 2 \times 0.415 = 0.83 \text{ bits}.$$
Each answer alone only excludes one outcome, but together they exclude two: the combination is worth more than the parts.
:::

::: {.lemma name="Chain rule for surprisal"}
For any sequence of random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$,
$$I(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} I(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}),$$
where $I(x_i \mid \cdot) = -\log_2 p(x_i \mid \cdot)$ is the conditional surprisal.
:::

This follows from the chain rule of probability $p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1})$ and the additivity of the logarithm.

## Probability trees

Possible answers to "What was the outcome?" can be arranged in a **probability tree**:

- each node is a possible answer, i.e. an event $E \in \mathcal F$, carrying probability $P(E)$; the root is $\Omega$;
- each branching splits an answer $E$ into more detailed, mutually exclusive answers $E_1, \ldots, E_k$ with $\bigcup_k E_k = E$; the $k$-th edge carries the conditional probability $P(E_k \mid E)$;
- at each level, the nodes form a partition of $\Omega$.

Conceptually, **each branching is a sub-question** and each edge an answer to it. The probability of a node is the product of the conditional probabilities along the path from the root; correspondingly, since $I = -\log P$, **the information of a node is the sum of the informations along the edges** leading to it. The same probability space can be split in many different ways, giving different trees.

::: {.example name="Two trees for two coins"}
*Tree A* (one coin at a time):

```
Omega --1/2--> {hh,ht}  "first coin h?"  --1/2--> {hh}
  |                                     +-1/2--> {ht}
  +---1/2--> {th,tt}                    --1/2--> {th}
                                        +-1/2--> {tt}
```
Each edge carries 1 bit; each leaf is reached after two edges, i.e. 2 bits, consistent with $I(\{hh\}) = 2$.

*Tree B* (an unbalanced split): first ask "was it $tt$?",

```
Omega --1/4--> {tt}
  +---3/4--> {hh,ht,th}  --2/3--> {hh,ht}  --1/2--> {hh}
                         |                 +-1/2--> {ht}
                         +-1/3--> {th}
```
Now the leaf $\{hh\}$ is reached through edges carrying $-\log\tfrac34 - \log\tfrac23 - \log\tfrac12 = 2$ bits — the same total, distributed differently along the path. The leaf $\{tt\}$ is reached in one step, also with $-\log\tfrac14 = 2$ bits.
:::

## Information for a probability space

The surprisal $I(E)$ measures how much information event $E$ gives about the outcome. We now ask: **how much information is needed, on average, to completely specify the outcome?** This will be the information content of the probability space as a whole.

::: {.example name="Urn with three colours"}
An urn contains red, blue and green balls in proportions $50\%, 25\%, 25\%$. We draw one ball. To identify its colour we may first ask "is it red?" ($E_1 = \{r\}$ vs $\bar E_1 = \{b, g\}$, each answer worth 1 bit). If not red, we ask "is it blue?" — a second bit. Thus red is identified with 1 bit, blue and green with 2 bits. On average
$$\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 = 1.5 \text{ bits},$$
which is the *average number of yes/no questions* needed to identify the outcome.
:::

::: {.example name="Urn with five colours"}
Now red, blue, green, yellow, purple in proportions $50\%, 12.5\%, 12.5\%, 12.5\%, 12.5\%$. Ask "red?" (1 bit); if not, "blue or green?" (2nd bit); then "blue?" or "yellow?" (3rd bit). Red costs 1 question, every other colour 3. On average
$$\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 2 \text{ bits}.$$
Note that each question was designed to split the remaining probability *in half*, so every answer was worth exactly one bit.
:::

Formalising the argument: for each outcome $a \in \Omega$ of a discrete space there is an elementary event $\{a\}$ with information $-\log P(\{a\})$. The average information needed to specify the outcome is
$$H(S) = \langle I \rangle = -\sum_{a \in \Omega} P(\{a\}) \log P(\{a\}),$$
a single number for the whole space $S = (\Omega, \mathcal F, P)$. Introducing a random variable $X : \Omega \to \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}$ with $p_X(x_i) = P(\{a\})$ when $X(a) = x_i$ (in the urn, $r \mapsto x_1$, $b \mapsto x_2$, $g \mapsto x_3$):

::: {.definition name="Shannon entropy"}
The **Shannon entropy** of a discrete random variable $X$ with distribution $p_X$ is
$$H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal X} p_X(x)\,\log_2 p_X(x) = \mathbb E[I(X)] .$$
By convention $0\log 0 = 0$.
:::

The entropy is the *expected* number of bits carried by a random outcome — the average information needed to specify it. This operational reading will be made rigorous by the source-coding theorem in Chapter 3.

::: {.example name="Entropy of Bernoulli"}
For $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$, $H(X) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \equiv h_2(p)$. The **binary entropy function** $h_2$ is symmetric around $p = 1/2$, where it takes its maximum value $h_2(1/2) = 1$ bit. It vanishes at $p = 0$ or $p = 1$ — no information from a certain outcome. A few values worth remembering: $h_2(0.1) \approx 0.469$, $h_2(0.11) \approx 0.5$, $h_2(0.25) \approx 0.811$.
:::

::: {.example name="Four-outcome distribution (Cover and Thomas)"}
Let $X$ take values $a, b, c, d$ with probabilities $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18$. Then
$$H(X) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 1.75 \text{ bits}.$$
An efficient questioning strategy is "Is $X = a$?", then "Is $X = b$?", then "Is $X = c$?"; the expected number of questions is exactly $1.75$. We will see in Chapter 3 that no strategy can use fewer than $H(X)$ questions on average.
:::

::: {.example name="The game of sixty-three (MacKay)"}
An integer $x$ is chosen uniformly in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 63\}$. Ask: "is $x \geq 32$?", "is $x \bmod 32 \geq 16$?", "is $x \bmod 16 \geq 8$?", "is $x \bmod 8 \geq 4$?", "is $x \bmod 4 \geq 2$?", "is $x \bmod 2 = 1$?". Each question halves the set of candidates and therefore yields exactly one bit; after six questions $x$ is known, consistent with $H = \log_2 64 = 6$ bits. Writing the answers as $1$ = yes, $0$ = no gives precisely the binary expansion of $x$ — the bits of information *are* the binary digits.
:::

::: {.example name="Uniform distribution maximises entropy"}
For a variable $X$ taking $M$ values, the entropy is maximised when $p_X$ is uniform, in which case $H(X) = \log_2 M$. Non-uniform distributions give strictly less entropy — a preview of the general result proved in Chapter 2.
:::

## An axiomatic derivation of entropy

Had we not derived the surprisal first, we could have obtained Shannon's formula directly from requirements on $H$ itself, following an argument reported by Bialek. We look for a functional $H[X]$ measuring the information content of the distribution of $X$.

**Step 1: equiprobable outcomes.** If $p_X(x_i) = 1/N$ for all $i$, then $H$ should be a monotonically increasing function of the number of outcomes: $H[X] = g(N)$.

**Step 2: composition.** Let $N = k^m$. We can draw a tree with $m$ levels and $k$ equiprobable branches at each level, and map $X \mapsto (X_1, \ldots, X_m)$ where each $X_j$ is an independent variable with $k$ equally likely outcomes — each outcome of $X$ is identified by answering $m$ independent sub-questions. (Two coins: $k = 2$, $m = 2$.) The total information must be the sum of the parts:
$$g(k^m) = \sum_{j=1}^m H[X_j] = m\, g(k).$$

::: {.lemma}
The only monotonic function satisfying $g(k^m) = m\, g(k)$ for all integers $k, m$ is $g(x) = \alpha \log x$.
:::

::: {.proof}
Fix integers $k, \ell \geq 2$ and $n \geq 1$, and choose $m$ such that $k^m \leq \ell^n < k^{m+1}$, i.e. $m = \lfloor n \log \ell / \log k \rfloor$. (E.g. $k = 4$, $\ell = 3$, $n = 3$: $m = \lfloor 2.37 \rfloor = 2$.) Taking logarithms and dividing by $n \log k$:
$$\frac mn \leq \frac{\log \ell}{\log k} < \frac mn + \frac1n \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Bigl|\frac{\log \ell}{\log k} - \frac mn\Bigr| \leq \frac1n .$$
By monotonicity $g(k^m) \leq g(\ell^n) < g(k^{m+1})$, i.e. $m\, g(k) \leq n\, g(\ell) < (m+1) g(k)$; dividing by $n\, g(k)$,
$$\Bigl|\frac{g(\ell)}{g(k)} - \frac mn\Bigr| \leq \frac1n .$$
By the triangle inequality, $|g(\ell)/g(k) - \log \ell/\log k| \leq 2/n$ for every $n$. Letting $n \to \infty$ gives $g(\ell)/g(k) = \log \ell / \log k$ for all $k, \ell$, i.e. $g(x) = \alpha \log x$.
:::

Thus for $N$ equiprobable outcomes $H[X] = \log N$ (choosing $\alpha$ so that we work in bits).

**Step 3: non-uniform probabilities.** Suppose the probabilities $p_i$ are rational (any real can be approximated arbitrarily well). Write $p_i = r_i / s_i$ and let $M = \prod_l s_l$; then $p_i = k_i / M$ with $k_i = r_i \prod_{l \neq i} s_l \in \mathbb N$ and $\sum_i k_i = M$.

Imagine a variable $Y$ with $M$ equiprobable outcomes arranged into $N$ groups, group $i$ containing $k_i$ outcomes. Let $X$ be the group label (so $P(X = i) = k_i / M = p_i$) and $Z_i$ the position within group $i$ (uniform on $k_i$ values). Identifying $Y$ takes two sub-questions — first the group, then the element — so
$$H[Y] = H[X] + \sum_i p_i\, H[Z_i],$$
where the second term is averaged because the different branches are reached with different probabilities. Since $H[Y] = \log M$ and $H[Z_i] = \log k_i$,
$$\log M = H[X] + \sum_i p_i \log k_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H[X] = -\sum_i p_i \log\frac{k_i}{M} = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i . \qquad\blacksquare$$

::: {.example name="Step 3 in action"}
Take $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16)$. With $M = 6$: $k = (3, 2, 1)$. Then $H[Y] = \log 6 \approx 2.585$, and the within-group terms give $\tfrac12 \log 3 + \tfrac13 \log 2 + \tfrac16 \log 1 \approx 0.792 + 0.333 = 1.126$. Hence $H[X] \approx 2.585 - 1.126 = 1.459$ bits, which agrees with $-\tfrac12\log\tfrac12 - \tfrac13\log\tfrac13 - \tfrac16\log\tfrac16 \approx 0.5 + 0.528 + 0.431 = 1.459$. $\checkmark$
:::

The composition rule used in Step 3 is the **grouping property** of entropy, which we state in general below.

## Elementary properties

The entropy is well-behaved as a function of the distribution (proofs in Chapter 2).

::: {.proposition name="Basic properties of entropy"}
For any discrete random variable $X$ with distribution on $\mathcal X$:

1. $H(X) \geq 0$, with equality iff $X$ is deterministic.
2. $H(X) \leq \log_2 |\mathcal X|$, with equality iff $X$ is uniform.
3. $H$ is a concave function of $p_X$.
4. **Grouping property.** If two outcomes $x_1, x_2$ are merged into one with probability $p_1 + p_2$, then
$$H(X) = H(X_{merged}) + (p_1 + p_2)\,h_2\bigl(p_1/(p_1 + p_2)\bigr).$$
:::

::: {.corollary name="Entropy of a joint variable"}
For a pair $(X, Y)$ with joint distribution $p(x, y)$,
$$H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log_2 p(x, y) \leq H(X) + H(Y),$$
with equality iff $X, Y$ are independent (sub-additivity, proved in Chapter 2).
:::

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
A fair die is rolled. Compute (i) the surprisal of the event "the outcome is 6", (ii) the entropy of the rolling process, (iii) the surprisal of the event "the outcome is even".
:::

::: {.solution}
(i) $I(\{6\}) = -\log_2(1/6) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits.
(ii) $H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits (uniform on 6 outcomes).
(iii) $I(\{2, 4, 6\}) = -\log_2(3/6) = 1$ bit — exactly one bit of information (equivalent to a coin toss).
:::

::: {.exercise}
Show that for a Bernoulli$(p)$ variable the entropy is maximised at $p = 1/2$ by direct differentiation. What is the second derivative at the maximum?
:::

::: {.solution}
$H'(p) = -\log_2 p + \log_2(1 - p) = \log_2\bigl((1-p)/p\bigr)$. Setting $H' = 0$ gives $p = 1/2$. The second derivative is $H''(p) = -[1/(p\ln 2) + 1/((1 - p)\ln 2)]$, which at $p = 1/2$ equals $-4/\ln 2 \approx -5.77$. Negative $H''$ confirms concavity — a global maximum.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Verify the grouping property for a three-outcome variable with probabilities $(1/2, 1/4, 1/4)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Direct: $H = -\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 = 1.5$ bits. Grouping the last two into a merged outcome of probability $1/2$: $H_{merged} = h_2(1/2) = 1$ bit, plus $1/2 \cdot h_2(1/2) = 0.5$ bits, total $1.5$ bits. $\checkmark$
:::

::: {.exercise}
In the two-coin space, check that $E_1 = \{hh, ht\}$ ("first coin $h$") and $E_2 = \{ht, th\}$ ("coins differ") are independent, and compute $I(E_1)$, $I(E_2)$ and $I(E_1 \cap E_2)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$E_1 \cap E_2 = \{ht\}$, $P = 1/4 = P(E_1)P(E_2) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12$: independent. $I(E_1) = I(E_2) = 1$ bit and $I(\{ht\}) = 2 = I(E_1) + I(E_2)$ bits, as additivity requires.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Twelve balls look identical, but one is either heavier or lighter than the others. How much information is needed to identify the odd ball *and* whether it is heavy or light? A balance has three outcomes (left heavy, right heavy, balanced). What is the minimum number of weighings needed in principle?
:::

::: {.solution}
There are $12 \times 2 = 24$ equiprobable hypotheses, so $\log_2 24 \approx 4.58$ bits are needed. A weighing has three outcomes and can deliver at most $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits. Hence at least $\lceil 4.58 / 1.58 \rceil = 3$ weighings are needed — and three indeed suffice if each weighing is designed so that its three outcomes are as close to equiprobable as possible (MacKay §4.1). The lesson: *good experiments maximise the entropy of their outcome*.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
Shannon's definition is *operational* — it derives its meaning from the coding theorems of Chapter 3. Purely axiomatic derivations (like the one above) show that the formula is *unique*, but do not tell you *why* it is useful.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
When computing entropies by hand, remember the identity $\log_2 n = \log_2 e \cdot \ln n \approx 1.4427 \ln n$: it lets you convert between nats and bits without a calculator.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
The convention $0\log 0 = 0$ is genuine, not a hack: $p \log(1/p) \to 0$ as $p \to 0^+$. But if you compute $I(x)$ for an event that has empirical probability zero from a finite sample, the value is $+\infty$, and downstream computations will be nonsense. Always regularise.
:::
