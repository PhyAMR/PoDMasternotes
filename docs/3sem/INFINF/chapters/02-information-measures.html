3  Information-theoretic measures

Entropy is only the first in a family of information measures. This chapter first proves the basic properties of the entropy, then introduces the three other quantities we will use constantly — the Kullback–Leibler divergence, the conditional entropy and the mutual information — and develops the identities and inequalities that connect them. Every result here will be used in the coding theorems of Chapters 3–4 and, later, in variational inference (Chapter 10).

3.1 Properties of Shannon entropy

3.1.1 Range

Let \(X\) take values in an alphabet \(\mathcal A_X\) with \(|\mathcal A_X| = N\). Then \[0 \leq H[X] \leq \log N .\] The minimum is attained iff one outcome has probability one (\(\exists i: p_i = 1\)); the maximum is attained iff all outcomes are equiprobable (\(p_i = 1/N\) for all \(i\)).

Proof. Lower bound. \(0 \leq p_i \leq 1\) implies \(-p_i \log p_i \geq 0\), with equality only for \(p_i \in \{0, 1\}\); so \(H = 0\) iff a single outcome has probability one.

Upper bound. Maximise \(H\) under the constraint \(\sum_i p_i = 1\) with a Lagrange multiplier (natural logs for convenience): \[\mathcal L[\{p_i\}, \lambda] = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i + \lambda\Bigl(\sum_i p_i - 1\Bigr).\] Stationarity gives \[\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial p_i} = -1 - \ln p_i + \lambda = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; p_i = e^{\lambda - 1} \equiv \mu \;\;\forall i, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \lambda} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_i p_i = 1 .\] All \(p_i\) are equal, and normalisation gives \(\mu = 1/N\). Because \(H\) is concave (below), this stationary point is the global maximum, \(H = \log N\). A second proof, via the KL divergence, is given below.

3.1.2 Concavity

Recall that \(f\) is concave if \(f(\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2) \geq \lambda f(x_1) + (1 - \lambda) f(x_2)\) for all \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\) (the chord lies below the graph), and convex if the inequality is reversed (the chord lies above).

The Shannon entropy is a concave function of the distribution: for any two distributions \(p, q\) on the same alphabet and \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\), \[H(\lambda p + (1 - \lambda)q) \geq \lambda H(p) + (1 - \lambda)H(q),\] with equality (for \(0 < \lambda < 1\)) iff \(p = q\).

The mixture \(\lambda p + (1 - \lambda) q\) has a concrete meaning. Let \(X_1 \sim p\) and \(X_2 \sim q\), and let \(Z = X_1\) with probability \(\lambda\) and \(Z = X_2\) with probability \(1 - \lambda\). Then \(p_Z = \lambda p + (1 - \lambda) q\), and concavity states that mixing two distributions increases entropy: not knowing which source produced the symbol adds uncertainty. The proof is given after we introduce the KL divergence.

Let \(p = (1, 0)\) and \(q = (0, 1)\): both \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are deterministic, \(H(p) = H(q) = 0\). Their mixture is \((\lambda, 1 - \lambda)\), with entropy \(h_2(\lambda) > 0\) for every \(0 < \lambda < 1\). All the uncertainty of \(Z\) comes from the coin that selects the source.

3.2 Kullback–Leibler divergence

The KL divergence (or relative entropy) measures how different a distribution \(p\) is from another distribution \(q\).

For distributions \(p, q\) on a discrete alphabet \(\mathcal X\) (write \(p_i = p(x_i)\), \(q_i = q(x_i)\)), \[D_{KL}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i}.\] By convention \(0\log(0/q) = 0\) and \(p\log(p/0) = +\infty\) for \(p > 0\).

For any two distributions \(p, q\) on the same alphabet, \(D_{KL}(p \| q) \geq 0\), with equality iff \(p = q\).

The proof (below) uses Jensen’s inequality. Because of Gibbs’ inequality, \(D_{KL}\) can be read as a measure of discriminability between two distributions — a kind of “distance” on the space of distributions.

Remark. The KL divergence is not a metric: it is not symmetric (\(D(p \| q) \neq D(q \| p)\) in general) and it does not satisfy the triangle inequality. It does, however, satisfy \(D(p \| q) = 0 \iff p = q\), and it is jointly convex (below).

3.2.1 Interpretation: the cost of a wrong model

Suppose \(X\) takes value \(x_i\) with probability \(p_i\), but an observer (mistakenly) believes the probabilities are \(q_i\). When the outcome \(x_i\) is revealed, the observer assigns it surprisal \(-\log q_i\) — they think they have received \(-\log q_i\) bits. Since \(x_i\) occurs with probability \(p_i\), the average perceived information is the cross entropy \[H(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i .\] The cross entropy is always at least the true entropy, and the excess is exactly the KL divergence: \[D_{KL}(p \| q) = H(p, q) - H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i + \sum_i p_i \log p_i = \sum_i p_i (\log p_i - \log q_i) \geq 0 .\] The observer always overestimates the information obtained; the overestimate is \(D_{KL}(p \| q)\). In Chapter 3 this becomes concrete: \(D(p\|q)\) is the number of extra bits per symbol paid by a code designed for \(q\) when the source is really \(p\).

Let \(p = (1 - \epsilon, \epsilon)\) with \(\epsilon \ll 1\). The entropy is tiny, \[H(p) = -\epsilon\log\epsilon - (1 - \epsilon)\log(1 - \epsilon) \approx \epsilon\,(\log(1/\epsilon) + \log e) ,\] because the outcome is nearly always \(x_1\), which carries almost no information (\(-\log(1 - \epsilon) \approx \epsilon \log e\)). An observer who assumes \(q = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) will nearly always see \(x_1\) and believe they received one full bit: \[H(p, q) = -(1 - \epsilon)\log\tfrac12 - \epsilon\log\tfrac12 = 1 \gg H(p).\]

Conversely, let \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), so \(H(p) = 1\), but the observer believes \(q = (1 - \epsilon, \epsilon)\). Half of the time they see the “rare” outcome \(x_2\) and think they received \(-\log\epsilon \gg 1\) bits: \[H(p, q) = -\tfrac12 \log\bigl(\epsilon(1 - \epsilon)\bigr) \approx \tfrac12 \log\frac1\epsilon \gg 1 .\] Here \(D(p\|q) \approx \tfrac12\log(1/\epsilon) - 1\) diverges as \(\epsilon \to 0\), whereas in the previous example the divergence \(D(p\|q) = 1 - H(p) \to 1\) stays bounded: a vivid illustration of the asymmetry of KL. Assigning near-zero probability to something that actually happens is punished very severely.

For \(\text{Bern}(a)\) vs \(\text{Bern}(b)\), \[D(a \| b) = a \log \frac{a}{b} + (1 - a)\log\frac{1 - a}{1 - b}.\] When \(b = 1/2\) this reduces to \(1 - h_2(a)\), the “distance from uniform” of a biased coin. Numerically, \(D(0.5 \| 0.25) \approx 0.208\) bits but \(D(0.25 \| 0.5) \approx 0.189\) bits.

3.2.2 Entropy as distance from uniform

Let \(u_i = 1/N\) be the uniform distribution on the same alphabet. Then \[H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log\Bigl(\frac{p_i}{u_i} \cdot u_i\Bigr) = -D_{KL}(p \| u) - \sum_i p_i \log\frac1N = \log N - D_{KL}(p \| u).\] Combined with Gibbs’ inequality this gives a one-line proof of \(H(p) \leq \log N\), with equality iff \(p = u\). The entropy is the maximal entropy minus how far the distribution is from uniform.

3.2.3 Proofs

If \(f\) is convex and \(X\) is a random variable, \(\mathbb E[f(X)] \geq f(\mathbb E[X])\). If \(f\) is strictly convex, equality holds iff \(X\) is constant (almost surely). For concave \(f\) the inequality is reversed.

Proof. For a discrete \(X\) with values \(x_i\) and probabilities \(p_i\), peel off one term at a time. Define \(p_i^{(1)} = p_i/(1 - p_1)\) for \(i \neq 1\) (a normalised distribution). By convexity, \[f\Bigl(\sum_i p_i x_i\Bigr) = f\Bigl(p_1 x_1 + (1 - p_1)\sum_{i\neq 1} p_i^{(1)} x_i\Bigr) \leq p_1 f(x_1) + (1 - p_1)\, f\Bigl(\sum_{i \neq 1} p_i^{(1)} x_i\Bigr).\] Repeating the step on the last term with \(p_i^{(2)} = p_i/(1 - p_1 - p_2)\), and so on, after \(N - 1\) steps we arrive at \(f(\sum_i p_i x_i) \leq \sum_i p_i f(x_i)\). (Equivalently, a short induction on \(N\).)

Proof (Proof of Gibbs’ inequality). Let \(Z = q(X)/p(X)\) with \(X \sim p\), and apply Jensen to the convex function \(-\log\): \[D_{KL}(p\|q) = \sum_i p_i \Bigl(-\log\frac{q_i}{p_i}\Bigr) = \mathbb E[-\log Z] \geq -\log \mathbb E[Z] = -\log \sum_i p_i \frac{q_i}{p_i} = -\log \sum_i q_i = 0 .\] Since \(-\log\) is strictly convex, equality requires \(q_i/p_i\) constant, i.e. \(p = q\).

For non-negative \(a_i\) and positive \(b_i\), \[\sum_i a_i \log \frac{a_i}{b_i} \geq \Bigl(\sum_i a_i\Bigr)\log \frac{\sum_i a_i}{\sum_i b_i},\] with equality iff \(a_i / b_i\) is constant across \(i\).

Proof. \(t \mapsto t\log t\) is strictly convex. Let \(A = \sum a_i\), \(B = \sum b_i\), weights \(w_i = b_i/B\) and points \(t_i = a_i/b_i\). Jensen gives \(\sum_i w_i t_i \log t_i \geq (\sum_i w_i t_i)\log(\sum_i w_i t_i)\), i.e. \(\frac1B\sum_i a_i\log\frac{a_i}{b_i} \geq \frac AB\log\frac AB\). Multiply by \(B\).

The log-sum inequality is the workhorse behind almost every information-theoretic inequality that follows.

\(D(p \| q)\) is convex in the pair \((p, q)\): for \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\), \[D(\lambda p_1 + (1 - \lambda)p_2 \,\|\, \lambda q_1 + (1 - \lambda)q_2) \leq \lambda D(p_1 \| q_1) + (1 - \lambda)D(p_2 \| q_2).\] In words, mixing decreases discriminability.

Proof. For each \(i\), apply the log-sum inequality to the two-term sums \(a = (\lambda p_{1i}, (1-\lambda)p_{2i})\), \(b = (\lambda q_{1i}, (1 - \lambda) q_{2i})\): \[(\lambda p_{1i} + (1-\lambda)p_{2i})\log\frac{\lambda p_{1i} + (1-\lambda)p_{2i}}{\lambda q_{1i} + (1-\lambda)q_{2i}} \leq \lambda p_{1i}\log\frac{\lambda p_{1i}}{\lambda q_{1i}} + (1-\lambda)p_{2i}\log\frac{(1-\lambda)p_{2i}}{(1-\lambda)q_{2i}} .\] The factors \(\lambda\) and \(1 - \lambda\) cancel inside the logarithms; summing over \(i\) gives the claim.

Proof (Proof of concavity of entropy). Using \(H(p) = \log N - D(p\|u)\) and convexity of \(D\) in its first argument (take \(q_1 = q_2 = u\)): \[H(\lambda p + (1-\lambda) q) = \log N - D(\lambda p + (1-\lambda)q \,\|\, u) \geq \log N - \lambda D(p\|u) - (1-\lambda) D(q\|u) = \lambda H(p) + (1 - \lambda) H(q).\]

3.3 Joint entropy, conditional entropy, mutual information

Let \(X, Y\) take values \(\{x_i\}\), \(\{y_j\}\), with joint probabilities \(p_{ij} \equiv p_{X,Y}(x_i, y_j)\) and marginals \(p_i = \sum_j p_{ij}\), \(q_j = \sum_i p_{ij}\).

The joint entropy is the entropy of the joint distribution, \[H[X, Y] = -\sum_{i,j} p_{ij} \log p_{ij}.\]

If we learn that \(X = x_i\), then \(Y\) has the conditional distribution \(p_{Y|X}(y_j \mid x_i) = p_{ij}/p_i\), with entropy \[H[Y \mid x_i] = -\sum_j \frac{p_{ij}}{p_i}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i} .\]

The conditional entropy of \(Y\) given \(X\) is the average of \(H[Y \mid x_i]\) over the outcomes of \(X\): \[H[Y \mid X] = \sum_i p_i\, H[Y \mid x_i] = -\sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i}.\] It is the average uncertainty remaining in \(Y\) once \(X\) is known. Since each \(H[Y \mid x_i]\) is a genuine entropy, \(H[Y \mid X] \geq 0\).

\[H[X, Y] = H[X] + H[Y \mid X] = H[Y] + H[X \mid Y].\] More generally, \(H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} H(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})\).

Proof. Write \(p_{ij} = p_i \cdot (p_{ij}/p_i)\) inside the logarithm: \[-\sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log p_{ij} = -\sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log p_i - \sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i} = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i + H[Y \mid X].\] The second form follows by symmetry; the \(n\)-variable version by induction.

The interpretation is simple: the uncertainty about the pair is the uncertainty about \(X\) plus the uncertainty about \(Y\) once \(X\) is known. This generalises the chain rule for surprisal of Chapter 1 (take expectations of \(I(x, y) = I(x) + I(y \mid x)\)).

The mutual information between \(X\) and \(Y\) is \[I[X : Y] = H[X] + H[Y] - H[X, Y] = H[Y] - H[Y \mid X] = H[X] - H[X \mid Y].\]

Rearranging, \[H[X] = H[X \mid Y] + I[X : Y], \qquad H[Y] = H[Y \mid X] + I[X : Y].\] So \(I[X:Y]\) is the information that \(X\) provides about \(Y\), and vice versa: the information needed to specify \(X\) splits into the part supplied by knowing \(Y\) plus the residual \(H[X \mid Y]\).

\[I[X : Y] = D_{KL}(p_{ij} \,\|\, p_i q_j) = \sum_{i,j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{p_i q_j} \geq 0,\] with equality iff \(p_{ij} = p_i q_j\), i.e. iff \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.

Independent variables carry no information about each other; and mutual information measures how far the joint distribution is from the independent one. Similarly, the conditional entropy is itself a KL divergence: with \(u_j = 1/N_Y\), \[H[Y \mid X] = -\sum_{ij} p_{ij} \log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i} = \log N_Y - D_{KL}(p_{ij} \,\|\, p_i\, u_j).\]

  1. Conditioning reduces entropy (on average): \(H[X \mid Y] \leq H[X]\), with equality iff \(X \perp Y\).
  2. Sub-additivity: \(H[X, Y] \leq H[X] + H[Y]\), with equality iff \(X \perp Y\).
  3. Independence bound: \(H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \leq \sum_i H(X_i)\).

Let \((X, Y)\) have the joint distribution

\(p(x,y)\) \(x=1\) \(x=2\) \(x=3\) \(x=4\)
\(y=1\) 1/8 1/16 1/32 1/32
\(y=2\) 1/16 1/8 1/32 1/32
\(y=3\) 1/16 1/16 1/16 1/16
\(y=4\) 1/4 0 0 0

The marginals are \(p_X = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)\) and \(p_Y = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\), so \(H(X) = 7/4\) and \(H(Y) = 2\) bits. Conditioning on each row, \[H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac14\bigl[H(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18) + H(\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac18,\tfrac18) + H(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14) + H(1,0,0,0)\bigr] = \tfrac14\bigl[\tfrac74 + \tfrac74 + 2 + 0\bigr] = \tfrac{11}{8}.\] Then \(H(X, Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y) = 27/8\), \(H(Y \mid X) = 27/8 - 7/4 = 13/8\), and \(I(X;Y) = 7/4 - 11/8 = 3/8\) bits. Note \(H(X \mid Y) \neq H(Y \mid X)\). Note also that observing \(y = 3\) increases the uncertainty about \(X\) (\(H(X \mid Y = 3) = 2 > H(X)\)); only the average over \(y\) is guaranteed to decrease it.

Of three balls, two have equal weight and one is either heavier or lighter. Let \(X \in \{1^+, 1^-, 2^+, 2^-, 3^+, 3^-\}\), where \(n^\pm\) means “ball \(n\) is odd and heavier/lighter”, all equiprobable: \(H[X] = \log 6 = 1 + \log 3\) bits. Each weighing has three outcomes (\(g, l, e\) = left greater, lighter, equal) and yields at most \(\log 3\) bits, so at least two weighings are needed.

Weigh ball 1 against 2 (outcome \(Y_1\)), then ball 1 against 3 (outcome \(Y_2\)). This identifies \(X\):

\(Y_1\) \(Y_2\) \(X\)
\(e\) \(g\) \(3^-\)
\(e\) \(l\) \(3^+\)
\(g\) \(g\) \(1^+\)
\(g\) \(e\) \(2^-\)
\(l\) \(l\) \(1^-\)
\(l\) \(e\) \(2^+\)

\(Y_1\) has three equiprobable outcomes, so \(H[Y_1] = \log 3\). Given \(Y_1\), only two values of \(Y_2\) remain possible, each with probability \(1/2\) (e.g. \(P(Y_2 = e \mid Y_1 = e) = 0\)), hence \(H[Y_2 \mid Y_1] = 1\) and \(H[Y_1, Y_2] = 1 + \log 3 = H[X]\). Since \((Y_1, Y_2)\) determines \(X\), \(I[X : Y_1, Y_2] = H[X]\). Individually \(I[X : Y_1] = I[X : Y_2] = \log 3\), so \[I[X : Y_1, Y_2] = 1 + \log 3 < I[X : Y_1] + I[X : Y_2] = 2\log 3 :\] the two measurements are not independent and their information about \(X\) is partly redundant.

A binary symmetric channel with crossover probability \(f\) and uniform input has \(H(Y) = 1\) and \(H(Y \mid X) = h_2(f)\), so it carries \(I(X; Y) = 1 - h_2(f)\) bits per symbol. At \(f = 0\) (noise-free) it carries the full 1 bit; at \(f = 1/2\) (pure noise) zero. Chapter 4 shows this is the channel’s capacity.

3.4 Venn diagram of information

The quantities \(H(X), H(Y), H(X, Y)\), \(H(X \mid Y), H(Y \mid X), I(X; Y)\) obey the same set-theoretic identities as two overlapping sets: \(H(X, Y)\) is the union, \(I(X;Y)\) the intersection, \(H(X \mid Y)\) and \(H(Y \mid X)\) the two set differences. This is the device generations of textbooks use to remember the relations:

 <---------------- H(X,Y) ---------------->
 <----------- H(X) ---------->
 |   H(X|Y)    |   I(X;Y)    |   H(Y|X)   |
               <----------- H(Y) --------->

Remark. The Venn-diagram picture breaks for three or more random variables because the “interaction information” \(I(X; Y; Z) = I(X;Y) - I(X;Y \mid Z)\) can be negative. Example: \(X, Y\) independent fair bits and \(Z = X \oplus Y\). Then \(I(X;Y) = 0\) but \(I(X;Y \mid Z) = 1\) — knowing \(Z\), \(X\) determines \(Y\) — so \(I(X;Y;Z) = -1\). Don’t over-extend the analogy.

3.5 Conditional mutual information and the data-processing inequality

\(I(X; Y \mid Z) = H(X \mid Z) - H(X \mid Y, Z)\) — the information \(Y\) provides about \(X\) when \(Z\) is already known. It satisfies the chain rule \[I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z).\]

An operational classic: post-processing cannot increase information.

If \(X \to Y \to Z\) is a Markov chain (i.e. \(Z \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Y\)), then \[I(X; Y) \geq I(X; Z).\] Equality holds iff \(I(X; Y \mid Z) = 0\), i.e. \(Z\) preserves all the information \(Y\) had about \(X\).

Proof. Expand \(I(X; Y, Z)\) in two ways with the chain rule: \(I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Y) + I(X; Z \mid Y) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z)\). The Markov property makes \(I(X; Z \mid Y) = 0\), so \(I(X; Y) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z) \geq I(X; Z)\).

In particular, for any function \(g\), \(I(X; g(Y)) \leq I(X; Y)\): no processing of data can create information about the source that was not already there. This is the theoretical basis of “sufficient statistics”: \(T(Y)\) is sufficient for \(X\) exactly when equality holds.

3.6 Concavity of mutual information

For fixed conditional \(p(y \mid x)\), \(I(X; Y)\) is a concave function of \(p(x)\). For fixed marginal \(p(x)\), \(I(X; Y)\) is a convex function of \(p(y \mid x)\).

The first statement guarantees that maximising \(I(X;Y)\) over input distributions — the definition of channel capacity in Chapter 4 — is a concave maximisation problem with no spurious local maxima. The second underlies rate-distortion theory.

3.7 Exercises

Show that \(H(X, Y) \leq H(X) + H(Y)\) with equality iff \(X, Y\) are independent, using the log-sum inequality directly (not via mutual information).

Solution. Write \(H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y) = \sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log[p(x,y)/(p(x)p(y))]\). By the log-sum inequality with \(a_{xy} = p(x,y)\) and \(b_{xy} = p(x)p(y)\) (both summing to 1), \[\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} \geq 1 \cdot \log \tfrac{1}{1} = 0.\] So the difference is \(\geq 0\), with equality iff \(p(x,y)/[p(x)p(y)]\) is constant — necessarily equal to 1 — iff independence.

Compute \(I(X; Y)\) for a binary symmetric channel with error probability \(f\) when the input is not uniform, \(P(X = 1) = \pi\).

Solution. \(P(Y = 1) = \pi(1 - f) + (1 - \pi) f \equiv \rho\). \(H(Y) = h_2(\rho)\) and \(H(Y \mid X) = h_2(f)\) regardless of \(\pi\). So \(I(X; Y) = h_2(\pi(1-f) + (1-\pi)f) - h_2(f)\), which is maximal (\(= 1 - h_2(f)\)) at \(\pi = 1/2\) where \(\rho = 1/2\).

Prove the data-processing inequality for the specific case \(Z = g(Y)\) with \(g\) a deterministic function.

Solution. \(X \to Y \to g(Y)\) is automatically a Markov chain, since given \(Y\) the value of \(Z\) is fixed. Then \(I(X;Z) = H(X) - H(X \mid Z) \leq H(X) - H(X \mid Y, Z)\) (conditioning reduces entropy), and \(H(X \mid Y, Z) = H(X \mid Y)\) because \(Z\) is a function of \(Y\). Hence \(I(X;Z) \leq H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = I(X;Y)\).

Let \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\) and \(q = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)\). Compute \(H(p)\), \(H(p, q)\), \(D(p\|q)\) and \(D(q\|p)\), and verify \(H(p) = \log 3 - D(p \| q)\).

Solution. \(H(p) = 1.5\) bits. \(H(p, q) = \log 3 \approx 1.585\). \(D(p\|q) = H(p,q) - H(p) \approx 0.085\) bits, and indeed \(\log 3 - 0.085 = 1.5\) since \(q\) is uniform. \(D(q\|p) = \tfrac13[\log\tfrac{1/3}{1/2} + 2\log\tfrac{1/3}{1/4}] = \tfrac13[-0.585 + 2(0.415)] \approx 0.082\) bits \(\neq D(p\|q)\).

3.8 Callouts

The three interpretations of mutual information — as a KL divergence, as a reduction of entropy, and as an inclusion–exclusion of joint and marginal entropies — are all useful. Which one you invoke depends on what you want to prove.

When bounding an information quantity, try (i) Jensen’s inequality applied to \(-\log\); (ii) the log-sum inequality; (iii) the data-processing inequality. These three moves cover most of the proofs in Cover & Thomas.

The KL divergence \(D(p \| q)\) is asymmetric. In machine learning the direction matters: minimising \(D(p_{\text{data}} \| q_\theta)\) is maximum likelihood (“mass-covering”), whereas minimising \(D(q_\theta \| p)\) is what variational inference does (“mode-seeking”, Chapter 10).

### Range

::: {.proposition name="Range of the entropy"}
Let $X$ take values in an alphabet $\mathcal A_X$ with $|\mathcal A_X| = N$. Then
$$0 \leq H[X] \leq \log N .$$
The minimum is attained iff one outcome has probability one ($\exists i: p_i = 1$); the maximum is attained iff all outcomes are equiprobable ($p_i = 1/N$ for all $i$).
:::

::: {.proof}
*Lower bound.* $0 \leq p_i \leq 1$ implies $-p_i \log p_i \geq 0$, with equality only for $p_i \in \{0, 1\}$; so $H = 0$ iff a single outcome has probability one.

*Upper bound.* Maximise $H$ under the constraint $\sum_i p_i = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier (natural logs for convenience):
$$\mathcal L[\{p_i\}, \lambda] = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i + \lambda\Bigl(\sum_i p_i - 1\Bigr).$$
Stationarity gives
$$\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial p_i} = -1 - \ln p_i + \lambda = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; p_i = e^{\lambda - 1} \equiv \mu \;\;\forall i, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \lambda} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_i p_i = 1 .$$
All $p_i$ are equal, and normalisation gives $\mu = 1/N$. Because $H$ is concave (below), this stationary point is the global maximum, $H = \log N$. A second proof, via the KL divergence, is given below.
:::

### Concavity

Recall that $f$ is **concave** if $f(\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2) \geq \lambda f(x_1) + (1 - \lambda) f(x_2)$ for all $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ (the chord lies below the graph), and **convex** if the inequality is reversed (the chord lies above).

::: {.proposition name="Concavity of entropy"}
The Shannon entropy is a concave function of the distribution: for any two distributions $p, q$ on the same alphabet and $\lambda \in [0, 1]$,
$$H(\lambda p + (1 - \lambda)q) \geq \lambda H(p) + (1 - \lambda)H(q),$$
with equality (for $0 < \lambda < 1$) iff $p = q$.
:::

The mixture $\lambda p + (1 - \lambda) q$ has a concrete meaning. Let $X_1 \sim p$ and $X_2 \sim q$, and let $Z = X_1$ with probability $\lambda$ and $Z = X_2$ with probability $1 - \lambda$. Then $p_Z = \lambda p + (1 - \lambda) q$, and concavity states that **mixing two distributions increases entropy**: not knowing *which* source produced the symbol adds uncertainty. The proof is given after we introduce the KL divergence.

::: {.example name="Mixing deterministic variables"}
Let $p = (1, 0)$ and $q = (0, 1)$: both $X_1$ and $X_2$ are deterministic, $H(p) = H(q) = 0$. Their mixture is $(\lambda, 1 - \lambda)$, with entropy $h_2(\lambda) > 0$ for every $0 < \lambda < 1$. All the uncertainty of $Z$ comes from the coin that selects the source.
:::

## Kullback–Leibler divergence

The KL divergence (or **relative entropy**) measures how *different* a distribution $p$ is from another distribution $q$.

::: {.definition name="Kullback–Leibler divergence"}
For distributions $p, q$ on a discrete alphabet $\mathcal X$ (write $p_i = p(x_i)$, $q_i = q(x_i)$),
$$D_{KL}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i}.$$
By convention $0\log(0/q) = 0$ and $p\log(p/0) = +\infty$ for $p > 0$.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Gibbs' inequality"}
For any two distributions $p, q$ on the same alphabet, $D_{KL}(p \| q) \geq 0$, with equality iff $p = q$.
:::

The proof (below) uses Jensen's inequality. Because of Gibbs' inequality, $D_{KL}$ can be read as a measure of **discriminability** between two distributions — a kind of "distance" on the space of distributions.

::: {.remark}
The KL divergence is *not* a metric: it is not symmetric ($D(p \| q) \neq D(q \| p)$ in general) and it does not satisfy the triangle inequality. It does, however, satisfy $D(p \| q) = 0 \iff p = q$, and it is jointly convex (below).
:::

### Interpretation: the cost of a wrong model

Suppose $X$ takes value $x_i$ with probability $p_i$, but an observer (mistakenly) believes the probabilities are $q_i$. When the outcome $x_i$ is revealed, the observer assigns it surprisal $-\log q_i$ — they think they have received $-\log q_i$ bits. Since $x_i$ occurs with probability $p_i$, the average perceived information is the **cross entropy**
$$H(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i .$$
The cross entropy is always at least the true entropy, and the excess is exactly the KL divergence:
$$D_{KL}(p \| q) = H(p, q) - H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i + \sum_i p_i \log p_i = \sum_i p_i (\log p_i - \log q_i) \geq 0 .$$
**The observer always overestimates the information obtained; the overestimate is $D_{KL}(p \| q)$.** In Chapter 3 this becomes concrete: $D(p\|q)$ is the number of *extra bits per symbol* paid by a code designed for $q$ when the source is really $p$.

::: {.example name="Wrongly assuming a fair coin"}
Let $p = (1 - \epsilon, \epsilon)$ with $\epsilon \ll 1$. The entropy is tiny,
$$H(p) = -\epsilon\log\epsilon - (1 - \epsilon)\log(1 - \epsilon) \approx \epsilon\,(\log(1/\epsilon) + \log e) ,$$
because the outcome is nearly always $x_1$, which carries almost no information ($-\log(1 - \epsilon) \approx \epsilon \log e$). An observer who assumes $q = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ will nearly always see $x_1$ and believe they received one full bit:
$$H(p, q) = -(1 - \epsilon)\log\tfrac12 - \epsilon\log\tfrac12 = 1 \gg H(p).$$
:::

::: {.example name="Wrongly assuming a biased coin"}
Conversely, let $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, so $H(p) = 1$, but the observer believes $q = (1 - \epsilon, \epsilon)$. Half of the time they see the "rare" outcome $x_2$ and think they received $-\log\epsilon \gg 1$ bits:
$$H(p, q) = -\tfrac12 \log\bigl(\epsilon(1 - \epsilon)\bigr) \approx \tfrac12 \log\frac1\epsilon \gg 1 .$$
Here $D(p\|q) \approx \tfrac12\log(1/\epsilon) - 1$ diverges as $\epsilon \to 0$, whereas in the previous example the divergence $D(p\|q) = 1 - H(p) \to 1$ stays bounded: a vivid illustration of the asymmetry of KL. Assigning near-zero probability to something that actually happens is punished very severely.
:::

::: {.example name="KL of Bernoullis"}
For $\text{Bern}(a)$ vs $\text{Bern}(b)$,
$$D(a \| b) = a \log \frac{a}{b} + (1 - a)\log\frac{1 - a}{1 - b}.$$
When $b = 1/2$ this reduces to $1 - h_2(a)$, the "distance from uniform" of a biased coin. Numerically, $D(0.5 \| 0.25) \approx 0.208$ bits but $D(0.25 \| 0.5) \approx 0.189$ bits.
:::

### Entropy as distance from uniform

Let $u_i = 1/N$ be the uniform distribution on the same alphabet. Then
$$H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log\Bigl(\frac{p_i}{u_i} \cdot u_i\Bigr) = -D_{KL}(p \| u) - \sum_i p_i \log\frac1N = \log N - D_{KL}(p \| u).$$
Combined with Gibbs' inequality this gives a one-line proof of $H(p) \leq \log N$, with equality iff $p = u$. **The entropy is the maximal entropy minus how far the distribution is from uniform.**

### Proofs

::: {.lemma name="Jensen's inequality"}
If $f$ is convex and $X$ is a random variable, $\mathbb E[f(X)] \geq f(\mathbb E[X])$. If $f$ is strictly convex, equality holds iff $X$ is constant (almost surely). For concave $f$ the inequality is reversed.
:::

::: {.proof}
For a discrete $X$ with values $x_i$ and probabilities $p_i$, peel off one term at a time. Define $p_i^{(1)} = p_i/(1 - p_1)$ for $i \neq 1$ (a normalised distribution). By convexity,
$$f\Bigl(\sum_i p_i x_i\Bigr) = f\Bigl(p_1 x_1 + (1 - p_1)\sum_{i\neq 1} p_i^{(1)} x_i\Bigr) \leq p_1 f(x_1) + (1 - p_1)\, f\Bigl(\sum_{i \neq 1} p_i^{(1)} x_i\Bigr).$$
Repeating the step on the last term with $p_i^{(2)} = p_i/(1 - p_1 - p_2)$, and so on, after $N - 1$ steps we arrive at $f(\sum_i p_i x_i) \leq \sum_i p_i f(x_i)$. (Equivalently, a short induction on $N$.)
:::

::: {.proof name="Proof of Gibbs' inequality"}
Let $Z = q(X)/p(X)$ with $X \sim p$, and apply Jensen to the convex function $-\log$:
$$D_{KL}(p\|q) = \sum_i p_i \Bigl(-\log\frac{q_i}{p_i}\Bigr) = \mathbb E[-\log Z] \geq -\log \mathbb E[Z] = -\log \sum_i p_i \frac{q_i}{p_i} = -\log \sum_i q_i = 0 .$$
Since $-\log$ is strictly convex, equality requires $q_i/p_i$ constant, i.e. $p = q$.
:::

::: {.lemma name="Log-sum inequality"}
For non-negative $a_i$ and positive $b_i$,
$$\sum_i a_i \log \frac{a_i}{b_i} \geq \Bigl(\sum_i a_i\Bigr)\log \frac{\sum_i a_i}{\sum_i b_i},$$
with equality iff $a_i / b_i$ is constant across $i$.
:::

::: {.proof}
$t \mapsto t\log t$ is strictly convex. Let $A = \sum a_i$, $B = \sum b_i$, weights $w_i = b_i/B$ and points $t_i = a_i/b_i$. Jensen gives $\sum_i w_i t_i \log t_i \geq (\sum_i w_i t_i)\log(\sum_i w_i t_i)$, i.e. $\frac1B\sum_i a_i\log\frac{a_i}{b_i} \geq \frac AB\log\frac AB$. Multiply by $B$.
:::

The log-sum inequality is the workhorse behind almost every information-theoretic inequality that follows.

::: {.proposition name="Joint convexity of KL"}
$D(p \| q)$ is convex in the pair $(p, q)$: for $\lambda \in [0, 1]$,
$$D(\lambda p_1 + (1 - \lambda)p_2 \,\|\, \lambda q_1 + (1 - \lambda)q_2) \leq \lambda D(p_1 \| q_1) + (1 - \lambda)D(p_2 \| q_2).$$
In words, **mixing decreases discriminability**.
:::

::: {.proof}
For each $i$, apply the log-sum inequality to the two-term sums $a = (\lambda p_{1i}, (1-\lambda)p_{2i})$, $b = (\lambda q_{1i}, (1 - \lambda) q_{2i})$:
$$(\lambda p_{1i} + (1-\lambda)p_{2i})\log\frac{\lambda p_{1i} + (1-\lambda)p_{2i}}{\lambda q_{1i} + (1-\lambda)q_{2i}} \leq \lambda p_{1i}\log\frac{\lambda p_{1i}}{\lambda q_{1i}} + (1-\lambda)p_{2i}\log\frac{(1-\lambda)p_{2i}}{(1-\lambda)q_{2i}} .$$
The factors $\lambda$ and $1 - \lambda$ cancel inside the logarithms; summing over $i$ gives the claim.
:::

::: {.proof name="Proof of concavity of entropy"}
Using $H(p) = \log N - D(p\|u)$ and convexity of $D$ in its first argument (take $q_1 = q_2 = u$):
$$H(\lambda p + (1-\lambda) q) = \log N - D(\lambda p + (1-\lambda)q \,\|\, u) \geq \log N - \lambda D(p\|u) - (1-\lambda) D(q\|u) = \lambda H(p) + (1 - \lambda) H(q).$$
:::

## Joint entropy, conditional entropy, mutual information

Let $X, Y$ take values $\{x_i\}$, $\{y_j\}$, with joint probabilities $p_{ij} \equiv p_{X,Y}(x_i, y_j)$ and marginals $p_i = \sum_j p_{ij}$, $q_j = \sum_i p_{ij}$.

::: {.definition name="Joint entropy"}
The **joint entropy** is the entropy of the joint distribution,
$$H[X, Y] = -\sum_{i,j} p_{ij} \log p_{ij}.$$
:::

If we learn that $X = x_i$, then $Y$ has the conditional distribution $p_{Y|X}(y_j \mid x_i) = p_{ij}/p_i$, with entropy
$$H[Y \mid x_i] = -\sum_j \frac{p_{ij}}{p_i}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i} .$$

::: {.definition name="Conditional entropy"}
The **conditional entropy** of $Y$ given $X$ is the average of $H[Y \mid x_i]$ over the outcomes of $X$:
$$H[Y \mid X] = \sum_i p_i\, H[Y \mid x_i] = -\sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i}.$$
It is the average uncertainty remaining in $Y$ once $X$ is known. Since each $H[Y \mid x_i]$ is a genuine entropy, $H[Y \mid X] \geq 0$.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Chain rule for entropy"}
$$H[X, Y] = H[X] + H[Y \mid X] = H[Y] + H[X \mid Y].$$
More generally, $H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} H(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})$.
:::

::: {.proof}
Write $p_{ij} = p_i \cdot (p_{ij}/p_i)$ inside the logarithm:
$$-\sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log p_{ij} = -\sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log p_i - \sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i} = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i + H[Y \mid X].$$
The second form follows by symmetry; the $n$-variable version by induction.
:::

The interpretation is simple: **the uncertainty about the pair is the uncertainty about $X$ plus the uncertainty about $Y$ once $X$ is known.** This generalises the chain rule for surprisal of Chapter 1 (take expectations of $I(x, y) = I(x) + I(y \mid x)$).

::: {.definition name="Mutual information"}
The **mutual information** between $X$ and $Y$ is
$$I[X : Y] = H[X] + H[Y] - H[X, Y] = H[Y] - H[Y \mid X] = H[X] - H[X \mid Y].$$
:::

Rearranging,
$$H[X] = H[X \mid Y] + I[X : Y], \qquad H[Y] = H[Y \mid X] + I[X : Y].$$
So $I[X:Y]$ is **the information that $X$ provides about $Y$, and vice versa**: the information needed to specify $X$ splits into the part supplied by knowing $Y$ plus the residual $H[X \mid Y]$.

::: {.proposition name="Mutual information as a KL divergence"}
$$I[X : Y] = D_{KL}(p_{ij} \,\|\, p_i q_j) = \sum_{i,j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{p_i q_j} \geq 0,$$
with equality iff $p_{ij} = p_i q_j$, i.e. iff $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
:::

Independent variables carry no information about each other; and mutual information measures *how far* the joint distribution is from the independent one. Similarly, the conditional entropy is itself a KL divergence: with $u_j = 1/N_Y$,
$$H[Y \mid X] = -\sum_{ij} p_{ij} \log\frac{p_{ij}}{p_i} = \log N_Y - D_{KL}(p_{ij} \,\|\, p_i\, u_j).$$

::: {.corollary name="Consequences"}
1. **Conditioning reduces entropy (on average):** $H[X \mid Y] \leq H[X]$, with equality iff $X \perp Y$.
2. **Sub-additivity:** $H[X, Y] \leq H[X] + H[Y]$, with equality iff $X \perp Y$.
3. **Independence bound:** $H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \leq \sum_i H(X_i)$.
:::

::: {.example name="A worked joint distribution (Cover and Thomas Ex. 2.2.1)"}
Let $(X, Y)$ have the joint distribution

| $p(x,y)$ | $x=1$ | $x=2$ | $x=3$ | $x=4$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $y=1$ | 1/8 | 1/16 | 1/32 | 1/32 |
| $y=2$ | 1/16 | 1/8 | 1/32 | 1/32 |
| $y=3$ | 1/16 | 1/16 | 1/16 | 1/16 |
| $y=4$ | 1/4 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The marginals are $p_X = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)$ and $p_Y = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$, so $H(X) = 7/4$ and $H(Y) = 2$ bits. Conditioning on each row,
$$H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac14\bigl[H(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18) + H(\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac18,\tfrac18) + H(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14) + H(1,0,0,0)\bigr] = \tfrac14\bigl[\tfrac74 + \tfrac74 + 2 + 0\bigr] = \tfrac{11}{8}.$$
Then $H(X, Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y) = 27/8$, $H(Y \mid X) = 27/8 - 7/4 = 13/8$, and $I(X;Y) = 7/4 - 11/8 = 3/8$ bits. Note $H(X \mid Y) \neq H(Y \mid X)$. Note also that observing $y = 3$ *increases* the uncertainty about $X$ ($H(X \mid Y = 3) = 2 > H(X)$); only the *average* over $y$ is guaranteed to decrease it.
:::

::: {.example name="Three balls and a balance (MacKay)"}
Of three balls, two have equal weight and one is either heavier or lighter. Let $X \in \{1^+, 1^-, 2^+, 2^-, 3^+, 3^-\}$, where $n^\pm$ means "ball $n$ is odd and heavier/lighter", all equiprobable: $H[X] = \log 6 = 1 + \log 3$ bits. Each weighing has three outcomes ($g, l, e$ = left greater, lighter, equal) and yields at most $\log 3$ bits, so at least two weighings are needed.

Weigh ball 1 against 2 (outcome $Y_1$), then ball 1 against 3 (outcome $Y_2$). This identifies $X$:

| $Y_1$ | $Y_2$ | $X$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $e$ | $g$ | $3^-$ |
| $e$ | $l$ | $3^+$ |
| $g$ | $g$ | $1^+$ |
| $g$ | $e$ | $2^-$ |
| $l$ | $l$ | $1^-$ |
| $l$ | $e$ | $2^+$ |

$Y_1$ has three equiprobable outcomes, so $H[Y_1] = \log 3$. Given $Y_1$, only two values of $Y_2$ remain possible, each with probability $1/2$ (e.g. $P(Y_2 = e \mid Y_1 = e) = 0$), hence $H[Y_2 \mid Y_1] = 1$ and $H[Y_1, Y_2] = 1 + \log 3 = H[X]$. Since $(Y_1, Y_2)$ determines $X$, $I[X : Y_1, Y_2] = H[X]$. Individually $I[X : Y_1] = I[X : Y_2] = \log 3$, so
$$I[X : Y_1, Y_2] = 1 + \log 3 < I[X : Y_1] + I[X : Y_2] = 2\log 3 :$$
the two measurements are not independent and their information about $X$ is partly redundant.
:::

::: {.example name="Bit conveyed by a noisy channel"}
A binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $f$ and uniform input has $H(Y) = 1$ and $H(Y \mid X) = h_2(f)$, so it carries $I(X; Y) = 1 - h_2(f)$ bits per symbol. At $f = 0$ (noise-free) it carries the full 1 bit; at $f = 1/2$ (pure noise) zero. Chapter 4 shows this is the channel's capacity.
:::

## Venn diagram of information

The quantities $H(X), H(Y), H(X, Y)$, $H(X \mid Y), H(Y \mid X), I(X; Y)$ obey the same set-theoretic identities as two overlapping sets: $H(X, Y)$ is the union, $I(X;Y)$ the intersection, $H(X \mid Y)$ and $H(Y \mid X)$ the two set differences. This is the device generations of textbooks use to remember the relations:

```
 <---------------- H(X,Y) ---------------->
 <----------- H(X) ---------->
 |   H(X|Y)    |   I(X;Y)    |   H(Y|X)   |
               <----------- H(Y) --------->
```

::: {.remark}
The Venn-diagram picture *breaks* for three or more random variables because the "interaction information" $I(X; Y; Z) = I(X;Y) - I(X;Y \mid Z)$ can be negative. Example: $X, Y$ independent fair bits and $Z = X \oplus Y$. Then $I(X;Y) = 0$ but $I(X;Y \mid Z) = 1$ — knowing $Z$, $X$ determines $Y$ — so $I(X;Y;Z) = -1$. Don't over-extend the analogy.
:::

## Conditional mutual information and the data-processing inequality

::: {.definition name="Conditional mutual information"}
$I(X; Y \mid Z) = H(X \mid Z) - H(X \mid Y, Z)$ — the information $Y$ provides about $X$ when $Z$ is already known. It satisfies the chain rule
$$I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z).$$
:::

An operational classic: post-processing cannot increase information.

::: {.theorem name="Data-processing inequality"}
If $X \to Y \to Z$ is a Markov chain (i.e. $Z \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Y$), then
$$I(X; Y) \geq I(X; Z).$$
Equality holds iff $I(X; Y \mid Z) = 0$, i.e. $Z$ preserves all the information $Y$ had about $X$.
:::

::: {.proof}
Expand $I(X; Y, Z)$ in two ways with the chain rule: $I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Y) + I(X; Z \mid Y) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z)$. The Markov property makes $I(X; Z \mid Y) = 0$, so $I(X; Y) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z) \geq I(X; Z)$.
:::

In particular, for any function $g$, $I(X; g(Y)) \leq I(X; Y)$: **no processing of data can create information about the source that was not already there.** This is the theoretical basis of "sufficient statistics": $T(Y)$ is sufficient for $X$ exactly when equality holds.

## Concavity of mutual information

::: {.proposition name="Concavity/convexity of mutual information"}
For fixed conditional $p(y \mid x)$, $I(X; Y)$ is a concave function of $p(x)$. For fixed marginal $p(x)$, $I(X; Y)$ is a convex function of $p(y \mid x)$.
:::

The first statement guarantees that maximising $I(X;Y)$ over input distributions — the definition of channel capacity in Chapter 4 — is a concave maximisation problem with no spurious local maxima. The second underlies rate-distortion theory.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Show that $H(X, Y) \leq H(X) + H(Y)$ with equality iff $X, Y$ are independent, using the log-sum inequality directly (not via mutual information).
:::

::: {.solution}
Write $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y) = \sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log[p(x,y)/(p(x)p(y))]$. By the log-sum inequality with $a_{xy} = p(x,y)$ and $b_{xy} = p(x)p(y)$ (both summing to 1),
$$\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} \geq 1 \cdot \log \tfrac{1}{1} = 0.$$
So the difference is $\geq 0$, with equality iff $p(x,y)/[p(x)p(y)]$ is constant — necessarily equal to 1 — iff independence.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Compute $I(X; Y)$ for a binary symmetric channel with error probability $f$ when the input is *not* uniform, $P(X = 1) = \pi$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$P(Y = 1) = \pi(1 - f) + (1 - \pi) f \equiv \rho$. $H(Y) = h_2(\rho)$ and $H(Y \mid X) = h_2(f)$ regardless of $\pi$. So $I(X; Y) = h_2(\pi(1-f) + (1-\pi)f) - h_2(f)$, which is maximal ($= 1 - h_2(f)$) at $\pi = 1/2$ where $\rho = 1/2$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Prove the data-processing inequality for the specific case $Z = g(Y)$ with $g$ a deterministic function.
:::

::: {.solution}
$X \to Y \to g(Y)$ is automatically a Markov chain, since given $Y$ the value of $Z$ is fixed. Then $I(X;Z) = H(X) - H(X \mid Z) \leq H(X) - H(X \mid Y, Z)$ (conditioning reduces entropy), and $H(X \mid Y, Z) = H(X \mid Y)$ because $Z$ is a function of $Y$. Hence $I(X;Z) \leq H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = I(X;Y)$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Let $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$ and $q = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$. Compute $H(p)$, $H(p, q)$, $D(p\|q)$ and $D(q\|p)$, and verify $H(p) = \log 3 - D(p \| q)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$H(p) = 1.5$ bits. $H(p, q) = \log 3 \approx 1.585$. $D(p\|q) = H(p,q) - H(p) \approx 0.085$ bits, and indeed $\log 3 - 0.085 = 1.5$ since $q$ is uniform. $D(q\|p) = \tfrac13[\log\tfrac{1/3}{1/2} + 2\log\tfrac{1/3}{1/4}] = \tfrac13[-0.585 + 2(0.415)] \approx 0.082$ bits $\neq D(p\|q)$.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
The three interpretations of mutual information — as a KL divergence, as a reduction of entropy, and as an inclusion–exclusion of joint and marginal entropies — are all useful. Which one you invoke depends on what you want to prove.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
When bounding an information quantity, try (i) Jensen's inequality applied to $-\log$; (ii) the log-sum inequality; (iii) the data-processing inequality. These three moves cover most of the proofs in Cover & Thomas.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
The KL divergence $D(p \| q)$ is *asymmetric*. In machine learning the direction matters: minimising $D(p_{\text{data}} \| q_\theta)$ is maximum likelihood ("mass-covering"), whereas minimising $D(q_\theta \| p)$ is what variational inference does ("mode-seeking", Chapter 10).
:::
