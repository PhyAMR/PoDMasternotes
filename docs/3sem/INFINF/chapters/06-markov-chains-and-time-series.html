7  Markov chains and information in time series

The information framework of the previous chapters treated symbols as i.i.d. Real data are almost never i.i.d.: language has grammar, DNA has motifs, prices have trends. To measure the information content of a sequence with memory we need to consider stochastic processes — Markov chains are the simplest interesting case.

7.1 Markov chains

A discrete-time Markov chain on state space \(\mathcal S\) is a sequence \(\{X_t\}_{t \geq 0}\) such that \[P(X_{t+1} = s' \mid X_t = s, X_{t-1}, \ldots, X_0) = P(X_{t+1} = s' \mid X_t = s) = T(s, s'),\] where \(T\) is the transition matrix. The distribution at time \(t\) evolves as \(\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t\,T\).

A distribution \(\pi^*\) is stationary for \(T\) if \(\pi^* = \pi^*\,T\). If additionally \[\pi^*(s)\,T(s, s') = \pi^*(s')\,T(s', s) \quad \forall s, s',\] the chain satisfies detailed balance and is called reversible.

An irreducible, aperiodic Markov chain on a finite state space has a unique stationary distribution \(\pi^*\), and \(\pi_t \to \pi^*\) geometrically as \(t \to \infty\), from any initial distribution.

7.2 Entropy rate

For a stochastic process the entropy of a single symbol undersells the information: correlations between successive symbols mean each new symbol conveys less information than the marginal entropy suggests.

The entropy rate of a stationary process \(\{X_t\}\) is \[H_\infty(X) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} H(X_n \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}),\] whenever the limit exists. For a Markov chain the entropy rate simplifies to \[H_\infty(X) = H(X_2 \mid X_1) = \sum_s \pi^*(s)\,H(T(s, \cdot)).\]

Shannon’s own experiments (1951) estimated the entropy rate of English text at \(\sim 1{-}1.5\) bits per letter — much lower than the naive \(\log_2 26 \approx 4.7\) bits/letter — because of strong statistical dependencies between letters, words and grammar. This is exactly the compression ratio (~5–6×) that modern text compressors achieve.

Remark. The entropy rate is the asymptotic compression bound: a universal code can compress a long ergodic sequence at rate arbitrarily close to \(H_\infty\), but no less.

7.3 Entropy production in Markov chains

Physicists care about a related quantity: the entropy produced by a non-equilibrium chain per step.

For a Markov chain with transition matrix \(T\) and stationary distribution \(\pi^*\), the entropy production rate is \[\sigma = \sum_{s, s'}\pi^*(s)\,T(s, s')\,\log\frac{T(s, s')}{T(s', s)}.\] Equivalently, \(\sigma = \sum_{s, s'}\pi^*(s)T(s, s')\log\frac{\pi^*(s)T(s, s')}{\pi^*(s')T(s', s)}\): the KL divergence between forward and reverse trajectory distributions.

\(\sigma \geq 0\), with equality iff detailed balance holds. Non-zero entropy production is the information-theoretic signature of non-equilibrium (breaking of time-reversal symmetry).

7.4 Hidden Markov models

Often the state of interest is not directly observable: a hidden state \(S_t\) emits an observed symbol \(Y_t\) through an emission distribution \(p(y \mid s)\). This is a hidden Markov model (HMM).

An HMM is a pair \((\{S_t\}, \{Y_t\})\) where \(\{S_t\}\) is a Markov chain on \(\mathcal S\) and each \(Y_t\) is emitted independently given \(S_t\) from a distribution \(p(y \mid s)\).

Standard HMM problems — inference of \(S_t\) from \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_T\) (forward-backward, Viterbi) and learning \(T\) and emission parameters (Baum–Welch, i.e. EM) — are all instances of general inference algorithms on trees or chains, and their information-theoretic complexity is well-studied.

Gene-finding algorithms model the DNA as an HMM with hidden states \(S_t \in \{\text{exon, intron, intergenic, \ldots}\}\) and observed nucleotides \(Y_t \in \{A, C, G, T\}\). Viterbi decoding assigns the most likely state sequence — a genomic annotation.

7.5 Spectrum of a Markov chain

The rate at which a Markov chain approaches stationarity is set by the second-largest eigenvalue of its transition matrix:

For a reversible chain with eigenvalues \(1 = \lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \geq \ldots \geq \lambda_{|\mathcal S|}\), the total-variation distance to stationarity satisfies \[\|\pi_t - \pi^*\|_{TV} \leq \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{|\mathcal S|}\,\lambda_2^t .\] The spectral gap \(1 - \lambda_2\) controls the rate of convergence; the mixing time is \(t_{mix} \sim (\log |\mathcal S|)/(1 - \lambda_2)\).

7.6 Convergence and independence

For an ergodic chain successive states become approximately independent at long lags, in the sense that \[I(X_t; X_{t+k}) \to 0 \text{ as } k \to \infty ,\] at a rate governed by the spectral gap. This mixing property is what makes long-run averages over a chain trajectory approximate expectations under \(\pi^*\) — the basis of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (Chapter 7).

For an ergodic chain with stationary distribution \(\pi^*\) and any bounded \(f : \mathcal S \to \mathbb R\), \[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} f(X_t) \to \sum_s \pi^*(s)\,f(s) = \mathbb E_{\pi^*}[f]\] almost surely as \(n \to \infty\).

7.7 Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation of a Markov-chain observable decays exponentially in the lag:

For a reversible chain, the autocorrelation of \(f\) satisfies \[C_f(k) = \mathbb E_{\pi^*}[f(X_0) f(X_k)] - \mathbb E_{\pi^*}[f]^2 = \sum_{i \geq 2} \langle f, e_i\rangle^2\,\lambda_i^k,\] where \(\{e_i\}\) are the eigenvectors of \(T\) orthonormalised in \(\ell^2(\pi^*)\). The dominant term decays as \(\lambda_2^k\).

Remark (Effective sample size). The integrated autocorrelation time \(\tau_f = 1 + 2\sum_{k \geq 1} C_f(k)/C_f(0)\) measures how many “independent” samples an \(n\)-step chain provides. The effective sample size is \(n/\tau_f\). Diagnosing large \(\tau_f\) is the main tool for identifying poor MCMC mixing.

7.8 Exercises

For a two-state Markov chain on \(\{0, 1\}\) with transition probabilities \(T(0, 1) = a\) and \(T(1, 0) = b\), find the stationary distribution and entropy rate.

Solution. \(\pi^*(0) = b/(a + b)\), \(\pi^*(1) = a/(a + b)\). Entropy rate: \[H_\infty = \frac{b}{a+b}h_2(a) + \frac{a}{a+b}h_2(b).\]

Compute the entropy production of a three-state cyclic chain with rates \(T(0 \to 1) = T(1 \to 2) = T(2 \to 0) = p\) (forward) and equal \(q\) (backward), \(p + q < 1\).

Solution. By symmetry \(\pi^* = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)\), and \[\sigma = 3 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot p \log \tfrac{p}{q} + 3 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot q \log \tfrac{q}{p} = (p - q)\log(p/q).\] Note \(\sigma > 0\) iff \(p \neq q\), and equals zero iff the chain is at detailed balance.

Show that for a reversible chain, \(\lambda_2\) satisfies \(\lambda_2 = 1 - 2\lambda_2^{eff}\) where \(\lambda_2^{eff}\) is the smallest positive eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian of \(T\). Interpret physically.

Solution. Detailed balance \(\pi^*(s)T(s, s') = \pi^*(s')T(s', s)\) makes the symmetric operator \(L = I - T\) self-adjoint in \(\ell^2(\pi^*)\); its spectrum is \(\{1 - \lambda_i\}\). The gap \(1 - \lambda_2\) is the smallest positive eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian — a Cheeger-type quantity that lower-bounds the conductance of the chain. Physically it is the relaxation rate of the slowest mode.

7.9 Callouts

Markov chains give an analytical framework for time series that fits neatly into information theory. Real time series (financial, biological) are only approximately Markov, but the framework is a useful null model.

When estimating an entropy rate from data, always use a conditional entropy estimator: naive plug-in estimation of \(H(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) has severe bias in high dimensions.

Long correlations kill mixing: chains with \(\lambda_2\) very close to 1 need exponentially many samples to converge and are effectively unusable as samplers. Always diagnose with \(\tau_f\) before trusting an MCMC estimate.

## Spectrum of a Markov chain

The rate at which a Markov chain approaches stationarity is set by the second-largest eigenvalue of its transition matrix:

::: {.proposition name="Spectral gap and mixing"}
For a reversible chain with eigenvalues $1 = \lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \geq \ldots \geq \lambda_{|\mathcal S|}$, the total-variation distance to stationarity satisfies
$$\|\pi_t - \pi^*\|_{TV} \leq \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{|\mathcal S|}\,\lambda_2^t .$$

:::

## Convergence and independence

For an ergodic chain successive states become approximately independent at long lags, in the sense that
$$I(X_t; X_{t+k}) \to 0 \text{ as } k \to \infty ,$$
at a rate governed by the spectral gap. This *mixing* property is what makes long-run averages over a chain trajectory approximate expectations under $\pi^*$ — the basis of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (Chapter 7).

::: {.corollary name="Ergodic theorem for Markov chains"}
For an ergodic chain with stationary distribution $\pi^*$ and any bounded $f : \mathcal S \to \mathbb R$,
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} f(X_t) \to \sum_s \pi^*(s)\,f(s) = \mathbb E_{\pi^*}[f]$$
almost surely as $n \to \infty$.
:::

## Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation of a Markov-chain observable decays exponentially in the lag:

::: {.proposition name="Autocorrelation of Markov chain"}
For a reversible chain, the autocorrelation of $f$ satisfies
$$C_f(k) = \mathbb E_{\pi^*}[f(X_0) f(X_k)] - \mathbb E_{\pi^*}[f]^2 = \sum_{i \geq 2} \langle f, e_i\rangle^2\,\lambda_i^k,$$
where $\{e_i\}$ are the eigenvectors of $T$ orthonormalised in $\ell^2(\pi^*)$. The dominant term decays as $\lambda_2^k$.
:::

::: {.remark name="Effective sample size"}
The **integrated autocorrelation time** $\tau_f = 1 + 2\sum_{k \geq 1} C_f(k)/C_f(0)$ measures how many "independent" samples an $n$-step chain provides. The *effective sample size* is $n/\tau_f$. Diagnosing large $\tau_f$ is the main tool for identifying poor MCMC mixing.
:::
