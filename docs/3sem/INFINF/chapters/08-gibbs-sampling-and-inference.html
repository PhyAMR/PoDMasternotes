9  Gibbs sampling and latent variables

Metropolis is universal but blunt: it proposes blindly and then corrects by rejection. When the target has structure — conditional distributions we can sample exactly, as in Bayesian models with conjugate priors and latent variables — a specialised algorithm, Gibbs sampling, exploits it and never rejects. This chapter derives Gibbs sampling as a special case of Metropolis–Hastings and works out in detail its most important application: inference in mixture models through latent variables, which turns out to be a stochastic cousin of K-means clustering.

9.1 Metropolis–Hastings

Metropolis required a symmetric proposal. With a general proposal \(T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)\), the kernel \[P(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x) = T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)A(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x) + R(\mathbf x)\,\delta(\mathbf x - \mathbf x')\] satisfies detailed balance with the Hastings acceptance probability \[A(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x) = \min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(\mathbf x')\,T(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf x')}{\pi(\mathbf x)\,T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)}\Bigr\}.\]

Proof. For \(\mathbf x' \neq \mathbf x\) (the \(\delta\) term is trivially symmetric), \[\begin{aligned} P(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x) &= T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x)\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(\mathbf x')T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')}{\pi(\mathbf x)T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)}\Bigr\} = \min\bigl\{T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x),\ T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x')\bigr\} \\ &= T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x')\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x)}{T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x')}\Bigr\} = P(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x'). \end{aligned}\]

The ratio \(T(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf x')/T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)\) corrects for proposals that favour some directions. The freedom to choose \(T\) is what we now exploit.

9.2 The Gibbs sampling algorithm

Write \(\mathbf x = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)\) and, for each \(k\), \[\mathbf x_{<k} = (x_1, \ldots, x_{k-1}), \qquad \mathbf x_{>k} = (x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_d), \qquad \mathbf x_{-k} = (\mathbf x_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k}).\] Gibbs sampling applies when we can sample each full conditional \(\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf x_{-k})\).

One sweep updates the coordinates in turn, always conditioning on the most recent values: \[\begin{aligned} x_1' &\sim \pi(x_1 \mid \mathbf x_{>1}), \\ x_2' &\sim \pi(x_2 \mid x_1', \mathbf x_{>2}), \\ &\ \ \vdots \\ x_k' &\sim \pi(x_k \mid \mathbf x'_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k}), \\ &\ \ \vdots \\ x_d' &\sim \pi(x_d \mid \mathbf x'_{<d}). \end{aligned}\] In the random-scan variant, each step picks a coordinate \(k\) uniformly at random and resamples it.

Each update is a Metropolis–Hastings move with proposal “change only \(x_k\), drawing it from its conditional”: \[T(x_k' \mid \mathbf x) = \pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf x'_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k}).\]

With this proposal, the Hastings acceptance probability is exactly 1.

Proof. Abbreviate \(\mathbf c = (\mathbf x'_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k})\), the coordinates that do not change. The move is \((x_k, \mathbf c) \to (x_k', \mathbf c)\), and \[A = \min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(x_k', \mathbf c)\;\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)}{\pi(x_k, \mathbf c)\;\pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)}\Bigr\}.\] Factorising \(\pi(x_k', \mathbf c) = \pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)\) and \(\pi(x_k, \mathbf c) = \pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)\), \[A = \min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)\,\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)}{\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)\,\pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)}\Bigr\} = 1 .\]

So every single-coordinate update leaves \(\pi\) invariant, and so does their composition (a full sweep). Note that a systematic sweep as a whole does not satisfy detailed balance (the reversed sweep updates coordinates in the opposite order), but it does leave \(\pi\) stationary, which is what matters; the random-scan version is reversible. Under mild conditions (e.g. \(\pi > 0\) everywhere) the chain is ergodic and converges to \(\pi\).

Remark. “Acceptance 1” does not mean “efficient”. Gibbs moves only along coordinate axes, with step sizes set by the conditional widths. When the target is strongly correlated, conditionals are narrow and the chain crawls.

Let \((x_1, x_2) \sim \mathcal N\Bigl(\mathbf 0, \begin{pmatrix}1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}\Bigr)\). The full conditionals are \[x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \mathcal N(\rho x_2,\ 1 - \rho^2), \qquad x_2 \mid x_1 \sim \mathcal N(\rho x_1,\ 1 - \rho^2).\] After one sweep, \(x_1^{(t+1)} = \rho x_2^{(t)} + \ldots = \rho^2 x_1^{(t)} + \text{noise}\): the sequence \(x_1^{(t)}\) is an AR(1) process with coefficient \(\rho^2\). By Chapter 7 its integrated autocorrelation time is \[A = \frac{1 + \rho^2}{1 - \rho^2}.\] For \(\rho = 0.5\), \(A \approx 1.7\) (excellent); for \(\rho = 0.99\), \(A \approx 100\); for \(\rho = 0.999\), \(A \approx 1000\). The chain zig-zags along the narrow ridge with steps of size \(\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\) while it needs to travel distances of order 1. Reparameterising (e.g. rotating to \(u = x_1 + x_2\), \(v = x_1 - x_2\), which are independent) or blocking (updating \((x_1, x_2)\) jointly) cures the problem.

9.3 When Gibbs sampling helps: conjugacy

Gibbs is useful when the full conditionals belong to families we can sample directly. The paradigm is conjugacy: a prior is conjugate to a likelihood if the posterior is in the same family as the prior.

likelihood conjugate prior posterior
Bernoulli/Binomial(\(\theta\)) Beta\((a, b)\) Beta\((a + k, b + n - k)\)
Categorical/Multinomial(\(\mathbf p\)) Dirichlet\((\boldsymbol\gamma)\) Dirichlet\((\boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf n)\)
Normal(\(\mu\)), known \(\sigma^2\) Normal\((\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) Normal (precisions add)
Normal, known \(\mu\), unknown \(\sigma^2\) Inverse-Gamma\((\alpha, \beta)\) Inverse-Gamma\((\alpha + n/2, \beta + \tfrac12\sum(x_i - \mu)^2)\)
Poisson(\(\lambda\)) Gamma\((\alpha, \beta)\) Gamma\((\alpha + \sum x_i, \beta + n)\)

In a multi-parameter model, the joint posterior is rarely in a standard family, but if each parameter has a conjugate prior given the others, every full conditional is standard and Gibbs applies.

Model: \(y_i \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\beta^\top\mathbf x_i, \sigma^2)\), priors \(\boldsymbol\beta \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \tau^2 I)\) and \(\sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(\alpha, \beta)\). The full conditionals are

  • \(\boldsymbol\beta \mid \sigma^2, \mathbf y \sim \mathcal N(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}, \Sigma_\beta)\), with \(\Sigma_\beta = (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X/\sigma^2 + I/\tau^2)^{-1}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \Sigma_\beta\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y/\sigma^2\);
  • \(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \mathbf y \sim \text{InvGamma}\bigl(\alpha + n/2,\ \beta + \tfrac12\|\mathbf y - \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\bigr)\).

Alternating the two conjugate draws samples the joint posterior, which is not itself a standard distribution.

9.4 Mixture models and latent variables

9.4.1 The mixture model

Suppose each data point \(x\) is drawn from one of \(K\) Gaussians with means \(\boldsymbol\mu = (\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_K)\) and a common, known standard deviation \(\sigma\), the \(k\)-th being chosen with probability \(p_k\): \[f(x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \sum_{k=1}^K p_k\,(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x - \mu_k)^2}{2\sigma^2}}.\] For \(N\) independent points \(\mathbf x = \{x_i\}\), the likelihood and the posterior are \[f(\mathbf x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \prod_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^K p_k(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_k)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p \mid \mathbf x) = \frac{f(\mathbf x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p)f(\boldsymbol\mu)f(\mathbf p)}{c(\mathbf x)} .\] One could run Metropolis on this posterior, but it is unpleasant: the “sum inside the product” makes every likelihood evaluation cost \(O(NK)\), and the posterior has no convenient structure.

9.4.2 Introducing latent variables

The trick is to add variables. For each point introduce a latent label \(Z_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\}\) saying which component generated it: \[Z_i \mid \mathbf p \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf p), \quad P(Z_i = k) = p_k; \qquad f(x_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu, Z_i) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_{Z_i})^2}{2\sigma^2}} .\] Summing over the labels recovers the original model, \(f(\mathbf x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \sum_{\mathbf Z} f(\mathbf x, \mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p)\). But the complete-data likelihood — given the labels — is much simpler: \[f(\mathbf x, \mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \prod_i p_{Z_i}(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_{Z_i})^2}{2\sigma^2}} = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}\prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{n_k}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i : Z_i = k}(x_i - \mu_k)^2},\] with \(n_k = \sum_i \chi(Z_i = k)\) the number of points in component \(k\). The product over points has become a product over components, each depending only on its own points. Two benefits:

  1. the likelihood is cheap to evaluate;
  2. with conjugate priors, every full conditional is a standard distribution, so Gibbs sampling applies.

Remark. Sampling the augmented posterior \(f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p, \mathbf Z \mid \mathbf x)\) and simply ignoring \(\mathbf Z\) in the output gives samples from the marginal \(f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p \mid \mathbf x)\). Making the space bigger makes sampling easier — a recurring theme (cf. the momentum variables of HMC in Chapter 11).

9.4.3 Priors

  • Means: \(\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) independently, \(f(\boldsymbol\mu) = (2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-K/2}\prod_k e^{-(\mu_k - \mu_0)^2/2\sigma_0^2}\). Taking \(\mu_0 = 0\), \(\sigma_0 \to \infty\) gives a flat prior.
  • Weights: \(\mathbf p \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma)\), \(f(\mathbf p) = B(\boldsymbol\gamma)^{-1}\prod_k p_k^{\gamma_k - 1}\) on the simplex. \(\gamma_k = 1\) is uniform on the simplex.

The joint posterior is \[f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf Z, \mathbf p \mid \mathbf x) \propto \prod_{k=1}^K\Bigl[e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i:Z_i=k}(x_i - \mu_k)^2}\;e^{-\frac{(\mu_k - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}}\;p_k^{n_k + \gamma_k - 1}\Bigr].\]

9.4.4 Full conditionals

Each conditional is obtained by keeping only the factors that contain the variable of interest.

Means. For \(\mu_k\), only the \(k\)-th factor matters: \[f(\mu_k \mid \mathbf x, \mathbf Z, \mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu_{-k}) \propto \exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i:Z_i = k}(x_i - \mu_k)^2 - \frac{(\mu_k - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\Bigr].\] The exponent is quadratic in \(\mu_k\); completing the square, \[\mu_k \mid \ldots \sim \mathcal N(\mu_k', \sigma_k'^2), \qquad \mu_k' = \frac{\mu_0/\sigma_0^2 + n_k\bar X_k/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma_0^2 + n_k/\sigma^2}, \qquad \sigma_k'^2 = \Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n_k}{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{-1},\] with \(\bar X_k = \frac1{n_k}\sum_{i:Z_i = k}x_i\) the mean of the points currently assigned to \(k\). Precisions add, and the mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the data mean (exactly as for the Gaussian channel of Chapter 5).

Weights. Only \(\prod_k p_k^{n_k + \gamma_k - 1}\) depends on \(\mathbf p\), so \[\mathbf p \mid \ldots \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma'), \qquad \boldsymbol\gamma' = \boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf n .\] The prior parameters \(\gamma_k\) act as “pseudo-counts” added to the observed counts \(n_k\).

Labels. For \(Z_i\), only the factor involving point \(i\) matters; normalising over its \(K\) possible values, \[P(Z_i = k \mid \ldots) = \frac{p_k\,e^{-(x_i - \mu_k)^2/2\sigma^2}}{\sum_{l}p_l\,e^{-(x_i - \mu_l)^2/2\sigma^2}} \equiv p'_{ik}, \qquad Z_i \mid \ldots \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf p'_i).\] This is Bayes’ theorem for “which component produced \(x_i\)?”, with prior \(p_k\) and likelihood \(\mathcal N(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma^2)\).

Initialise \(\mathbf Z\) (e.g. at random). Repeat:

  1. for each \(k\): compute \(n_k\), \(\bar X_k\) and sample \(\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu'_k, \sigma'^2_k)\);
  2. sample \(\mathbf p \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf n)\);
  3. for each \(i\): compute \(p'_{ik}\) and sample \(Z_i \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf p'_i)\).

Generate data from \(K = 4\) components with \(\mu_k = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}\), \(\sigma = 0.5\) and \(p_k = 0.25\). Starting from random labels, the sampled \(\mu_k\) converge to the true values within a handful of sweeps, and the agreement between true and inferred labels rises just as quickly (Allegra, Fig. 10.1). It saturates at about \(97\%\): with neighbouring means \(4\sigma\) apart, a point falls past the midpoint towards a neighbour with probability \(1 - \Phi(2) \approx 2.3\%\) per side, and such points are genuinely ambiguous — no method can classify them correctly. The posterior spread of each \(\mu_k\) is \(\approx \sigma/\sqrt{n_k}\).

9.4.5 Gibbs sampling and K-means

Soft K-means clustering alternates two steps:

  • assignment: each point gets a “responsibility” for each cluster, \(r_{ik} \propto e^{-(x_i - \mu_k)^2/2\sigma^2}\) (normalised over \(k\));
  • update: each mean moves to the responsibility-weighted average, \(\mu_k = \sum_i r_{ik}x_i\big/\sum_i r_{ik}\).

Now take the Gibbs sampler in the limit \(\sigma_0 \to \infty\) (flat prior on the means) and \(\gamma_k = \gamma \to \infty\) (weights pinned at \(p_k = 1/K\)). Then:

  • \(\mu_k\) is drawn from a Gaussian centred at \(\bar X_k = \sum_i\chi(Z_i = k)x_i/\sum_i\chi(Z_i = k)\) with small width \(\sigma/\sqrt{n_k}\), so \(\mu_k \approx \bar X_k\);
  • \(Z_i\) is drawn with \(P(Z_i = k) \propto e^{-(x_i - \mu_k)^2/2\sigma^2} = r_{ik}\).

Averaging over the random labels, \(\mathbb E[\chi(Z_i = k)] = r_{ik}\), so \(\mu_k \approx \sum_i r_{ik}x_i/\sum_i r_{ik}\): the Gibbs sampler is a stochastic version of soft K-means, and the two agree when clusters are well separated. Conversely, this reveals that K-means implicitly assumes a mixture of isotropic Gaussians with equal widths and equal weights — not at all obvious from its formulation. (The deterministic counterpart that maximises the likelihood instead of sampling is the EM algorithm.)

9.4.6 Label switching

The mixture likelihood is invariant under permutations of the component labels, so with symmetric priors the posterior has \(K!\) identical modes. A Gibbs chain typically stays in one labelling (moving between them requires relabelling all points at once — an exponentially unlikely sequence of single-site updates). For estimating label-invariant quantities this is harmless; for component-specific summaries one imposes an ordering (\(\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \cdots\)) or post-processes the samples.

9.5 Modern context

Gibbs sampling remains the method of choice for models with discrete latent variables — mixtures, hidden Markov models, topic models such as LDA, Ising-type models — where gradient-based methods (HMC, Chapter 11) do not apply. For continuous parameters with strong correlations, blocking, reparameterisation or Hamiltonian methods are usually preferable.

9.6 Exercises

Derive the full conditional of \(\mu_k\) in the mixture model by completing the square, and check the limits \(n_k = 0\) and \(\sigma_0 \to \infty\).

Solution. The exponent is \(-\tfrac12\bigl[\mu_k^2(n_k/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2) - 2\mu_k(n_k\bar X_k/\sigma^2 + \mu_0/\sigma_0^2)\bigr] + \text{const}\), i.e. a Gaussian with precision \(n_k/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2\) and mean (linear coefficient)/(precision) \(= \mu'_k\). If \(n_k = 0\) (empty cluster) the conditional is the prior \(\mathcal N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\). If \(\sigma_0 \to \infty\), it is \(\mathcal N(\bar X_k, \sigma^2/n_k)\) — the frequentist sampling distribution of the mean.

Write a Gibbs sampler for the joint posterior of \((\mu, \tau)\) for data \(x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, 1/\tau)\) with priors \(\mu \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, 1/\lambda_0)\) and \(\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a, b)\).

Solution. \(\mu \mid \tau, \mathbf x \sim \mathcal N\Bigl(\frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + \tau\sum_i x_i}{\lambda_0 + N\tau}, \frac{1}{\lambda_0 + N\tau}\Bigr)\); \(\tau \mid \mu, \mathbf x \sim \text{Gamma}\bigl(a + N/2,\ b + \tfrac12\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2\bigr)\). Alternate the two draws.

For the bivariate Gaussian with correlation \(\rho\), how many Gibbs sweeps are needed for an effective sample size of 1000 when \(\rho = 0.95\)? How many if one first rotates to \(u = (x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt2\), \(v = (x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt 2\)?

Solution. \(A = (1 + 0.9025)/(1 - 0.9025) \approx 19.5\), so about \(19{,}500\) sweeps. After rotation \(u\) and \(v\) are independent (\(\operatorname{Var} u = 1 + \rho\), \(\operatorname{Var} v = 1 - \rho\)), the conditionals equal the marginals, and every sweep is an independent draw: 1000 sweeps.

For a two-component mixture with means \(\pm 3\) and \(\sigma = 1\), explain why the Gibbs sampler does not visit the “label-swapped” mode \((\mu_1, \mu_2) \to (\mu_2, \mu_1)\).

Solution. To swap, all points currently labelled 1 must be relabelled 2 and vice versa. Each single-site update is conditioned on the current means, under which relabelling a point costs a factor \(\approx e^{-(6)^2/2}\) in probability; the means only move once many labels have changed. The path between the two modes passes through configurations of exponentially small posterior probability, so the chain essentially never makes the transition. This is harmless for label-invariant summaries; otherwise impose \(\mu_1 < \mu_2\).

9.7 Callouts

Gibbs is simple and exact when conjugacy holds. When a conditional is not standard, replace that single update by a Metropolis step targeting the conditional (Metropolis-within-Gibbs) — still a valid sampler.

Blocking correlated variables (updating them jointly) and reparameterising to decorrelate them are the standard cures for slow Gibbs mixing.

Gibbs can fail on multimodal targets whose conditionals are unimodal: each coordinate move is local in the joint space.

The diagnostics of Chapter 7 (burn-in, autocorrelation, ESS, \(\hat R\)) apply without change. Acceptance rate 1 says nothing about how well the chain explores the target.

Metropolis is universal but blunt: it proposes blindly and then corrects by rejection. When the target has structure — conditional distributions we can sample exactly, as in Bayesian models with conjugate priors and latent variables — a specialised algorithm, **Gibbs sampling**, exploits it and never rejects. This chapter derives Gibbs sampling as a special case of Metropolis–Hastings and works out in detail its most important application: inference in mixture models through latent variables, which turns out to be a stochastic cousin of K-means clustering.

## Metropolis–Hastings

Metropolis required a symmetric proposal. With a general proposal $T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)$, the kernel
$$P(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x) = T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)A(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x) + R(\mathbf x)\,\delta(\mathbf x - \mathbf x')$$
satisfies detailed balance with the **Hastings acceptance probability**
$$A(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x) = \min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(\mathbf x')\,T(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf x')}{\pi(\mathbf x)\,T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)}\Bigr\}.$$

::: {.proof}
For $\mathbf x' \neq \mathbf x$ (the $\delta$ term is trivially symmetric),
$$\begin{aligned}
P(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x) &= T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x)\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(\mathbf x')T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')}{\pi(\mathbf x)T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)}\Bigr\} = \min\bigl\{T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x),\ T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x')\bigr\} \\
&= T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x')\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{T(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\pi(\mathbf x)}{T(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x')}\Bigr\} = P(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf x')\pi(\mathbf x').
\end{aligned}$$
:::

The ratio $T(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf x')/T(\mathbf x' \mid \mathbf x)$ corrects for proposals that favour some directions. The freedom to choose $T$ is what we now exploit.

## The Gibbs sampling algorithm

Write $\mathbf x = (x_1, \ldots, x_d)$ and, for each $k$,
$$\mathbf x_{<k} = (x_1, \ldots, x_{k-1}), \qquad \mathbf x_{>k} = (x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_d), \qquad \mathbf x_{-k} = (\mathbf x_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k}).$$
Gibbs sampling applies when we can sample each **full conditional** $\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf x_{-k})$.

::: {.definition name="Gibbs sampler (systematic scan)"}
One sweep updates the coordinates in turn, always conditioning on the *most recent* values:
$$\begin{aligned}
x_1' &\sim \pi(x_1 \mid \mathbf x_{>1}), \\
x_2' &\sim \pi(x_2 \mid x_1', \mathbf x_{>2}), \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
x_k' &\sim \pi(x_k \mid \mathbf x'_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k}), \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
x_d' &\sim \pi(x_d \mid \mathbf x'_{<d}).
\end{aligned}$$
In the *random-scan* variant, each step picks a coordinate $k$ uniformly at random and resamples it.
:::

Each update is a Metropolis–Hastings move with proposal "change only $x_k$, drawing it from its conditional":
$$T(x_k' \mid \mathbf x) = \pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf x'_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k}).$$

::: {.theorem name="Gibbs moves are always accepted"}
With this proposal, the Hastings acceptance probability is exactly 1.
:::

::: {.proof}
Abbreviate $\mathbf c = (\mathbf x'_{<k}, \mathbf x_{>k})$, the coordinates that do not change. The move is $(x_k, \mathbf c) \to (x_k', \mathbf c)$, and
$$A = \min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(x_k', \mathbf c)\;\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)}{\pi(x_k, \mathbf c)\;\pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)}\Bigr\}.$$
Factorising $\pi(x_k', \mathbf c) = \pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)$ and $\pi(x_k, \mathbf c) = \pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)$,
$$A = \min\Bigl\{1, \frac{\pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)\,\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)}{\pi(x_k \mid \mathbf c)\pi(\mathbf c)\,\pi(x_k' \mid \mathbf c)}\Bigr\} = 1 .$$
:::

So every single-coordinate update leaves $\pi$ invariant, and so does their composition (a full sweep). Note that a systematic sweep as a whole does *not* satisfy detailed balance (the reversed sweep updates coordinates in the opposite order), but it does leave $\pi$ stationary, which is what matters; the random-scan version is reversible. Under mild conditions (e.g. $\pi > 0$ everywhere) the chain is ergodic and converges to $\pi$.

::: {.remark}
"Acceptance 1" does not mean "efficient". Gibbs moves only along coordinate axes, with step sizes set by the *conditional* widths. When the target is strongly correlated, conditionals are narrow and the chain crawls.
:::

::: {.example name="Gibbs on a correlated Gaussian"}
Let $(x_1, x_2) \sim \mathcal N\Bigl(\mathbf 0, \begin{pmatrix}1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}\Bigr)$. The full conditionals are
$$x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \mathcal N(\rho x_2,\ 1 - \rho^2), \qquad x_2 \mid x_1 \sim \mathcal N(\rho x_1,\ 1 - \rho^2).$$
After one sweep, $x_1^{(t+1)} = \rho x_2^{(t)} + \ldots = \rho^2 x_1^{(t)} + \text{noise}$: the sequence $x_1^{(t)}$ is an AR(1) process with coefficient $\rho^2$. By Chapter 7 its integrated autocorrelation time is
$$A = \frac{1 + \rho^2}{1 - \rho^2}.$$
For $\rho = 0.5$, $A \approx 1.7$ (excellent); for $\rho = 0.99$, $A \approx 100$; for $\rho = 0.999$, $A \approx 1000$. The chain zig-zags along the narrow ridge with steps of size $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$ while it needs to travel distances of order 1. **Reparameterising** (e.g. rotating to $u = x_1 + x_2$, $v = x_1 - x_2$, which are independent) or **blocking** (updating $(x_1, x_2)$ jointly) cures the problem.
:::

## When Gibbs sampling helps: conjugacy

Gibbs is useful when the full conditionals belong to families we can sample directly. The paradigm is **conjugacy**: a prior is conjugate to a likelihood if the posterior is in the same family as the prior.

| likelihood | conjugate prior | posterior |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Bernoulli/Binomial($\theta$) | Beta$(a, b)$ | Beta$(a + k, b + n - k)$ |
| Categorical/Multinomial($\mathbf p$) | Dirichlet$(\boldsymbol\gamma)$ | Dirichlet$(\boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf n)$ |
| Normal($\mu$), known $\sigma^2$ | Normal$(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ | Normal (precisions add) |
| Normal, known $\mu$, unknown $\sigma^2$ | Inverse-Gamma$(\alpha, \beta)$ | Inverse-Gamma$(\alpha + n/2, \beta + \tfrac12\sum(x_i - \mu)^2)$ |
| Poisson($\lambda$) | Gamma$(\alpha, \beta)$ | Gamma$(\alpha + \sum x_i, \beta + n)$ |

In a multi-parameter model, the joint posterior is rarely in a standard family, but if each parameter has a conjugate prior *given the others*, every full conditional is standard and Gibbs applies.

::: {.example name="Gibbs for Bayesian linear regression"}
Model: $y_i \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\beta^\top\mathbf x_i, \sigma^2)$, priors $\boldsymbol\beta \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \tau^2 I)$ and $\sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(\alpha, \beta)$. The full conditionals are

- $\boldsymbol\beta \mid \sigma^2, \mathbf y \sim \mathcal N(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}, \Sigma_\beta)$, with $\Sigma_\beta = (\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X/\sigma^2 + I/\tau^2)^{-1}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \Sigma_\beta\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y/\sigma^2$;
- $\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \mathbf y \sim \text{InvGamma}\bigl(\alpha + n/2,\ \beta + \tfrac12\|\mathbf y - \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\bigr)$.

Alternating the two conjugate draws samples the joint posterior, which is not itself a standard distribution.
:::

## Mixture models and latent variables

### The mixture model

Suppose each data point $x$ is drawn from one of $K$ Gaussians with means $\boldsymbol\mu = (\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_K)$ and a common, known standard deviation $\sigma$, the $k$-th being chosen with probability $p_k$:
$$f(x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \sum_{k=1}^K p_k\,(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x - \mu_k)^2}{2\sigma^2}}.$$
For $N$ independent points $\mathbf x = \{x_i\}$, the likelihood and the posterior are
$$f(\mathbf x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \prod_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^K p_k(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_k)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p \mid \mathbf x) = \frac{f(\mathbf x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p)f(\boldsymbol\mu)f(\mathbf p)}{c(\mathbf x)} .$$
One could run Metropolis on this posterior, but it is unpleasant: the "sum inside the product" makes every likelihood evaluation cost $O(NK)$, and the posterior has no convenient structure.

### Introducing latent variables

The trick is to **add** variables. For each point introduce a latent label $Z_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ saying which component generated it:
$$Z_i \mid \mathbf p \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf p), \quad P(Z_i = k) = p_k; \qquad f(x_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu, Z_i) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_{Z_i})^2}{2\sigma^2}} .$$
Summing over the labels recovers the original model, $f(\mathbf x \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \sum_{\mathbf Z} f(\mathbf x, \mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p)$. But the **complete-data likelihood** — given the labels — is much simpler:
$$f(\mathbf x, \mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p) = \prod_i p_{Z_i}(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_{Z_i})^2}{2\sigma^2}} = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}\prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{n_k}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i : Z_i = k}(x_i - \mu_k)^2},$$
with $n_k = \sum_i \chi(Z_i = k)$ the number of points in component $k$. The product over points has become a product over components, each depending only on its own points. Two benefits:

1. the likelihood is cheap to evaluate;
2. with **conjugate priors**, every full conditional is a standard distribution, so Gibbs sampling applies.

::: {.remark}
Sampling the *augmented* posterior $f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p, \mathbf Z \mid \mathbf x)$ and simply ignoring $\mathbf Z$ in the output gives samples from the marginal $f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf p \mid \mathbf x)$. Making the space *bigger* makes sampling *easier* — a recurring theme (cf. the momentum variables of HMC in Chapter 11).
:::

### Priors

- Means: $\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ independently, $f(\boldsymbol\mu) = (2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-K/2}\prod_k e^{-(\mu_k - \mu_0)^2/2\sigma_0^2}$. Taking $\mu_0 = 0$, $\sigma_0 \to \infty$ gives a flat prior.
- Weights: $\mathbf p \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma)$, $f(\mathbf p) = B(\boldsymbol\gamma)^{-1}\prod_k p_k^{\gamma_k - 1}$ on the simplex. $\gamma_k = 1$ is uniform on the simplex.

The joint posterior is
$$f(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf Z, \mathbf p \mid \mathbf x) \propto \prod_{k=1}^K\Bigl[e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i:Z_i=k}(x_i - \mu_k)^2}\;e^{-\frac{(\mu_k - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}}\;p_k^{n_k + \gamma_k - 1}\Bigr].$$

### Full conditionals

Each conditional is obtained by keeping only the factors that contain the variable of interest.

**Means.** For $\mu_k$, only the $k$-th factor matters:
$$f(\mu_k \mid \mathbf x, \mathbf Z, \mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu_{-k}) \propto \exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i:Z_i = k}(x_i - \mu_k)^2 - \frac{(\mu_k - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\Bigr].$$
The exponent is quadratic in $\mu_k$; completing the square,
$$\mu_k \mid \ldots \sim \mathcal N(\mu_k', \sigma_k'^2), \qquad \mu_k' = \frac{\mu_0/\sigma_0^2 + n_k\bar X_k/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma_0^2 + n_k/\sigma^2}, \qquad \sigma_k'^2 = \Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n_k}{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{-1},$$
with $\bar X_k = \frac1{n_k}\sum_{i:Z_i = k}x_i$ the mean of the points currently assigned to $k$. **Precisions add, and the mean is a precision-weighted average** of the prior mean and the data mean (exactly as for the Gaussian channel of Chapter 5).

**Weights.** Only $\prod_k p_k^{n_k + \gamma_k - 1}$ depends on $\mathbf p$, so
$$\mathbf p \mid \ldots \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma'), \qquad \boldsymbol\gamma' = \boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf n .$$
The prior parameters $\gamma_k$ act as "pseudo-counts" added to the observed counts $n_k$.

**Labels.** For $Z_i$, only the factor involving point $i$ matters; normalising over its $K$ possible values,
$$P(Z_i = k \mid \ldots) = \frac{p_k\,e^{-(x_i - \mu_k)^2/2\sigma^2}}{\sum_{l}p_l\,e^{-(x_i - \mu_l)^2/2\sigma^2}} \equiv p'_{ik}, \qquad Z_i \mid \ldots \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf p'_i).$$
This is Bayes' theorem for "which component produced $x_i$?", with prior $p_k$ and likelihood $\mathcal N(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma^2)$.

::: {.definition name="Gibbs sampler for the Gaussian mixture"}
Initialise $\mathbf Z$ (e.g. at random). Repeat:

1. for each $k$: compute $n_k$, $\bar X_k$ and sample $\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu'_k, \sigma'^2_k)$;
2. sample $\mathbf p \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf n)$;
3. for each $i$: compute $p'_{ik}$ and sample $Z_i \sim \text{Cat}(\mathbf p'_i)$.
:::

::: {.example name="Four well-separated components"}
Generate data from $K = 4$ components with $\mu_k = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$, $\sigma = 0.5$ and $p_k = 0.25$. Starting from random labels, the sampled $\mu_k$ converge to the true values within a handful of sweeps, and the agreement between true and inferred labels rises just as quickly (Allegra, Fig. 10.1). It saturates at about $97\%$: with neighbouring means $4\sigma$ apart, a point falls past the midpoint towards a neighbour with probability $1 - \Phi(2) \approx 2.3\%$ per side, and such points are genuinely ambiguous — no method can classify them correctly. The posterior spread of each $\mu_k$ is $\approx \sigma/\sqrt{n_k}$.
:::

### Gibbs sampling and K-means

**Soft K-means** clustering alternates two steps:

- *assignment*: each point gets a "responsibility" for each cluster, $r_{ik} \propto e^{-(x_i - \mu_k)^2/2\sigma^2}$ (normalised over $k$);
- *update*: each mean moves to the responsibility-weighted average, $\mu_k = \sum_i r_{ik}x_i\big/\sum_i r_{ik}$.

Now take the Gibbs sampler in the limit $\sigma_0 \to \infty$ (flat prior on the means) and $\gamma_k = \gamma \to \infty$ (weights pinned at $p_k = 1/K$). Then:

- $\mu_k$ is drawn from a Gaussian centred at $\bar X_k = \sum_i\chi(Z_i = k)x_i/\sum_i\chi(Z_i = k)$ with small width $\sigma/\sqrt{n_k}$, so $\mu_k \approx \bar X_k$;
- $Z_i$ is drawn with $P(Z_i = k) \propto e^{-(x_i - \mu_k)^2/2\sigma^2} = r_{ik}$.

Averaging over the random labels, $\mathbb E[\chi(Z_i = k)] = r_{ik}$, so $\mu_k \approx \sum_i r_{ik}x_i/\sum_i r_{ik}$: **the Gibbs sampler is a stochastic version of soft K-means**, and the two agree when clusters are well separated. Conversely, this reveals that K-means *implicitly assumes* a mixture of isotropic Gaussians with equal widths and equal weights — not at all obvious from its formulation. (The deterministic counterpart that maximises the likelihood instead of sampling is the EM algorithm.)

### Label switching

The mixture likelihood is invariant under permutations of the component labels, so with symmetric priors the posterior has $K!$ identical modes. A Gibbs chain typically stays in one labelling (moving between them requires relabelling all points at once — an exponentially unlikely sequence of single-site updates). For estimating label-invariant quantities this is harmless; for component-specific summaries one imposes an ordering ($\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \cdots$) or post-processes the samples.

## Modern context

Gibbs sampling remains the method of choice for models with **discrete latent variables** — mixtures, hidden Markov models, topic models such as LDA, Ising-type models — where gradient-based methods (HMC, Chapter 11) do not apply. For continuous parameters with strong correlations, blocking, reparameterisation or Hamiltonian methods are usually preferable.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Derive the full conditional of $\mu_k$ in the mixture model by completing the square, and check the limits $n_k = 0$ and $\sigma_0 \to \infty$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The exponent is $-\tfrac12\bigl[\mu_k^2(n_k/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2) - 2\mu_k(n_k\bar X_k/\sigma^2 + \mu_0/\sigma_0^2)\bigr] + \text{const}$, i.e. a Gaussian with precision $n_k/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2$ and mean (linear coefficient)/(precision) $= \mu'_k$. If $n_k = 0$ (empty cluster) the conditional is the prior $\mathcal N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$. If $\sigma_0 \to \infty$, it is $\mathcal N(\bar X_k, \sigma^2/n_k)$ — the frequentist sampling distribution of the mean.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Write a Gibbs sampler for the joint posterior of $(\mu, \tau)$ for data $x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, 1/\tau)$ with priors $\mu \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, 1/\lambda_0)$ and $\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a, b)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\mu \mid \tau, \mathbf x \sim \mathcal N\Bigl(\frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + \tau\sum_i x_i}{\lambda_0 + N\tau}, \frac{1}{\lambda_0 + N\tau}\Bigr)$; $\tau \mid \mu, \mathbf x \sim \text{Gamma}\bigl(a + N/2,\ b + \tfrac12\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2\bigr)$. Alternate the two draws.
:::

::: {.exercise}
For the bivariate Gaussian with correlation $\rho$, how many Gibbs sweeps are needed for an effective sample size of 1000 when $\rho = 0.95$? How many if one first rotates to $u = (x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt2$, $v = (x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt 2$?
:::

::: {.solution}
$A = (1 + 0.9025)/(1 - 0.9025) \approx 19.5$, so about $19{,}500$ sweeps. After rotation $u$ and $v$ are independent ($\operatorname{Var} u = 1 + \rho$, $\operatorname{Var} v = 1 - \rho$), the conditionals equal the marginals, and every sweep is an independent draw: 1000 sweeps.
:::

::: {.exercise}
For a two-component mixture with means $\pm 3$ and $\sigma = 1$, explain why the Gibbs sampler does not visit the "label-swapped" mode $(\mu_1, \mu_2) \to (\mu_2, \mu_1)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
To swap, all points currently labelled 1 must be relabelled 2 and vice versa. Each single-site update is conditioned on the current means, under which relabelling a point costs a factor $\approx e^{-(6)^2/2}$ in probability; the means only move once many labels have changed. The path between the two modes passes through configurations of exponentially small posterior probability, so the chain essentially never makes the transition. This is harmless for label-invariant summaries; otherwise impose $\mu_1 < \mu_2$.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
Gibbs is *simple and exact* when conjugacy holds. When a conditional is not standard, replace that single update by a Metropolis step targeting the conditional (**Metropolis-within-Gibbs**) — still a valid sampler.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
Blocking correlated variables (updating them jointly) and reparameterising to decorrelate them are the standard cures for slow Gibbs mixing.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
Gibbs can fail on multimodal targets whose conditionals are unimodal: each coordinate move is local in the joint space.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
The diagnostics of Chapter 7 (burn-in, autocorrelation, ESS, $\hat R$) apply without change. Acceptance rate 1 says nothing about how well the chain explores the target.
:::
