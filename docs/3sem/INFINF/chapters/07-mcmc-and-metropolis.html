8  Markov Chain Monte Carlo and Metropolis

Suppose we want to compute an expectation \(\mathbb E_\pi[f]\) against a distribution \(\pi\) we can evaluate only up to a normalisation constant — a Bayesian posterior, or a Boltzmann distribution in high dimension. Direct integration is impossible; independent sampling is impossible; but dependent sampling via a cleverly-designed Markov chain works. This chapter introduces the Metropolis algorithm and its theoretical justification.

8.1 The Monte Carlo approach

The core Monte Carlo idea is dead simple:

Given i.i.d. samples \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim \pi\) and a function \(f : \mathcal X \to \mathbb R\) with finite variance, the Monte Carlo estimator of \(\mathbb E_\pi[f]\) is \[\widehat{\mathbb E}_n[f] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i).\] By the law of large numbers \(\widehat{\mathbb E}_n[f] \to \mathbb E_\pi[f]\) a.s.; by the CLT the error is \(O(1/\sqrt n)\).

The rate \(1/\sqrt n\) is dimension-free — it does not degrade with the size of \(\mathcal X\). This is the fundamental advantage of Monte Carlo over deterministic quadrature in high dimension.

8.2 Simple ways to sample a distribution

For low-dimensional or well-structured \(\pi\) there are direct sampling algorithms:

For a real random variable \(X\) with CDF \(F\): draw \(U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)\) and set \(X = F^{-1}(U)\). Then \(X \sim F\). Requires \(F^{-1}\) in closed form or via numerical inversion.

To sample \(\pi\), choose a proposal \(q\) with \(\pi(x) \leq M\,q(x)\) for some \(M \geq 1\). Draw \(X \sim q\) and accept with probability \(\pi(X)/(M q(X))\). Accepted samples are exactly \(\sim \pi\). Acceptance probability is \(1/M\); the method fails in high dimension because \(M\) grows exponentially.

Remark. Direct methods fail on the problem that motivates most of Bayesian statistics: sampling from a high-dimensional posterior \(\pi(\boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf y) \propto p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\theta) p(\boldsymbol\theta)\) where the normalisation is intractable. This is where MCMC comes in.

8.3 Markov Chain Monte Carlo

The MCMC insight: construct a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is \(\pi\), run it long enough that its state distribution has converged to \(\pi\), and use its trajectory to estimate expectations. The samples \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) are not independent, but by the ergodic theorem (Chapter 6) time averages still converge to expectations.

Given a Markov chain with stationary distribution \(\pi\) and initial burn-in \(b\), \[\widehat{\mathbb E}_n[f] = \frac{1}{n - b}\sum_{t = b + 1}^{n} f(X_t) \to \mathbb E_\pi[f] \text{ a.s.}\] The finite-sample error scales as \(\sqrt{\tau_f}/\sqrt n\) where \(\tau_f\) is the integrated autocorrelation time of \(f\) (Chapter 6).

8.4 The Metropolis algorithm

The workhorse MCMC algorithm is Metropolis (1953) and its Hastings extension:

Given a target \(\pi\) known up to a constant and a proposal \(q(y \mid x)\):

  1. From current state \(X_t = x\), draw a proposal \(y \sim q(\cdot \mid x)\).
  2. Compute the acceptance ratio \[A(x \to y) = \min\left(1,\ \frac{\pi(y)\,q(x \mid y)}{\pi(x)\,q(y \mid x)}\right).\]
  3. Set \(X_{t+1} = y\) with probability \(A(x \to y)\); otherwise \(X_{t+1} = x\).

For a symmetric proposal (\(q(y \mid x) = q(x \mid y)\)), the ratio reduces to \(A = \min(1, \pi(y)/\pi(x))\).

The Metropolis–Hastings kernel \(T(x, y) = q(y \mid x) A(x \to y)\) satisfies detailed balance with respect to \(\pi\): \(\pi(x)T(x, y) = \pi(y)T(y, x)\). Hence \(\pi\) is stationary for the chain.

Proof. Direct computation from the definition of \(A\): the min preserves detailed balance because the acceptance ratio is exactly the ratio of forward to backward flux under \(\pi\otimes q\).

Target \(\pi(x) \propto e^{-x^2/2}\). Proposal: \(y = x + \sigma_p \xi\) with \(\xi \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\). Acceptance: \(A = \min(1, e^{(x^2 - y^2)/2})\). For \(\sigma_p \approx 2.4\) the chain has \(\sim 24\%\) acceptance and mixes well — this is the theoretical optimum for a 1D Gaussian target.

8.5 Tuning the proposal

The step size \(\sigma_p\) of a Gaussian random-walk proposal has a sweet spot:

For a Gaussian random-walk Metropolis on a \(d\)-dimensional target with roughly Gaussian shape, the asymptotically-optimal acceptance rate is \(\alpha^* \approx 0.234\), achieved by scaling \(\sigma_p^2 \propto d^{-1}\).

Remark. The rule of thumb “aim for 20–40% acceptance” comes from this result and works surprisingly well across problems. Too-small \(\sigma_p\) gives \(\alpha \to 1\) but the chain crawls; too-large gives \(\alpha \to 0\) and the chain sticks at the current state.

8.6 Practical Metropolis

Suppose we want to sample the posterior of a Bayesian linear regression \(y_i = \boldsymbol\beta^\top \mathbf x_i + \varepsilon_i\) with a Gaussian likelihood and an informative Gaussian prior on \(\boldsymbol\beta\). The unnormalised posterior is \[\pi(\boldsymbol\beta) \propto \exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i - \boldsymbol\beta^\top \mathbf x_i)^2 - \tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\Bigr).\] A Gaussian random-walk Metropolis proposal on \(\boldsymbol\beta\) with \(\sigma_p^2 = 2.4^2/d\) times a preconditioner will typically converge in \(\sim 10^4\) iterations.

8.7 Multi-move / block updates

For high-dimensional targets, updating all coordinates jointly in a random-walk Metropolis has poor scaling. Two remedies:

Update each coordinate \(x_j\) separately, holding the others fixed. Each move accepts or rejects based on the conditional target \(\pi(x_j \mid x_{-j})\).

Component-wise updates are the Gibbs sampler of Chapter 8 in the limit that the conditional proposal is the conditional target.

For a target of the form \(\pi(\mathbf x) \propto e^{-U(\mathbf x)}\), HMC augments the state with a momentum variable and proposes \(\mathbf x \to \mathbf x'\) by running Hamilton’s equations of the augmented system, then does a Metropolis accept/reject on the joint energy. The proposal preserves the target and mixes at a rate that scales as \(O(d^{1/4})\) rather than \(O(d^{-1})\) for random walk.

Remark. HMC / NUTS is the algorithm behind Stan, PyMC and TensorFlow Probability. Its efficient handling of geometry via momentum is why it dominates modern Bayesian inference on continuous parameters.

8.8 Diagnostics

Verifying convergence of an MCMC run is a subtle empirical exercise. The standard tools are:

  • Trace plots of \(f(X_t)\) vs \(t\): should look like white noise around a stationary mean.
  • Effective sample size (ESS): \(n_{\text{eff}} = n / (1 + 2\sum_{k \geq 1}\hat\rho(k))\); ratios below \(0.1\) signal poor mixing.
  • Gelman–Rubin \(\hat R\): run \(M\) chains from over-dispersed starts and compare within-chain and between-chain variance. \(\hat R < 1.01\) is the modern convergence threshold.

Never trust an MCMC estimate from a single chain. Always run at least four in parallel, and inspect \(\hat R\) before quoting a posterior number.

8.9 Exercises

Simulate a Metropolis chain for a 2D banana-shaped target \(\pi(x, y) \propto \exp(-x^2/10 - (y - x^2/10)^2/2)\). Estimate the effective sample size for the second component.

Solution. A random-walk Metropolis with isotropic proposal has ESS \(\sim 10^2\) per \(10^4\) steps because the banana shape defeats isotropic scaling. HMC or preconditioning brings ESS \(\sim 10^3\) or better. This is the standard illustration in the Stan documentation of why HMC dominates for non-elliptical posteriors.

Prove that if \(q(y \mid x) = q(x \mid y)\) then the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability reduces to \(A = \min(1, \pi(y)/\pi(x))\), and verify detailed balance in this simplified form.

Solution. Substituting \(q(y|x) = q(x|y)\) into the Hastings formula makes the \(q\) terms cancel. Detailed balance: \(\pi(x) T(x,y) = \pi(x) q(y|x) \min(1, \pi(y)/\pi(x)) = q(y|x) \min(\pi(x), \pi(y)) = q(x|y) \min(\pi(x), \pi(y)) = \pi(y) T(y,x)\). ✓

8.10 Callouts

Metropolis is not the fastest MCMC algorithm; it is the most general. For specific problems (Gaussian latent variables, hierarchical models), specialised samplers like NUTS or slice-sampling do orders of magnitude better.

The MCMC estimator is biased by the initial burn-in period. Always discard a burn-in and check that discarding more doesn’t change your estimate significantly.

An MCMC chain that “looks converged” in a trace plot may still be missing modes of a multi-modal target. Trace plots detect within-mode stationarity, not between-mode mixing. Multi-modal posteriors need tempered chains or SMC.

Effective sample size is the only number that quantifies MCMC efficiency. Wall-clock time comparisons between samplers are only meaningful when normalised by ESS/sec.
