10  A problem of high-dimensional inference

This chapter is a running example that ties Chapters 7–8 (MCMC, Gibbs) to a realistic Bayesian problem. It mirrors Prof. Allegra’s Chapter 11.

10.1 9.1 Basic model

Suppose we observe \(n\) binary outcomes \(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\), drawn as \(y_i \sim \text{Bern}(\sigma(\theta))\) where \(\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}\) is the logistic function. The parameter \(\theta\) has a Gaussian prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\).

10.1.1 Likelihood and Metropolis approach

The unnormalised posterior is \[\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf y) \propto \exp\bigl(-\theta^2/(2\tau^2)\bigr)\prod_i \sigma(\theta)^{y_i}(1 - \sigma(\theta))^{1 - y_i}.\] There is no conjugate closed form. A Gaussian random-walk Metropolis proposal \(\theta' = \theta + \sigma_p\,\xi\) with \(\xi \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\) and standard acceptance ratio gives a working sampler.

10.1.2 Analytic form of the posterior

Even without conjugacy, the log-posterior admits a closed expression: \[\log \pi(\theta \mid \mathbf y) = -\frac{\theta^2}{2\tau^2} + n_1\,\theta - n\,\log(1 + e^\theta) + \text{const},\] with \(n_1 = \sum_i y_i\). The posterior is unimodal and concave, so a Laplace approximation around its mode gives a good analytic reference for benchmarking MCMC results.

Let \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \log\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf y)\) and \(J = -\partial^2_\theta \log\pi(\hat\theta \mid \mathbf y) > 0\). Then \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf y) \approx \mathcal N(\hat\theta, J^{-1})\) to leading order in \(n\). The mode \(\hat\theta\) solves \(-\hat\theta/\tau^2 + n_1 - n\sigma(\hat\theta) = 0\).

10.1.3 Simple numerical example

Simulate \(n = 200\) observations with true \(\theta = 0.5\) and prior \(\tau = 3\). Metropolis with \(\sigma_p = 0.3\) produces an acceptance rate \(\sim 40\%\) and an effective sample size of \(\sim 4000\) from \(10^4\) iterations. The posterior mean is close to \(0.5\) with standard deviation \(\sim 0.15\), agreeing with the Laplace approximation to 1%.

10.1.4 Gibbs sampling approach

For a scalar \(\theta\) there is nothing to Gibbs-decompose; the algorithm reduces to Metropolis. Gibbs becomes useful in the refined model below.

10.2 9.2 Refined model: hierarchical structure

Extend to \(G\) groups, each with its own signal \(\theta_g\): \[y_{gi} \sim \text{Bern}(\sigma(\theta_g)),\qquad \theta_g \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma_\theta^2),\qquad \mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 10),\ \sigma_\theta \sim \text{HalfNormal}(1) .\] The joint posterior has \(G + 2\) parameters.

10.2.1 Metropolis-within-Gibbs

Alternate:

  1. Update each \(\theta_g\) via a small Metropolis step conditional on \(\{y_{g1}, \ldots\}\) and the current \((\mu, \sigma_\theta)\).
  2. Update \(\mu\) from its conjugate Gaussian posterior given \(\{\theta_g\}\).
  3. Update \(\sigma_\theta\) from its (approximately conjugate) half-Cauchy posterior given \(\{\theta_g - \mu\}\).

This scheme was the dominant sampler for hierarchical Bayesian models before HMC/NUTS became standard.

10.2.2 Gibbs sampling

If we replaced the Bernoulli likelihood by a Gaussian one (e.g. \(y_{gi} \sim \mathcal N(\theta_g, \sigma_y^2)\)), full conjugacy would hold and pure Gibbs (no Metropolis) would sample the entire posterior in closed form. This is the classical example of hierarchical shrinkage — an analytical version of James–Stein estimation.

For small groups \(G\) with few observations each, hierarchical Bayes shrinks group estimates \(\theta_g\) toward the global mean \(\mu\) by exactly the same amount as James–Stein estimation. Bayes gives an operational meaning to shrinkage — via the hierarchical prior on \(\theta_g\).

10.3 Exercises

Derive the Laplace approximation of the basic-model posterior. Explain why it is an asymptotic approximation whose error tends to zero as \(n \to \infty\).

Solution. Compute \(\hat\theta\) numerically as the root of \(-\hat\theta/\tau^2 + n_1 - n\sigma(\hat\theta) = 0\); the second derivative is \(-1/\tau^2 - n\sigma(\hat\theta)(1 - \sigma(\hat\theta))\). As \(n \to \infty\) the posterior variance \(J^{-1} \to 0\) so the distribution concentrates at \(\hat\theta\); higher-order corrections to the Gaussian are \(O(1/n)\), formalised in the Bernstein–von Mises theorem.

Show that in the refined hierarchical model with Gaussian likelihood, the conditional distribution of \(\theta_g\) given the data and hyperparameters is Gaussian. Give its mean and variance.

Solution. \(\pi(\theta_g \mid \ldots) \propto \exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_y^2}\sum_{i}(y_{gi} - \theta_g)^2 - \tfrac{1}{2\sigma_\theta^2}(\theta_g - \mu)^2\bigr)\). Completing the square gives \[\theta_g \mid \ldots \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\frac{n_g \bar y_g / \sigma_y^2 + \mu / \sigma_\theta^2}{n_g/\sigma_y^2 + 1/\sigma_\theta^2},\ \left(n_g/\sigma_y^2 + 1/\sigma_\theta^2\right)^{-1}\right).\] The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the group sample mean and the hyperparameter mean — exactly the shrinkage interpretation.

10.4 Callouts

The high-dimensional-inference chapter is a testing ground for the algorithms of Chapters 7 and 8. Every method (Metropolis, Gibbs, Metropolis-within-Gibbs) should be benchmarked against the Laplace reference before believing its output.

When designing a hierarchical model, always draw a plate diagram of the dependencies before writing code. Half of Bayesian bugs come from a mismatch between the intended model and its implementation.

Gibbs samplers for hierarchical models can mix very slowly when the variance hyperparameter \(\sigma_\theta\) is close to zero (a “funnel” geometry). Reparameterisation (non-centred, i.e. \(\theta_g = \mu + \sigma_\theta z_g\) with \(z_g \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\)) is the standard fix.
