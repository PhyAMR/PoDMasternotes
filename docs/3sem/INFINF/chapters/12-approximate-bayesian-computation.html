13  Approximate Bayesian computation

Every method so far — Metropolis, Gibbs, variational Bayes, HMC — requires the likelihood \(f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf x)\) to be evaluable (and HMC, its gradient). In many scientific problems it is not: the likelihood may have no closed form, or be too expensive to compute. Often, however, the model is easy to simulate: given parameters \(\mathbf x\) we can generate synthetic data \(\mathbf y \sim f(\cdot\mid\mathbf x)\). Approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) performs Bayesian inference using simulations alone, by replacing the likelihood with a comparison between simulated and observed data.

13.1 Intractable likelihoods

Typical situations:

  • Latent dynamics. The observed data are a noisy function of a hidden stochastic process; the likelihood is a sum (or integral) over all hidden trajectories.
  • Implicit models. The model is a simulator — an agent-based model in ecology or epidemiology, a coalescent process in population genetics, a stochastic simulation of a physical system — with no density written down anywhere.
  • Intractable normalisation. The likelihood is known up to a parameter-dependent constant (e.g. a partition function), so Metropolis ratios cannot be computed.

A binary hidden state \(z_t \in \{0, 1\}\) evolves as a Markov chain that switches with probability \(x\) at each step: \[P(z_{t+1} \mid z_t) = \begin{pmatrix}1 - x & x \\ x & 1 - x\end{pmatrix}.\] We cannot see \(z_t\); we measure \(y_t\), which equals \(z_t\) with probability \(\gamma\) and is flipped with probability \(1 - \gamma\): \[p_{Y|Z}(0\mid0) = p_{Y|Z}(1\mid1) = \gamma, \qquad p_{Y|Z}(1\mid0) = p_{Y|Z}(0\mid1) = 1 - \gamma .\] We want to infer the switching rate \(x\) from an observed sequence \(y_1, \ldots, y_T\).

The likelihood is a sum over all \(2^T\) hidden paths, \[f(\mathbf y \mid x) = \sum_{z_1, \ldots, z_T}P(z_1)\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}P(z_{t+1}\mid z_t)\prod_{t=1}^T p_{Y|Z}(y_t\mid z_t),\] because the observations at different times are correlated through the hidden chain. (For this small model the sum can be organised recursively — the forward algorithm of HMMs — but for richer hidden dynamics no such shortcut exists.) Simulating data, on the other hand, is trivial: draw \(z_1\), flip it with probability \(x\) at each step, and corrupt each \(z_t\) with probability \(1 - \gamma\).

13.2 Exact rejection ABC for discrete data

Suppose the data take values in a discrete set \(\mathcal D\). Consider the following scheme, repeated to produce samples \(x_1, \ldots, x_N\):

  1. Draw \(x' \sim f_{\text{prior}}(x)\).
  2. Simulate \(\mathbf y' \sim f(\cdot \mid x')\).
  3. If \(\mathbf y' = \mathbf y\) (the observed data), accept: \(x_i = x'\). Otherwise go back to step 1.

The accepted values are an i.i.d. sample from the exact posterior \(f(x \mid \mathbf y)\).

Proof. The probability density of proposing \(x\) and accepting it is \[\sum_{\mathbf y' \in \mathcal D}f_{\text{prior}}(x)\,f(\mathbf y' \mid x)\,\chi(\mathbf y' = \mathbf y) = f_{\text{prior}}(x)\,f(\mathbf y \mid x) \propto f(x \mid \mathbf y).\] The likelihood is never evaluated: it is sampled, through the frequency with which \(x\) reproduces the data. The overall acceptance probability is \(\int f_{\text{prior}}(x)f(\mathbf y\mid x)\,dx = f(\mathbf y)\), the evidence.

Observed: \(k = 7\) heads in \(n = 10\) tosses. Prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal U(0,1)\). ABC: draw \(\theta'\), toss a \(\theta'\)-coin ten times, accept if the result has exactly 7 heads. (Matching the number of heads rather than the exact sequence is legitimate because \(k\) is a sufficient statistic — see below.) The accepted \(\theta\)’s are distributed as \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\), the exact posterior. The acceptance rate is \(f(k = 7) = \int_0^1\binom{10}{7}\theta^7(1-\theta)^3\,d\theta = 1/11\). With the full sequence it would be \(1/(11\binom{10}{7}) = 1/1320\) — same posterior, 120 times slower.

Exact matching becomes hopeless as the data grow: the acceptance rate is the evidence, which decays exponentially with the size of the data set. For continuous data it is exactly zero, since \(P(\mathbf y' = \mathbf y) = 0\).

13.3 The ABC algorithm

Two approximations make ABC work in general: compare summary statistics instead of raw data, and accept approximate matches.

Choose a summary statistic \(\eta : \mathcal D \to \mathbb R^s\), a distance (e.g. Euclidean) and a tolerance \(\epsilon > 0\). To generate each sample:

  1. draw \(x' \sim f_{\text{prior}}(x)\);
  2. simulate \(\mathbf y' \sim f(\cdot\mid x')\);
  3. if \(\|\eta(\mathbf y') - \eta(\mathbf y)\| < \epsilon\) accept \(x_i = x'\); otherwise go back to step 1.

The accepted pairs \((x, \mathbf y')\) are distributed as \[f_\epsilon(x, \mathbf y' \mid \mathbf y) = \frac{f_{\text{prior}}(x)\,f(\mathbf y'\mid x)\,\chi\bigl(\|\eta(\mathbf y') - \eta(\mathbf y)\| < \epsilon\bigr)}{\int dx\,d\mathbf y'\,f_{\text{prior}}(x)\,f(\mathbf y'\mid x)\,\chi\bigl(\|\eta(\mathbf y') - \eta(\mathbf y)\| < \epsilon\bigr)},\] so the samples of \(x\) come from the ABC posterior \[f_\epsilon(x \mid \mathbf y) = \int d\mathbf y'\,f_\epsilon(x, \mathbf y'\mid\mathbf y) \propto f_{\text{prior}}(x)\;P\bigl(\|\eta(\mathbf Y') - \eta(\mathbf y)\| < \epsilon \;\big|\; x\bigr).\] The exact likelihood \(f(\mathbf y\mid x)\) has been replaced by the probability that a simulation at \(x\) lands within \(\epsilon\) of the observed summaries. The basic intuition is that

a representative (enough) summary statistic \(\eta\) together with a small (enough) tolerance \(\epsilon\) produces a good (enough) approximation, \(f_\epsilon(x\mid\mathbf y) \approx f(x\mid\mathbf y)\).

Two sources of error must be kept in mind:

  1. Tolerance. As \(\epsilon \to 0\), \(f_\epsilon(x\mid\mathbf y) \to f(x \mid \eta(\mathbf y))\), the posterior given the summaries. For \(\epsilon > 0\) it is broader: ABC with tolerance \(\epsilon\) is exact inference for a model in which the observed summaries are corrupted by uniform noise of size \(\epsilon\).
  2. Insufficiency. \(f(x\mid\eta(\mathbf y))\) equals \(f(x\mid\mathbf y)\) only if \(\eta\) is sufficient; otherwise information is lost, and the ABC posterior is wider (or even biased).

13.3.1 Summary statistics and sufficiency

\(\eta(\mathbf y)\) is sufficient for \(x\) if \(f(\mathbf y \mid x)\) depends on \(\mathbf y\) only through \(\eta(\mathbf y)\) (more precisely, \(f(\mathbf y\mid x) = g(\eta(\mathbf y), x)h(\mathbf y)\) — Fisher–Neyman factorisation). Equivalently, \(f(x\mid\mathbf y) = f(x\mid\eta(\mathbf y))\) for every prior; in information-theoretic terms, \(I(X; \mathbf Y) = I(X; \eta(\mathbf Y))\) — equality in the data-processing inequality of Chapter 2.

For i.i.d. Bernoulli data the number of successes is sufficient; for i.i.d. Gaussian data with unknown mean and variance, \((\sum y_i, \sum y_i^2)\) is. For complex simulators sufficient statistics of low dimension rarely exist, and \(\eta\) is chosen by insight — moments, quantiles, autocorrelations, counts of characteristic events — or learned by regression or neural networks.

There is a trade-off: adding statistics reduces information loss but increases the dimension \(s\), and the acceptance rate of \(\|\eta' - \eta\| < \epsilon\) decays like \(\epsilon^s\) — the curse of dimensionality strikes again.

For the hidden Markov model above, natural summaries of the observed sequence are

  • the fraction of ones, \(\bar y\) (uninformative here: by symmetry it is \(\approx 1/2\) for any \(x\));
  • the number of switches \(s(\mathbf y) = \sum_t \chi(y_{t+1} \neq y_t)\), which increases with \(x\);
  • the lag-1 autocorrelation of \(y\).

Since the hidden state has lag-1 autocorrelation \(1 - 2x\) and each noisy observation multiplies it by \((2\gamma - 1)\), the expected switching frequency of \(y\) is \(\mathbb E[s]/(T-1) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 - (1 - 2x)(2\gamma - 1)^2\bigr)\). ABC with \(\eta = s(\mathbf y)/(T - 1)\) therefore concentrates around \(x\) values that reproduce the observed switching rate. If \(\gamma = 1/2\) the data carry no information about \(x\) and the ABC posterior correctly reproduces the prior.

13.4 Beyond rejection: ABC-MCMC and ABC-SMC

Plain rejection wastes most simulations on parameters drawn from the prior, far from the posterior. Two standard improvements:

From the current \(x\), propose \(x' \sim q(\cdot\mid x)\), simulate \(\mathbf y' \sim f(\cdot\mid x')\), and accept with probability \[\min\Bigl\{1, \frac{f_{\text{prior}}(x')\,q(x\mid x')}{f_{\text{prior}}(x)\,q(x'\mid x)}\Bigr\}\cdot\chi\bigl(\|\eta(\mathbf y') - \eta(\mathbf y)\| < \epsilon\bigr).\] This is Metropolis–Hastings on the augmented target \(f_\epsilon(x, \mathbf y'\mid\mathbf y)\), using the simulator as part of the proposal; the intractable likelihoods cancel. Its stationary distribution is \(f_\epsilon(x \mid \mathbf y)\).

Run a population of particles through a decreasing sequence of tolerances \(\epsilon_1 > \epsilon_2 > \cdots > \epsilon_T\). At each stage, resample particles from the previous population, perturb them, keep those whose simulations fall within the new tolerance, and reweight to correct for the perturbation. The population is gradually guided from the prior to the region of high posterior mass.

Remark (Simulation-based inference). Modern neural methods train a network on simulated pairs \((x, \mathbf y)\) to approximate the posterior \(f(x\mid\mathbf y)\) (neural posterior estimation), the likelihood, or the likelihood ratio directly. They can be seen as ABC with learned summary statistics and a learned (variational, cf. Chapter 10) posterior, and are far more simulation-efficient in high dimension.

13.5 Applications

Coalescent models generate gene genealogies whose likelihood involves an intractable sum over genealogies, but which are easy to simulate. ABC was developed in this field (Tavaré et al. 1997; Pritchard et al. 1999; Beaumont et al. 2002) to infer population sizes, migration rates and split times from DNA data — the same kind of question as the grey-seal problem of Chapter 9, but with demographic history included.

Stochastic compartmental and agent-based models of epidemics and of population dynamics are straightforward to simulate but have intractable likelihoods, especially with partial observation. ABC-SMC and neural simulation-based inference are routinely used to fit them to incidence time series.

13.6 Exercises

Show that ABC rejection with \(\epsilon = 0\) has acceptance probability zero for continuous data, and that for discrete data its acceptance probability equals the evidence \(f(\mathbf y)\).

Solution. For continuous \(\mathbf y\), \(P(\mathbf y' = \mathbf y \mid x) = 0\) for every \(x\). For discrete data, \(P(\text{accept}) = \int dx\,f_{\text{prior}}(x)P(\mathbf y' = \mathbf y\mid x) = \int dx\,f_{\text{prior}}(x)f(\mathbf y\mid x) = f(\mathbf y)\). (This even gives a — very inefficient — Monte Carlo estimator of the evidence.)

For \(\mathbf y \mid \theta \sim \mathcal N(\theta\mathbf 1, I_n)\) with prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\), show that ABC with \(\eta = \bar y\) recovers the exact posterior as \(\epsilon \to 0\), and compute the ABC posterior for finite \(\epsilon\) when the uniform window is replaced by a Gaussian kernel of width \(\epsilon\).

Solution. \(\bar y\) is sufficient (\(f(\mathbf y\mid\theta) \propto e^{-n(\bar y - \theta)^2/2}\times(\text{terms without }\theta)\)), so \(f(\theta\mid\bar y) = f(\theta\mid\mathbf y)\) and the \(\epsilon \to 0\) limit is exact. With a Gaussian kernel, the ABC “likelihood” is \(P(\bar y' \approx \bar y\mid\theta) \propto \mathcal N(\bar y; \theta, 1/n + \epsilon^2)\): the ABC posterior is the exact posterior for a model with noise variance inflated from \(1/n\) to \(1/n + \epsilon^2\), i.e. \(\mathcal N\bigl(\frac{\bar y/(1/n + \epsilon^2)}{1/\tau^2 + 1/(1/n + \epsilon^2)}, (1/\tau^2 + 1/(1/n + \epsilon^2))^{-1}\bigr)\). The tolerance should be small compared to the statistical uncertainty \(1/\sqrt n\).

In the noisy HMM, why is \(\bar y\) a useless summary statistic for \(x\)? Suggest a statistic and explain how it depends on \(x\) and \(\gamma\).

Solution. The hidden chain is symmetric, so its stationary distribution is \((1/2, 1/2)\) for every \(x\), and the symmetric noise preserves this: \(\mathbb E[\bar y] = 1/2\) regardless of \(x\). The switching frequency of \(\mathbf y\) depends on \(x\): \(\mathbb E[s]/(T-1) = \tfrac12[1 - (1-2x)(2\gamma-1)^2]\). It increases with \(x\) (for \(x < 1/2\)) and its sensitivity to \(x\) is proportional to \((2\gamma - 1)^2\), vanishing for completely noisy measurements.

13.7 Callouts

ABC turns a simulator into an inference engine. It is the natural tool whenever the model is defined by a simulation rather than by a density.

Validate an ABC pipeline on synthetic data generated with known parameters, and check that the posterior is stable as \(\epsilon\) decreases and as summary statistics are added or removed.

ABC posteriors are approximations with two uncontrolled errors — tolerance and insufficiency. Always report \(\epsilon\), the summaries used and the acceptance rate.

Show that ABC rejection with $\epsilon = 0$ has acceptance probability zero for continuous data, and that for discrete data its acceptance probability equals the evidence $f(\mathbf y)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The hidden chain is symmetric, so its stationary distribution is $(1/2, 1/2)$ for every $x$, and the symmetric noise preserves this: $\mathbb E[\bar y] = 1/2$ regardless of $x$. The switching frequency of $\mathbf y$ depends on $x$: $\mathbb E[s]/(T-1) = \tfrac12[1 - (1-2x)(2\gamma-1)^2]$. It increases with $x$ (for $x < 1/2$) and its sensitivity to $x$ is proportional to $(2\gamma - 1)^2$, vanishing for completely noisy measurements.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
ABC turns a simulator into an inference engine. It is the natural tool whenever the model is *defined* by a simulation rather than by a density.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
Validate an ABC pipeline on synthetic data generated with known parameters, and check that the posterior is stable as $\epsilon$ decreases and as summary statistics are added or removed.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
ABC posteriors are approximations with two uncontrolled errors — tolerance and insufficiency. Always report $\epsilon$, the summaries used and the acceptance rate.
:::
