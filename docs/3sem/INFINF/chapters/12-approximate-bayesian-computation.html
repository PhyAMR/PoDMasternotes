13  Approximate Bayesian computation

MCMC and VB all require the likelihood \(p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\theta)\) to be evaluable. What if the likelihood is only simulable — i.e. we can generate data from the model but cannot compute its density? That is the world of approximate Bayesian computation (ABC). This chapter mirrors Prof. Allegra’s Chapter 14.

13.1 12.1 Motivation: intractable likelihoods

Many scientific models are defined by a simulator: a stochastic dynamical system that emits synthetic data given parameters. The associated likelihood may be:

  • an intractable marginal (e.g. a hidden-state model where the joint is tractable but the observed marginal is not),
  • an unnormalised density with an intractable partition function (e.g. certain physics-inspired models),
  • entirely implicit (e.g. agent-based simulations in ecology and epidemiology).

For such models MCMC’s Metropolis acceptance ratio \(\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)\) cannot be computed. ABC solves this by working directly with simulations.

13.2 12.2 ABC rejection sampling

Given observed data \(\mathbf y_{obs}\), prior \(\pi(\theta)\), simulator \(S(\theta)\), distance \(d(\cdot, \cdot)\), and tolerance \(\epsilon\):

Repeat until \(N\) samples are accepted:

  1. Draw \(\theta^{(i)} \sim \pi(\theta)\).
  2. Simulate \(\mathbf y^{(i)} = S(\theta^{(i)})\).
  3. If \(d(\mathbf y^{(i)}, \mathbf y_{obs}) < \epsilon\), accept \(\theta^{(i)}\).

The accepted samples are distributed as \(\pi_\epsilon(\theta \mid \mathbf y_{obs})\), which converges to \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf y_{obs})\) as \(\epsilon \to 0\).

Remark. ABC replaces the (unavailable) likelihood ratio by an indicator on distance — a very general trick. Its downside is that acceptance is \(\ll 1\) when \(\epsilon\) is small, so ABC-rejection is impractical for anything but toy problems.

13.3 12.3 ABC-MCMC and ABC-SMC

Two improvements make ABC practical:

Combine ABC with a Metropolis proposal on \(\theta\): propose \(\theta' \sim q(\theta' \mid \theta)\), simulate \(\mathbf y'\), and accept with probability \[A = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta \mid \theta')}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta' \mid \theta)}\right)\cdot \mathbf 1[d(\mathbf y', \mathbf y_{obs}) < \epsilon] .\] The chain concentrates on regions of the parameter space where simulations resemble the data.

Sequential Monte Carlo variant: start with \(\epsilon_1\) large and gradually anneal \(\epsilon_t \searrow \epsilon_{final}\), reweighting particles as \(\epsilon\) shrinks. Widely used in population genetics and epidemiology.

13.4 12.4 Summary statistics

For high-dimensional data \(\mathbf y\), the raw distance \(d(\mathbf y', \mathbf y_{obs})\) is nearly always huge; ABC becomes uninformative. The standard remedy is to summarise \(\mathbf y\) by a low-dimensional statistic \(T(\mathbf y)\) and require \(d(T(\mathbf y'), T(\mathbf y_{obs})) < \epsilon\).

If \(T\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\), ABC on \(T\) has the same posterior as ABC on the raw data, i.e. no information is lost. In practice sufficient statistics rarely exist; carefully-chosen summary statistics (empirical moments, quantiles, autocorrelations) are used instead.

Remark (Learned summary statistics). Modern ABC uses neural networks to learn near-sufficient summary statistics automatically. Neural posterior estimation (Papamakarios & Murray, 2016) trains a conditional density \(q_\phi(\theta \mid T(\mathbf y))\) to match the true posterior, blending ABC with variational Bayes.

13.5 12.5 Applications

The coalescent model produces gene genealogies whose likelihood is intractable but easily simulable. ABC is the standard tool for inferring demographic parameters (population size, migration rates, split times) from DNA data. Beaumont et al. (2002) is the seminal reference.

Agent-based models of infectious-disease spread are intractable but simulable. ABC-SMC was used during COVID-19 to fit parameters of the UK’s SPI-M models given hospitalisation time series.

13.6 Exercises

Show that ABC rejection with \(\epsilon = 0\) (and continuous data) has acceptance probability zero.

Solution. For continuous \(\mathbf y\), \(P(\mathbf y' = \mathbf y_{obs}) = 0\). The Dirac-delta indicator is never triggered. Hence any positive tolerance \(\epsilon > 0\) is a necessary approximation for continuous data.

For a Gaussian likelihood \(\mathbf y \mid \theta \sim \mathcal N(\theta, I)\) with a Gaussian prior, show that ABC with sufficient statistic \(T = \bar y\) recovers the exact posterior in the limit \(\epsilon \to 0\).

Solution. \(\bar y\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\). As \(\epsilon \to 0\) the ABC posterior converges to the true posterior conditional on \(\bar y = \bar y_{obs}\), which by sufficiency equals the true posterior conditional on the full data.

13.7 Callouts

ABC is the tool for likelihood-free inference. Every scientific problem where a simulator is the model’s definition (not a computational shortcut) is a candidate.

When designing an ABC study, invest time in choosing summary statistics. The success of ABC hinges on their sufficiency; a bad choice makes the posterior arbitrarily far from the truth.

ABC posteriors are biased estimators of the true posterior. Studies that report ABC results should always report the tolerance \(\epsilon\) and, ideally, a robustness check as \(\epsilon\) is decreased.

Simulation-based inference (SBI), including modern neural variants, has largely superseded classical ABC for high-dimensional problems. Consult the sbi Python library for state-of-the-art tools.
