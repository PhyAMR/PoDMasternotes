11  Variational Bayes

MCMC is asymptotically exact but slow. Variational Bayes (VB) trades exactness for speed: it approximates a posterior by the closest member of a tractable family, converting inference into optimisation. This chapter mirrors Prof. Allegra’s Chapter 12.

11.1 10.1 The variational principle

Given a target posterior \(p(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)\) we cannot compute directly, choose a tractable family \(\mathcal Q = \{q_\phi(\mathbf z)\}\) parameterised by \(\phi\), and find the member closest to \(p\) in KL divergence: \[\phi^* = \arg\min_\phi D\bigl(q_\phi(\mathbf z) \,\|\, p(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)\bigr) .\]

The KL divergence \(D(q \| p)\) equals \(\log p(\mathbf x) - \mathcal L(\phi)\) where \[\mathcal L(\phi) = \mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)] - \mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log q_\phi(\mathbf z)]\] is the evidence lower bound. Since \(\log p(\mathbf x)\) does not depend on \(\phi\), maximising \(\mathcal L\) is equivalent to minimising the KL.

The ELBO decomposes as \(\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)] - D(q_\phi(\mathbf z) \| p(\mathbf z))\) — a reconstruction fit minus a complexity penalty.

11.2 10.2 Mean-field approximation

The simplest tractable family assumes independence across coordinates:

The mean-field family on \(\mathbb R^d\) is \[q(\mathbf z) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} q_j(z_j) .\]

Under mean field, the optimal factor for coordinate \(j\) has an explicit form.

The mean-field ELBO is maximised by iterating \[q_j^*(z_j) \propto \exp\!\bigl(\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)]\bigr),\] i.e. each factor’s log is the expected joint log-density under all other factors. This is coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI) — the analogue of Gibbs sampling but with expectations replacing samples.

11.3 10.3 Example: one Gaussian

Model: \(x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, 1/\lambda)\) with priors \(\mu \sim \mathcal N(0, 1/\lambda_0)\), \(\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)\). Mean-field factorisation \(q(\mu, \lambda) = q(\mu)q(\lambda)\):

  • \(q^*(\mu) = \mathcal N\bigl(m_n,\ 1/(\lambda_n + \lambda_0)\bigr)\) with \(\lambda_n = \mathbb E[\lambda] \cdot n\) and \(m_n\) a weighted mean.
  • \(q^*(\lambda) = \text{Gamma}(a_n, b_n)\) with \(a_n = a_0 + n/2\) and \(b_n\) a function of \(\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu)^2]\).

CAVI alternates between the two updates. Converges in \(\sim 10\) iterations to a solution that closely matches the exact NIG posterior.

11.4 10.4 Example: mixture distributions

For a \(K\)-component mixture model, mean-field VB introduces a variational cluster assignment \(q(z_i = k)\) and Gaussian variational distributions on each mean \(\mu_k\). The result is the variational EM algorithm — a Bayesian extension of standard EM that avoids over-fitting.

11.5 10.5 Variational autoencoders (VAE)

The most influential modern application of variational Bayes is the variational autoencoder (Kingma & Welling, 2014):

A VAE models data \(\mathbf x\) as generated by a latent code \(\mathbf z\) through a decoder network \(p_\theta(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)\) with \(\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\). The variational posterior is parameterised by an encoder network \(q_\phi(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x) = \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_\phi(\mathbf x),\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_\phi(\mathbf x))\). Training jointly optimises the ELBO w.r.t. \((\phi, \theta)\).

The reparameterisation trick \(\mathbf z = \boldsymbol\mu_\phi + \boldsymbol\Sigma_\phi^{1/2} \boldsymbol\epsilon\) with \(\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\) makes the ELBO differentiable through the sampling step, so stochastic gradients propagate cleanly through both networks.

Remark. VAEs revolutionised generative modelling: for the first time a single differentiable objective produced samples of natural images, speech, and molecules. Their descendants (β-VAE, VQ-VAE, hierarchical VAE) remain competitive with diffusion models for certain modalities.

11.6 Exercises

Derive the ELBO decomposition \(\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)] - D(q \| p(\mathbf z))\).

Solution. \(\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z) + \log p(\mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)] - D(q \| p(\mathbf z))\).

Show that mean-field VB underestimates posterior variance for correlated targets. Give a two-dimensional Gaussian example.

Solution. For a Gaussian target with covariance \(\Sigma\) and mean-field family \(q = \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol\sigma^2))\), the optimal variance of \(q_j\) is \(1/(\Sigma^{-1})_{jj}\), which is less than \(\Sigma_{jj}\) whenever off-diagonal elements are non-zero. The intuition: mean field cannot capture correlations, so it compensates by inflating the “certainty” of each coordinate.

11.7 Callouts

Variational Bayes is biased by construction: the mean-field family cannot represent the true posterior in general. For applications where posterior shape matters (uncertainty quantification, decision theory), use HMC; for applications where posterior mean is enough (predictions, model comparison), VB is fine.

When implementing CAVI, initialise the variational parameters using a rough clustering of the data. Random initialisation often gets stuck in local ELBO maxima.

The mean-field variance under-estimation is a serious limitation for tasks like Bayesian neural networks where calibrated uncertainty is the whole point. Use normalising flows or low-rank variational families to escape it.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Derive the ELBO decomposition $\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)] - D(q \| p(\mathbf z))$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z) + \log p(\mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)] - D(q \| p(\mathbf z))$.
:::
