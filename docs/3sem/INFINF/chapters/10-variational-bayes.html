11  Variational Bayes

MCMC is asymptotically exact but can be slow, and it is hard to know when it has converged. Variational Bayes (VB) trades exactness for speed: it approximates the posterior by the closest member of a tractable family of distributions, turning inference into optimisation. The measure of closeness is — once again — the KL divergence of Chapter 2, and the objective is a lower bound on the log-evidence. This chapter derives the variational principle, the mean-field update equations, works out two examples in full (a Gaussian, and the mixture model of Chapter 8), and ends with the variational autoencoder, where the same bound trains deep generative models.

11.1 The evidence lower bound

Let \(\mathbf y\) be the data and \(\mathbf z\) all unknowns (parameters and latent variables). For any distribution \(q(\mathbf z)\), \[\begin{aligned} \log f(\mathbf y) &= \int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log f(\mathbf y) = \int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\Bigl[\log\frac{f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)}{q(\mathbf z)} + \log\frac{q(\mathbf z)\,f(\mathbf y)}{f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)}\Bigr] \\ &= \underbrace{\int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log\frac{f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)}{q(\mathbf z)}}_{\mathcal L(q)} + \underbrace{\int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log\frac{q(\mathbf z)}{f(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y)}}_{D_{KL}(q \,\|\, f(\cdot \mid \mathbf y))}, \end{aligned}\] using \(f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)/f(\mathbf y) = f(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y)\).

\[\log f(\mathbf y) = \mathcal L(q) + D_{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf z)\,\|\,f(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y)\bigr), \qquad \mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)] - \mathbb E_q[\log q(\mathbf z)].\] Since \(D_{KL} \geq 0\), \(\mathcal L(q) \leq \log f(\mathbf y)\) for every \(q\), with equality iff \(q\) is the exact posterior.

The left-hand side does not depend on \(q\), so maximising the ELBO over \(q\) is the same as minimising \(D_{KL}(q\|f(\cdot\mid\mathbf y))\): we force \(q\) to be as close as possible to the posterior. Crucially, \(\mathcal L\) involves only the joint \(f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\) (likelihood × prior), never the intractable evidence. The VB recipe is:

choose a family \(\mathcal Q\) of tractable distributions, and find \(q^* = \arg\max_{q\in\mathcal Q}\mathcal L(q)\).

Two other readings of the ELBO are useful:

  • \(\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)] + H[q]\): an “energy” term rewarding \(q\) for sitting where the joint is large, plus an entropy term rewarding it for spreading out — exactly a (negative) variational free energy of statistical physics, with \(\log f(\mathbf y)\) playing the role of \(-F = \log Z\).
  • \(\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z)] - D_{KL}(q(\mathbf z)\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z))\): data fit minus a penalty for departing from the prior (used for the VAE below).

Remark (Which KL?). VB minimises \(D(q\|p)\), the “reverse” KL. Since \(D(q\|p) = \int q\log(q/p)\), it is huge wherever \(q > 0\) but \(p \approx 0\): \(q\) is pushed to avoid regions where the posterior is small. The result is mode-seeking and typically under-dispersed: for a multimodal posterior, \(q\) locks onto one mode; for a correlated posterior, \(q\) is too narrow. The “forward” KL \(D(p\|q)\) would be mass-covering instead, but it needs expectations under \(p\) — which is what we cannot compute.

11.2 The mean-field approximation

The most common choice is to assume that \(q\) factorises over groups of variables, \[q(\mathbf z) = \prod_i q_i(\mathbf z_i),\] with no assumption on the form of each factor. This is the mean-field approximation, named after the physics approximation in which each spin feels only the average field of the others.

11.2.1 Derivation of the update

Maximise \(\mathcal L\) over one factor \(q_j\), keeping the others fixed. Separating the terms that involve \(q_j\), \[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L(q) &= \int d\mathbf z\,\prod_i q_i(\mathbf z_i)\Bigl[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y) - \sum_i\log q_i(\mathbf z_i)\Bigr] \\ &= \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j(\mathbf z_j)\Bigl[\int\prod_{i\neq j}d\mathbf z_i\,q_i(\mathbf z_i)\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\Bigr] - \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j\log q_j + \text{const} \\ &= \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j(\mathbf z_j)\log\tilde f(\mathbf z_j, \mathbf y) - \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j(\mathbf z_j)\log q_j(\mathbf z_j) + \text{const}, \end{aligned}\] where “const” does not depend on \(q_j\) and we defined the distribution \(\tilde f\) by \[\log\tilde f(\mathbf z_j, \mathbf y) = \mathbb E_{i\neq j}[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)] + \text{const},\] the log-joint averaged over all other factors. Then \[\mathcal L(q) = -D_{KL}\bigl(q_j\,\|\,\tilde f\bigr) + \text{const},\] which is maximised by \(q_j = \tilde f\).

For fixed \(q_i\), \(i \neq j\), the optimal factor is \[\log q_j^*(\mathbf z_j) = \mathbb E_{i\neq j}\bigl[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\bigr] + \text{const}, \qquad q_j^*(\mathbf z_j) = \frac{\exp\bigl(\mathbb E_{i\neq j}[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)]\bigr)}{\int d\mathbf z_j\exp\bigl(\mathbb E_{i\neq j}[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)]\bigr)} .\]

Recipe: write the log of the joint over all hidden and observed variables, keep only the terms involving \(\mathbf z_j\), take the expectation over all other factors, and recognise the resulting functional form (normalisation is usually read off at the end).

The equations for the different \(j\) are coupled — each right-hand side depends on the other factors — so they are solved iteratively: initialise all factors and cycle through them, updating each in turn (coordinate-ascent variational inference, CAVI). Each update maximises \(\mathcal L\) with respect to one factor (\(\mathcal L\) is concave in each \(q_j\) separately), so \(\mathcal L\) increases monotonically and the iteration converges — to a local maximum, which may depend on the initialisation.

Remark (VB vs Gibbs). Compare with the Gibbs sampler of Chapter 8. Gibbs updates \(\mathbf z_j\) by drawing from \(f(\mathbf z_j \mid \mathbf z_{-j}, \mathbf y) \propto \exp\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\) evaluated at the current sample of the others. Mean-field VB updates the distribution \(q_j \propto \exp\mathbb E_{-j}\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\) using the average over the others. With conjugate models the two have the same functional forms; VB replaces random draws by expectations.

11.2.2 A correlated Gaussian: the variance underestimate

Let the target be \(f(\mathbf z) = \mathcal N(\mathbf z; \boldsymbol\mu, \Lambda^{-1})\) with precision matrix \(\Lambda\), and \(q = q_1(z_1)q_2(z_2)\). Then \[\log q_1^*(z_1) = \mathbb E_{z_2}\Bigl[-\tfrac12\Lambda_{11}(z_1 - \mu_1)^2 - \Lambda_{12}(z_1 - \mu_1)(z_2 - \mu_2)\Bigr] + \text{const},\] which is quadratic in \(z_1\), so \(q_1^*\) is Gaussian — this was derived, not assumed: \[q_1^* = \mathcal N\Bigl(m_1,\ \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Bigr), \qquad m_1 = \mu_1 - \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Lambda_{12}(m_2 - \mu_2),\] and symmetrically for \(q_2\). The self-consistent solution is \(m_1 = \mu_1\), \(m_2 = \mu_2\): the means are exact. But the variances are \(1/\Lambda_{11}\), the conditional variances, smaller than the true marginal variances \((\Lambda^{-1})_{11}\). For unit variances and correlation \(\rho\), \(q\) has variance \(1 - \rho^2\) against the true \(1\): for \(\rho = 0.9\), the mean-field standard deviation is \(0.44\) instead of \(1\). Mean field “fits inside” the elongated posterior ellipse.

11.3 Example: one Gaussian with unknown mean and precision

Data \(x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^{-1})\), \(i = 1..n\), with \(\tau\) the precision. The likelihood is \[f(\mathbf x \mid \mu, \tau) = \Bigl(\frac{\tau}{2\pi}\Bigr)^{n/2}\exp\Bigl[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2\Bigr].\] The conjugate prior is normal–gamma: \[\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0): f(\tau) = \frac{b_0^{a_0}}{\Gamma(a_0)}\tau^{a_0 - 1}e^{-b_0\tau}, \qquad \mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal N\bigl(\mu_0, (\lambda_0\tau)^{-1}\bigr): f(\mu \mid \tau) = \Bigl(\frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2\pi}\Bigr)^{1/2}e^{-\frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2}.\] The exact posterior is again normal–gamma — but not factorised (\(\mu\) and \(\tau\) are dependent). We approximate it with \(q(\mu, \tau) = q_\mu(\mu)q_\tau(\tau)\).

Factor for \(\mu\). Keeping the terms with \(\mu\), \[\log q_\mu^*(\mu) = \mathbb E_\tau\Bigl[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 - \frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] + \text{const} = -\frac{\mathbb E[\tau]}{2}\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] + \text{const}.\] Completing the square, \(q_\mu^* = \mathcal N(\mu', \lambda'^{-1})\) with \[\mu' = \frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + \sum_i x_i}{\lambda_0 + n} = \frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + n\bar x}{\lambda_0 + n}, \qquad \lambda' = (\lambda_0 + n)\,\mathbb E[\tau].\]

Factor for \(\tau\). Keeping the terms with \(\tau\) (note \(n/2\) from the likelihood, \(1/2\) from the prior on \(\mu\), \(a_0 - 1\) from the prior on \(\tau\)), \[\log q_\tau^*(\tau) = \Bigl(a_0 - 1 + \frac{n + 1}{2}\Bigr)\log\tau - \tau\Bigl(b_0 + \frac12\mathbb E_\mu\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr]\Bigr) + \text{const},\] so \(q_\tau^* = \text{Gamma}(a', b')\) with \[a' = a_0 + \frac{n + 1}{2}, \qquad b' = b_0 + \frac12\mathbb E_\mu\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr].\] The required moments under \(q_\mu = \mathcal N(\mu', \lambda'^{-1})\) are \(\mathbb E[\mu] = \mu'\) and \(\mathbb E[\mu^2] = \mu'^2 + \lambda'^{-1}\), giving \[\mathbb E_\mu\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] = \sum_i(x_i - \mu')^2 + \lambda_0(\mu' - \mu_0)^2 + \frac{n + \lambda_0}{\lambda'} ,\] and \(\mathbb E[\tau] = a'/b'\) under the Gamma.

Iteration. \(\mu'\) and \(a'\) do not change; only \(\lambda'\) and \(b'\) are coupled. Initialise \(\mathbb E[\tau]\), then alternate \[\lambda' = (\lambda_0 + n)\frac{a'}{b'}, \qquad b' = b_0 + \frac12\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu')^2 + \lambda_0(\mu' - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{n + \lambda_0}{\lambda'} .\]

With \(a_0 = b_0 = \lambda_0 = 0\): \(\mu' = \bar x\), \(a' = (n + 1)/2\). Substituting \(\lambda' = n a'/b'\) into the \(b'\) equation, \(b' = \tfrac12 n s^2 + \tfrac12 b'/a'\) with \(s^2 = \frac1n\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\), whose fixed point is \(b' = \frac{n s^2 a'}{2a' - 1} = \frac{(n + 1)s^2}{2}\). Then \(\mathbb E[\tau] = a'/b' = 1/s^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}_q[\mu] = 1/\lambda' = s^2/n\): the familiar maximum-likelihood variance estimate and the standard error of the mean. The iteration converges in a handful of steps from any starting \(\mathbb E[\tau]\), since the map \(b' \mapsto \tfrac12ns^2 + b'/(2a')\) is a contraction with factor \(1/(n + 1)\).

11.4 Example: mixture distributions

Return to the Gaussian mixture of Chapter 8 (known \(\sigma\), \(K\) components) with latent labels \(\mathbf Z\), weights \(\mathbf p \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma)\) and means \(\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu_{0k}, \sigma_{0k}^2)\). The joint is \[f(\mathbf x, \mathbf Z, \mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = f(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu)\,f(\mathbf Z \mid \mathbf p)\,f(\mathbf p)\,f(\boldsymbol\mu).\] Assume only that the labels decouple from the parameters, \[q(\mathbf Z, \mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = q(\mathbf Z)\,q(\mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu),\] and write \(r_{ik} \equiv q(Z_i = k) = \mathbb E_q[\chi(Z_i = k)]\) (the responsibilities).

Labels. Since \(\log f(\mathbf Z \mid \mathbf p) = \sum_{i,k}\chi(Z_i = k)\log p_k\) and \(\log f(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu) = -\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i,k}\chi(Z_i = k)(x_i - \mu_k)^2 + \text{const}\), \[\log q^*(\mathbf Z) = \sum_{i,k}\chi(Z_i = k)\Bigl[\mathbb E[\log p_k] - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu_k)^2]\Bigr] + \text{const}.\] This factorises over points: \(q^*(\mathbf Z) = \prod_i\prod_k r_{ik}^{\chi(Z_i = k)}\) with \[r_{ik} = \frac{\rho_{ik}}{\sum_{k'}\rho_{ik'}}, \qquad \rho_{ik} = \exp\Bigl(\mathbb E[\log p_k] - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu_k)^2]\Bigr).\]

Parameters. Taking the expectation over \(\mathbf Z\) (only \(\mathbb E[\chi(Z_i = k)] = r_{ik}\) enters, by linearity), \[\log q^*(\mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = \underbrace{\sum_k\Bigl(\gamma_k - 1 + \sum_i r_{ik}\Bigr)\log p_k}_{\text{depends on } \mathbf p} \ \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i,k}r_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)^2 - \sum_k\frac{(\mu_k - \mu_{0k})^2}{2\sigma_{0k}^2}}_{\text{depends on } \boldsymbol\mu} + \text{const}.\] The right-hand side is a sum of a term in \(\mathbf p\) and a term in \(\boldsymbol\mu\), so \(q^*(\mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = q^*(\mathbf p)q^*(\boldsymbol\mu)\) factorises automatically — a result of the calculation, not an assumption. Reading off the forms, with \(N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}\) the expected number of points in cluster \(k\): \[q^*(\mathbf p) = \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma'), \quad \gamma'_k = \gamma_k + N_k; \qquad q^*(\mu_k) = \mathcal N(\mu'_k, \sigma'^2_k),\quad \mu'_k = \frac{\mu_{0k}/\sigma_{0k}^2 + \sum_i r_{ik}x_i/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma_{0k}^2 + N_k/\sigma^2},\quad \sigma'^2_k = \Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma_{0k}^2} + \frac{N_k}{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{-1}.\] These are exactly the Gibbs conditionals of Chapter 8 with hard counts replaced by soft counts \(N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}\).

Moments needed for \(r_{ik}\). \[\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu_k)^2] = (x_i - \mu'_k)^2 + \sigma'^2_k, \qquad \mathbb E[\log p_k] = \psi(\gamma'_k) - \psi\Bigl(\sum_j\gamma'_j\Bigr),\] where \(\psi = \Gamma'/\Gamma\) is the digamma function (the second identity is a standard property of the Dirichlet). Hence \[\rho_{ik} = \exp\Bigl[\psi(\gamma'_k) - \psi\Bigl(\sum_j\gamma'_j\Bigr) - \frac{(x_i - \mu'_k)^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{\sigma'^2_k}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr].\]

Iterate until \(\mathcal L\) stops increasing:

  1. (E-like step) with \(\mu'_k, \sigma'_k, \gamma'_k\) fixed, compute \(\rho_{ik}\) and the responsibilities \(r_{ik} = \rho_{ik}/\sum_{k'}\rho_{ik'}\);
  2. (M-like step) with \(r_{ik}\) fixed, compute \(N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}\) and \(\sum_i r_{ik}x_i\), then update \(\gamma'_k\), \(\mu'_k\), \(\sigma'_k\).

This has the structure of the EM algorithm and of soft K-means: the responsibilities are a softmax of “log-prior weight minus squared distance”, corrected by the posterior uncertainty of the parameters (\(\sigma'^2_k\) and the digamma terms). A practical bonus: with a small Dirichlet concentration \(\gamma_k < 1\), the VB solution drives the weights of superfluous components to zero, effectively selecting the number of clusters — something maximum-likelihood EM cannot do.

11.5 The variational autoencoder

Mean field is the most common choice, but VB only requires that \(q\) belong to a tractable family. Let the family be parametrised by \(\boldsymbol\theta\): \(q = q(\mathbf z \mid \boldsymbol\theta)\). Writing the joint as likelihood × prior, \[\mathcal L(q) = \int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log\frac{f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z)f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z)}{q(\mathbf z)} = -D_{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf z)\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z)\bigr) + \mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\bigl[\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z)\bigr].\] Two contributions compete:

  • the prior term \(-D_{KL}(q\|f_{\text{prior}})\) keeps \(q\) from departing too far from the prior;
  • the data term \(\mathbb E_q\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)\) pushes \(q\) to concentrate on values of \(\mathbf z\) that explain the data well.

The best approximation is \(q^* = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal L(q(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol\theta))\), and \(\log f(\mathbf y) \geq \mathcal L(q^*)\).

11.5.1 Learning the likelihood too

Now suppose the data and the prior are given, but the likelihood is only known to belong to a family \(f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z, \boldsymbol\phi)\). A natural way to choose \(\boldsymbol\phi\) is to maximise the evidence \(\log f(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\phi)\) (empirical Bayes / maximum marginal likelihood). The evidence is intractable, but the ELBO bounds it, so we maximise the bound jointly: \[\log f(\mathbf y) \geq \max_{\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\phi}\Bigl[-D_{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf z\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z)\bigr) + \mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z, \boldsymbol\phi)\Bigr].\] Given only the prior and the data, this finds a self-consistent generative model of the data within a class of models, together with an approximate posterior.

11.5.2 Encoder and decoder

Take one latent vector \(\mathbf z_i \in \mathbb R^d\) per data point \(\mathbf y_i \in \mathbb R^D\), with \(d < D\): a low-dimensional representation of high-dimensional data. A simple and typical choice:

  • prior: \(f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z) = \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)\);
  • decoder (likelihood): \(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z), c\,I)\) — the data point is Gaussian around a nonlinear function of its latent code;
  • encoder (variational posterior): \(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y \sim q = \mathcal N\bigl(\mathbf e_{1,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y), \operatorname{diag}\,\mathbf e_{2,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y)\bigr)\) — mean and variances are functions of the data. Using one shared function of \(\mathbf y\) instead of separate parameters for each \(q(\mathbf z_i)\) is called amortised inference.

(Unless \(\mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}\) is linear, the true posterior is not Gaussian; the encoder gives the best Gaussian approximation.) The KL between Gaussians is available in closed form, \[D_{KL}\bigl(\mathcal N(\mathbf m, \Sigma)\,\|\,\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)\bigr) = \frac12\Bigl(\mathbf m^\top\mathbf m + \operatorname{Tr}\Sigma - d - \log\det\Sigma\Bigr),\] and the data term is a reconstruction error, so (per data point, up to constants) \[-\mathcal L = \underbrace{\frac12\Bigl(\|\mathbf e_1(\mathbf y)\|^2 + \sum_j e_{2j}(\mathbf y) - d - \sum_j\log e_{2j}(\mathbf y)\Bigr)}_{\text{regulariser}} + \underbrace{\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z)\|^2}{2c}}_{\text{reconstruction}} .\] \(q\) acts as a probabilistic encoder (each \(\mathbf y\) maps to a distribution over codes centred at \(\mathbf e_1(\mathbf y)\)), and \(f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)\) as a probabilistic decoder. Implementing \(\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2, \mathbf d\) as deep neural networks, \(\boldsymbol\theta\) and \(\boldsymbol\phi\) are their weights and biases, and the loss is minimised by stochastic gradient descent with back-propagation. Without the regulariser this would be an ordinary autoencoder; the KL term forces the codes to fill the prior \(\mathcal N(0, I)\), so that sampling \(\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\) and decoding produces new, realistic data.

11.5.3 The reparametrisation trick

The reconstruction term is an expectation over \(\mathbf z \sim q(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\), estimated with samples \(\mathbf z_k(\boldsymbol\theta)\): \[\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z)\|^2}{2c} \approx \frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z_k)\|^2}{2c}.\] But a random draw is not a differentiable function of \(\boldsymbol\theta\), so back-propagation cannot pass through it. The fix is to move the randomness outside the parameters: \[\mathbf z = \mathbf e_{1,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y) + \sqrt{\mathbf e_{2,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y)}\odot\boldsymbol\eta, \qquad \boldsymbol\eta \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I).\] Sample \(\boldsymbol\eta_k\) once, independently of \(\boldsymbol\theta\); then \[\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z)\|^2}{2c} \approx \frac1M\sum_k\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf e_1(\mathbf y) + \sqrt{\mathbf e_2(\mathbf y)}\odot\boldsymbol\eta_k)\|^2}{2c},\] a deterministic, differentiable function of \((\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\phi)\) to which standard back-propagation applies. In practice even \(M = 1\) sample per data point per step works, because the noise averages out over mini-batches.

11.6 Exercises

Derive the decomposition \(\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)] - D(q\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}})\).

Solution. \(\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z) + \log f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)] - D(q\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}})\).

Show that mean-field VB underestimates posterior variances for a correlated Gaussian target with covariance \(\Sigma\).

Solution. From the bivariate example, the optimal factor variance is \(1/\Lambda_{jj} = 1/(\Sigma^{-1})_{jj}\). By the Schur complement, \(1/(\Sigma^{-1})_{11} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \leq \Sigma_{11}\), with equality iff the coordinates are uncorrelated. Mean field reports the conditional variance, which is always smaller than the marginal one.

Compute \(D_{KL}(\mathcal N(m, s^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1))\) in one dimension and check the VAE formula.

Solution. \(D = \mathbb E_q[\log q - \log p] = \mathbb E_q\bigl[-\tfrac12\log(2\pi s^2) - \tfrac{(z-m)^2}{2s^2} + \tfrac12\log 2\pi + \tfrac{z^2}{2}\bigr] = -\tfrac12\log s^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(m^2 + s^2)\), i.e. \(\tfrac12(m^2 + s^2 - 1 - \log s^2)\), matching the formula with \(d = 1\). It vanishes iff \(m = 0, s = 1\).

In the one-Gaussian example with \(a_0 = b_0 = \lambda_0 = 0\), \(n = 10\) and \(s^2 = 4\), iterate the CAVI equations starting from \(\mathbb E[\tau] = 1\) and verify the fixed point.

Solution. \(a' = 5.5\), fixed point \(b' = (n+1)s^2/2 = 22\). Start: \(\lambda' = 10\), \(b' = \tfrac12(10\cdot4) + \tfrac12\cdot10/10 = 20.5\); \(\mathbb E\tau = 0.268\), \(\lambda' = 2.68\), \(b' = 20 + 1.86 = 21.86\); \(\mathbb E\tau = 0.2516\), \(\lambda' = 2.516\), \(b' = 20 + 1.987 = 21.987\); … converging to \(b' = 22\), \(\mathbb E[\tau] = 0.25 = 1/s^2\), \(\operatorname{Var}_q\mu = 0.4 = s^2/n\).

11.7 Callouts

VB is biased by construction: the true posterior is generally not in \(\mathcal Q\). Use it when speed matters and the posterior mean is what you need; use MCMC/HMC when calibrated uncertainty matters.

Monitor \(\mathcal L\) during CAVI: it must increase at every step. A decrease always means a bug in the update equations. Run from several initialisations and keep the solution with the largest \(\mathcal L\).

Mean-field VB underestimates posterior variance and can lock onto a single mode. Richer families (full-rank Gaussians, normalising flows) reduce, but do not remove, this bias.

MCMC is asymptotically exact but can be slow, and it is hard to know when it has converged. **Variational Bayes** (VB) trades exactness for speed: it approximates the posterior by the closest member of a tractable family of distributions, turning inference into *optimisation*. The measure of closeness is — once again — the KL divergence of Chapter 2, and the objective is a lower bound on the log-evidence. This chapter derives the variational principle, the mean-field update equations, works out two examples in full (a Gaussian, and the mixture model of Chapter 8), and ends with the variational autoencoder, where the same bound trains deep generative models.

## The evidence lower bound

Let $\mathbf y$ be the data and $\mathbf z$ all unknowns (parameters and latent variables). For **any** distribution $q(\mathbf z)$,
$$\begin{aligned}
\log f(\mathbf y) &= \int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log f(\mathbf y) = \int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\Bigl[\log\frac{f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)}{q(\mathbf z)} + \log\frac{q(\mathbf z)\,f(\mathbf y)}{f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)}\Bigr] \\
&= \underbrace{\int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log\frac{f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)}{q(\mathbf z)}}_{\mathcal L(q)} + \underbrace{\int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log\frac{q(\mathbf z)}{f(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y)}}_{D_{KL}(q \,\|\, f(\cdot \mid \mathbf y))},
\end{aligned}$$
using $f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)/f(\mathbf y) = f(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y)$.

::: {.theorem name="Evidence lower bound (ELBO)"}
$$\log f(\mathbf y) = \mathcal L(q) + D_{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf z)\,\|\,f(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y)\bigr), \qquad \mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)] - \mathbb E_q[\log q(\mathbf z)].$$
Since $D_{KL} \geq 0$, $\mathcal L(q) \leq \log f(\mathbf y)$ for every $q$, with equality iff $q$ is the exact posterior.
:::

The left-hand side does not depend on $q$, so **maximising the ELBO over $q$ is the same as minimising $D_{KL}(q\|f(\cdot\mid\mathbf y))$**: we force $q$ to be as close as possible to the posterior. Crucially, $\mathcal L$ involves only the *joint* $f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)$ (likelihood × prior), never the intractable evidence. The VB recipe is:

> choose a family $\mathcal Q$ of tractable distributions, and find $q^* = \arg\max_{q\in\mathcal Q}\mathcal L(q)$.

Two other readings of the ELBO are useful:

- $\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)] + H[q]$: an "energy" term rewarding $q$ for sitting where the joint is large, plus an entropy term rewarding it for spreading out — exactly a (negative) **variational free energy** of statistical physics, with $\log f(\mathbf y)$ playing the role of $-F = \log Z$.
- $\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z)] - D_{KL}(q(\mathbf z)\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z))$: data fit minus a penalty for departing from the prior (used for the VAE below).

::: {.remark name="Which KL?"}
VB minimises $D(q\|p)$, the "reverse" KL. Since $D(q\|p) = \int q\log(q/p)$, it is huge wherever $q > 0$ but $p \approx 0$: $q$ is pushed to **avoid** regions where the posterior is small. The result is **mode-seeking** and typically **under-dispersed**: for a multimodal posterior, $q$ locks onto one mode; for a correlated posterior, $q$ is too narrow. The "forward" KL $D(p\|q)$ would be mass-covering instead, but it needs expectations under $p$ — which is what we cannot compute.
:::

## The mean-field approximation

The most common choice is to assume that $q$ **factorises** over groups of variables,
$$q(\mathbf z) = \prod_i q_i(\mathbf z_i),$$
with no assumption on the form of each factor. This is the **mean-field** approximation, named after the physics approximation in which each spin feels only the *average* field of the others.

### Derivation of the update

Maximise $\mathcal L$ over one factor $q_j$, keeping the others fixed. Separating the terms that involve $q_j$,
$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(q) &= \int d\mathbf z\,\prod_i q_i(\mathbf z_i)\Bigl[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y) - \sum_i\log q_i(\mathbf z_i)\Bigr] \\
&= \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j(\mathbf z_j)\Bigl[\int\prod_{i\neq j}d\mathbf z_i\,q_i(\mathbf z_i)\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\Bigr] - \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j\log q_j + \text{const} \\
&= \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j(\mathbf z_j)\log\tilde f(\mathbf z_j, \mathbf y) - \int d\mathbf z_j\,q_j(\mathbf z_j)\log q_j(\mathbf z_j) + \text{const},
\end{aligned}$$
where "const" does not depend on $q_j$ and we defined the distribution $\tilde f$ by
$$\log\tilde f(\mathbf z_j, \mathbf y) = \mathbb E_{i\neq j}[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)] + \text{const},$$
the log-joint **averaged over all other factors**. Then
$$\mathcal L(q) = -D_{KL}\bigl(q_j\,\|\,\tilde f\bigr) + \text{const},$$
which is maximised by $q_j = \tilde f$.

::: {.theorem name="Mean-field update"}
For fixed $q_i$, $i \neq j$, the optimal factor is
$$\log q_j^*(\mathbf z_j) = \mathbb E_{i\neq j}\bigl[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)\bigr] + \text{const}, \qquad q_j^*(\mathbf z_j) = \frac{\exp\bigl(\mathbb E_{i\neq j}[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)]\bigr)}{\int d\mathbf z_j\exp\bigl(\mathbb E_{i\neq j}[\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)]\bigr)} .$$
:::

**Recipe:** write the log of the joint over all hidden and observed variables, keep only the terms involving $\mathbf z_j$, take the expectation over all *other* factors, and recognise the resulting functional form (normalisation is usually read off at the end).

The equations for the different $j$ are coupled — each right-hand side depends on the other factors — so they are solved **iteratively**: initialise all factors and cycle through them, updating each in turn (**coordinate-ascent variational inference**, CAVI). Each update maximises $\mathcal L$ with respect to one factor ($\mathcal L$ is concave in each $q_j$ separately), so $\mathcal L$ increases monotonically and the iteration converges — to a *local* maximum, which may depend on the initialisation.

::: {.remark name="VB vs Gibbs"}
Compare with the Gibbs sampler of Chapter 8. Gibbs updates $\mathbf z_j$ by drawing from $f(\mathbf z_j \mid \mathbf z_{-j}, \mathbf y) \propto \exp\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)$ evaluated at the *current sample* of the others. Mean-field VB updates the *distribution* $q_j \propto \exp\mathbb E_{-j}\log f(\mathbf z, \mathbf y)$ using the *average* over the others. With conjugate models the two have the same functional forms; VB replaces random draws by expectations.
:::

### A correlated Gaussian: the variance underestimate

::: {.example name="Mean field on a bivariate Gaussian (Bishop §10.1.2)"}
Let the target be $f(\mathbf z) = \mathcal N(\mathbf z; \boldsymbol\mu, \Lambda^{-1})$ with precision matrix $\Lambda$, and $q = q_1(z_1)q_2(z_2)$. Then
$$\log q_1^*(z_1) = \mathbb E_{z_2}\Bigl[-\tfrac12\Lambda_{11}(z_1 - \mu_1)^2 - \Lambda_{12}(z_1 - \mu_1)(z_2 - \mu_2)\Bigr] + \text{const},$$
which is quadratic in $z_1$, so $q_1^*$ is Gaussian — this was *derived*, not assumed:
$$q_1^* = \mathcal N\Bigl(m_1,\ \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Bigr), \qquad m_1 = \mu_1 - \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Lambda_{12}(m_2 - \mu_2),$$
and symmetrically for $q_2$. The self-consistent solution is $m_1 = \mu_1$, $m_2 = \mu_2$: **the means are exact**. But the variances are $1/\Lambda_{11}$, the *conditional* variances, smaller than the true marginal variances $(\Lambda^{-1})_{11}$. For unit variances and correlation $\rho$, $q$ has variance $1 - \rho^2$ against the true $1$: for $\rho = 0.9$, the mean-field standard deviation is $0.44$ instead of $1$. Mean field "fits inside" the elongated posterior ellipse.
:::

## Example: one Gaussian with unknown mean and precision

Data $x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^{-1})$, $i = 1..n$, with $\tau$ the precision. The likelihood is
$$f(\mathbf x \mid \mu, \tau) = \Bigl(\frac{\tau}{2\pi}\Bigr)^{n/2}\exp\Bigl[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2\Bigr].$$
The conjugate prior is **normal–gamma**:
$$\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0): f(\tau) = \frac{b_0^{a_0}}{\Gamma(a_0)}\tau^{a_0 - 1}e^{-b_0\tau}, \qquad \mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal N\bigl(\mu_0, (\lambda_0\tau)^{-1}\bigr): f(\mu \mid \tau) = \Bigl(\frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2\pi}\Bigr)^{1/2}e^{-\frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2}.$$
The exact posterior is again normal–gamma — but *not* factorised ($\mu$ and $\tau$ are dependent). We approximate it with $q(\mu, \tau) = q_\mu(\mu)q_\tau(\tau)$.

**Factor for $\mu$.** Keeping the terms with $\mu$,
$$\log q_\mu^*(\mu) = \mathbb E_\tau\Bigl[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 - \frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] + \text{const} = -\frac{\mathbb E[\tau]}{2}\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] + \text{const}.$$
Completing the square, $q_\mu^* = \mathcal N(\mu', \lambda'^{-1})$ with
$$\mu' = \frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + \sum_i x_i}{\lambda_0 + n} = \frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + n\bar x}{\lambda_0 + n}, \qquad \lambda' = (\lambda_0 + n)\,\mathbb E[\tau].$$

**Factor for $\tau$.** Keeping the terms with $\tau$ (note $n/2$ from the likelihood, $1/2$ from the prior on $\mu$, $a_0 - 1$ from the prior on $\tau$),
$$\log q_\tau^*(\tau) = \Bigl(a_0 - 1 + \frac{n + 1}{2}\Bigr)\log\tau - \tau\Bigl(b_0 + \frac12\mathbb E_\mu\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr]\Bigr) + \text{const},$$
so $q_\tau^* = \text{Gamma}(a', b')$ with
$$a' = a_0 + \frac{n + 1}{2}, \qquad b' = b_0 + \frac12\mathbb E_\mu\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr].$$
The required moments under $q_\mu = \mathcal N(\mu', \lambda'^{-1})$ are $\mathbb E[\mu] = \mu'$ and $\mathbb E[\mu^2] = \mu'^2 + \lambda'^{-1}$, giving
$$\mathbb E_\mu\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] = \sum_i(x_i - \mu')^2 + \lambda_0(\mu' - \mu_0)^2 + \frac{n + \lambda_0}{\lambda'} ,$$
and $\mathbb E[\tau] = a'/b'$ under the Gamma.

**Iteration.** $\mu'$ and $a'$ do not change; only $\lambda'$ and $b'$ are coupled. Initialise $\mathbb E[\tau]$, then alternate
$$\lambda' = (\lambda_0 + n)\frac{a'}{b'}, \qquad b' = b_0 + \frac12\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i - \mu')^2 + \lambda_0(\mu' - \mu_0)^2\Bigr] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{n + \lambda_0}{\lambda'} .$$

::: {.example name="Non-informative limit"}
With $a_0 = b_0 = \lambda_0 = 0$: $\mu' = \bar x$, $a' = (n + 1)/2$. Substituting $\lambda' = n a'/b'$ into the $b'$ equation, $b' = \tfrac12 n s^2 + \tfrac12 b'/a'$ with $s^2 = \frac1n\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2$, whose fixed point is $b' = \frac{n s^2 a'}{2a' - 1} = \frac{(n + 1)s^2}{2}$. Then $\mathbb E[\tau] = a'/b' = 1/s^2$ and $\operatorname{Var}_q[\mu] = 1/\lambda' = s^2/n$: the familiar maximum-likelihood variance estimate and the standard error of the mean. The iteration converges in a handful of steps from any starting $\mathbb E[\tau]$, since the map $b' \mapsto \tfrac12ns^2 + b'/(2a')$ is a contraction with factor $1/(n + 1)$.
:::

## Example: mixture distributions

Return to the Gaussian mixture of Chapter 8 (known $\sigma$, $K$ components) with latent labels $\mathbf Z$, weights $\mathbf p \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma)$ and means $\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(\mu_{0k}, \sigma_{0k}^2)$. The joint is
$$f(\mathbf x, \mathbf Z, \mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = f(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu)\,f(\mathbf Z \mid \mathbf p)\,f(\mathbf p)\,f(\boldsymbol\mu).$$
Assume only that the labels decouple from the parameters,
$$q(\mathbf Z, \mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = q(\mathbf Z)\,q(\mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu),$$
and write $r_{ik} \equiv q(Z_i = k) = \mathbb E_q[\chi(Z_i = k)]$ (the **responsibilities**).

**Labels.** Since $\log f(\mathbf Z \mid \mathbf p) = \sum_{i,k}\chi(Z_i = k)\log p_k$ and $\log f(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu) = -\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i,k}\chi(Z_i = k)(x_i - \mu_k)^2 + \text{const}$,
$$\log q^*(\mathbf Z) = \sum_{i,k}\chi(Z_i = k)\Bigl[\mathbb E[\log p_k] - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu_k)^2]\Bigr] + \text{const}.$$
This factorises over points: $q^*(\mathbf Z) = \prod_i\prod_k r_{ik}^{\chi(Z_i = k)}$ with
$$r_{ik} = \frac{\rho_{ik}}{\sum_{k'}\rho_{ik'}}, \qquad \rho_{ik} = \exp\Bigl(\mathbb E[\log p_k] - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu_k)^2]\Bigr).$$

**Parameters.** Taking the expectation over $\mathbf Z$ (only $\mathbb E[\chi(Z_i = k)] = r_{ik}$ enters, by linearity),
$$\log q^*(\mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = \underbrace{\sum_k\Bigl(\gamma_k - 1 + \sum_i r_{ik}\Bigr)\log p_k}_{\text{depends on } \mathbf p} \ \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i,k}r_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)^2 - \sum_k\frac{(\mu_k - \mu_{0k})^2}{2\sigma_{0k}^2}}_{\text{depends on } \boldsymbol\mu} + \text{const}.$$
The right-hand side is a sum of a term in $\mathbf p$ and a term in $\boldsymbol\mu$, so **$q^*(\mathbf p, \boldsymbol\mu) = q^*(\mathbf p)q^*(\boldsymbol\mu)$ factorises automatically** — a result of the calculation, not an assumption. Reading off the forms, with $N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}$ the expected number of points in cluster $k$:
$$q^*(\mathbf p) = \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol\gamma'), \quad \gamma'_k = \gamma_k + N_k; \qquad q^*(\mu_k) = \mathcal N(\mu'_k, \sigma'^2_k),\quad \mu'_k = \frac{\mu_{0k}/\sigma_{0k}^2 + \sum_i r_{ik}x_i/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma_{0k}^2 + N_k/\sigma^2},\quad \sigma'^2_k = \Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma_{0k}^2} + \frac{N_k}{\sigma^2}\Bigr)^{-1}.$$
These are exactly the Gibbs conditionals of Chapter 8 with **hard counts replaced by soft counts** $N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}$.

**Moments needed for $r_{ik}$.**
$$\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu_k)^2] = (x_i - \mu'_k)^2 + \sigma'^2_k, \qquad \mathbb E[\log p_k] = \psi(\gamma'_k) - \psi\Bigl(\sum_j\gamma'_j\Bigr),$$
where $\psi = \Gamma'/\Gamma$ is the digamma function (the second identity is a standard property of the Dirichlet). Hence
$$\rho_{ik} = \exp\Bigl[\psi(\gamma'_k) - \psi\Bigl(\sum_j\gamma'_j\Bigr) - \frac{(x_i - \mu'_k)^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{\sigma'^2_k}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr].$$

::: {.definition name="VB for the Gaussian mixture"}
Iterate until $\mathcal L$ stops increasing:

1. **(E-like step)** with $\mu'_k, \sigma'_k, \gamma'_k$ fixed, compute $\rho_{ik}$ and the responsibilities $r_{ik} = \rho_{ik}/\sum_{k'}\rho_{ik'}$;
2. **(M-like step)** with $r_{ik}$ fixed, compute $N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}$ and $\sum_i r_{ik}x_i$, then update $\gamma'_k$, $\mu'_k$, $\sigma'_k$.
:::

This has the structure of the EM algorithm and of soft K-means: the responsibilities are a softmax of "log-prior weight minus squared distance", corrected by the posterior uncertainty of the parameters ($\sigma'^2_k$ and the digamma terms). A practical bonus: with a small Dirichlet concentration $\gamma_k < 1$, the VB solution drives the weights of superfluous components to zero, effectively *selecting* the number of clusters — something maximum-likelihood EM cannot do.

## The variational autoencoder

Mean field is the most common choice, but VB only requires that $q$ belong to a tractable family. Let the family be **parametrised** by $\boldsymbol\theta$: $q = q(\mathbf z \mid \boldsymbol\theta)$. Writing the joint as likelihood × prior,
$$\mathcal L(q) = \int d\mathbf z\,q(\mathbf z)\log\frac{f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z)f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z)}{q(\mathbf z)} = -D_{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf z)\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z)\bigr) + \mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\bigl[\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z)\bigr].$$
Two contributions compete:

- the **prior term** $-D_{KL}(q\|f_{\text{prior}})$ keeps $q$ from departing too far from the prior;
- the **data term** $\mathbb E_q\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)$ pushes $q$ to concentrate on values of $\mathbf z$ that explain the data well.

The best approximation is $q^* = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal L(q(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol\theta))$, and $\log f(\mathbf y) \geq \mathcal L(q^*)$.

### Learning the likelihood too

Now suppose the data and the prior are given, but the likelihood is only known to belong to a family $f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z, \boldsymbol\phi)$. A natural way to choose $\boldsymbol\phi$ is to maximise the evidence $\log f(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\phi)$ (*empirical Bayes* / maximum marginal likelihood). The evidence is intractable, but the ELBO bounds it, so we maximise the bound jointly:
$$\log f(\mathbf y) \geq \max_{\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\phi}\Bigl[-D_{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf z\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z)\bigr) + \mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\log f(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z, \boldsymbol\phi)\Bigr].$$
Given only the prior and the data, this finds a self-consistent **generative model** of the data within a class of models, together with an approximate posterior.

### Encoder and decoder

Take one latent vector $\mathbf z_i \in \mathbb R^d$ per data point $\mathbf y_i \in \mathbb R^D$, with $d < D$: a low-dimensional representation of high-dimensional data. A simple and typical choice:

- **prior:** $f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z) = \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)$;
- **decoder (likelihood):** $\mathbf y \mid \mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z), c\,I)$ — the data point is Gaussian around a nonlinear function of its latent code;
- **encoder (variational posterior):** $\mathbf z \mid \mathbf y \sim q = \mathcal N\bigl(\mathbf e_{1,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y), \operatorname{diag}\,\mathbf e_{2,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y)\bigr)$ — mean and variances are functions of the data. Using one shared function of $\mathbf y$ instead of separate parameters for each $q(\mathbf z_i)$ is called **amortised inference**.

(Unless $\mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}$ is linear, the true posterior is not Gaussian; the encoder gives the best Gaussian approximation.) The KL between Gaussians is available in closed form,
$$D_{KL}\bigl(\mathcal N(\mathbf m, \Sigma)\,\|\,\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)\bigr) = \frac12\Bigl(\mathbf m^\top\mathbf m + \operatorname{Tr}\Sigma - d - \log\det\Sigma\Bigr),$$
and the data term is a reconstruction error, so (per data point, up to constants)
$$-\mathcal L = \underbrace{\frac12\Bigl(\|\mathbf e_1(\mathbf y)\|^2 + \sum_j e_{2j}(\mathbf y) - d - \sum_j\log e_{2j}(\mathbf y)\Bigr)}_{\text{regulariser}} + \underbrace{\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z)\|^2}{2c}}_{\text{reconstruction}} .$$
$q$ acts as a **probabilistic encoder** (each $\mathbf y$ maps to a *distribution* over codes centred at $\mathbf e_1(\mathbf y)$), and $f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)$ as a **probabilistic decoder**. Implementing $\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2, \mathbf d$ as deep neural networks, $\boldsymbol\theta$ and $\boldsymbol\phi$ are their weights and biases, and the loss is minimised by stochastic gradient descent with back-propagation. Without the regulariser this would be an ordinary autoencoder; the KL term forces the codes to fill the prior $\mathcal N(0, I)$, so that *sampling* $\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and decoding produces new, realistic data.

### The reparametrisation trick

The reconstruction term is an expectation over $\mathbf z \sim q(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$, estimated with samples $\mathbf z_k(\boldsymbol\theta)$:
$$\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z)\|^2}{2c} \approx \frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z_k)\|^2}{2c}.$$
But a random draw is not a differentiable function of $\boldsymbol\theta$, so back-propagation cannot pass through it. The fix is to move the randomness *outside* the parameters:
$$\mathbf z = \mathbf e_{1,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y) + \sqrt{\mathbf e_{2,\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf y)}\odot\boldsymbol\eta, \qquad \boldsymbol\eta \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I).$$
Sample $\boldsymbol\eta_k$ once, independently of $\boldsymbol\theta$; then
$$\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim q}\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf z)\|^2}{2c} \approx \frac1M\sum_k\frac{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf d_{\boldsymbol\phi}(\mathbf e_1(\mathbf y) + \sqrt{\mathbf e_2(\mathbf y)}\odot\boldsymbol\eta_k)\|^2}{2c},$$
a deterministic, differentiable function of $(\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol\phi)$ to which standard back-propagation applies. In practice even $M = 1$ sample per data point per step works, because the noise averages out over mini-batches.

## Exercises

::: {.exercise}
Derive the decomposition $\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)] - D(q\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}})$.
:::

::: {.solution}
$\mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z) + \log f_{\text{prior}}(\mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] = \mathbb E_q[\log f(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)] - D(q\,\|\,f_{\text{prior}})$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Show that mean-field VB *underestimates* posterior variances for a correlated Gaussian target with covariance $\Sigma$.
:::

::: {.solution}
From the bivariate example, the optimal factor variance is $1/\Lambda_{jj} = 1/(\Sigma^{-1})_{jj}$. By the Schur complement, $1/(\Sigma^{-1})_{11} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \leq \Sigma_{11}$, with equality iff the coordinates are uncorrelated. Mean field reports the *conditional* variance, which is always smaller than the marginal one.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Compute $D_{KL}(\mathcal N(m, s^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1))$ in one dimension and check the VAE formula.
:::

::: {.solution}
$D = \mathbb E_q[\log q - \log p] = \mathbb E_q\bigl[-\tfrac12\log(2\pi s^2) - \tfrac{(z-m)^2}{2s^2} + \tfrac12\log 2\pi + \tfrac{z^2}{2}\bigr] = -\tfrac12\log s^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(m^2 + s^2)$, i.e. $\tfrac12(m^2 + s^2 - 1 - \log s^2)$, matching the formula with $d = 1$. It vanishes iff $m = 0, s = 1$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
In the one-Gaussian example with $a_0 = b_0 = \lambda_0 = 0$, $n = 10$ and $s^2 = 4$, iterate the CAVI equations starting from $\mathbb E[\tau] = 1$ and verify the fixed point.
:::

::: {.solution}
$a' = 5.5$, fixed point $b' = (n+1)s^2/2 = 22$. Start: $\lambda' = 10$, $b' = \tfrac12(10\cdot4) + \tfrac12\cdot10/10 = 20.5$; $\mathbb E\tau = 0.268$, $\lambda' = 2.68$, $b' = 20 + 1.86 = 21.86$; $\mathbb E\tau = 0.2516$, $\lambda' = 2.516$, $b' = 20 + 1.987 = 21.987$; … converging to $b' = 22$, $\mathbb E[\tau] = 0.25 = 1/s^2$, $\operatorname{Var}_q\mu = 0.4 = s^2/n$.
:::

## Callouts

::: {.callout-note}
VB is biased by construction: the true posterior is generally not in $\mathcal Q$. Use it when speed matters and the posterior *mean* is what you need; use MCMC/HMC when calibrated uncertainty matters.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
Monitor $\mathcal L$ during CAVI: it must increase at every step. A decrease always means a bug in the update equations. Run from several initialisations and keep the solution with the largest $\mathcal L$.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
Mean-field VB underestimates posterior variance and can lock onto a single mode. Richer families (full-rank Gaussians, normalising flows) reduce, but do not remove, this bias.
:::
