4  Unitary evolution and the postulates

States change in two ways: by measurement, which is random and irreversible, and by the deterministic, reversible evolution of an isolated system, described by unitary operators. This chapter studies unitaries — in particular single-qubit rotations — derives them from the Schrödinger equation, discusses the incompatibility of measurements and the uncertainty relation, and collects the postulates of quantum mechanics for isolated systems.

4.1 Unitary operators

\(\hat U\) is unitary if \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U = \hat U\hat U^\dagger = \hat{\mathbb 1}\). It preserves scalar products, \(\braket{\hat U\phi|\hat U\psi} = \braket{\phi|\psi}\), hence norms and orthogonality; it maps orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases; its eigenvalues have unit modulus, \(e^{i\lambda}\).

A unitary transforms states as \[\ket\psi \mapsto \hat U\ket\psi, \qquad \hat\rho \mapsto \hat U\hat\rho\,\hat U^\dagger .\] Because \(\hat U\) is invertible, the transformation is reversible: no information is lost.

Three kinds of operators play very different roles and should not be confused:

  • observables \(\hat A = \hat A^\dagger\) label measurement outcomes; applying \(\hat A\) to a state, \(\hat A\ket\psi\), has no physical meaning (it is not even normalised);
  • density operators \(\hat\rho \geq 0\), \(\mathrm{Tr}\hat\rho = 1\) describe states;
  • unitaries \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U = \hat{\mathbb 1}\) describe transformations.

The Pauli matrices are special: they are both Hermitian and unitary, so \(\hat\sigma_x\) is at the same time “spin along \(x\)” (an observable) and “bit flip” (a transformation). Projectors \(\hat\Pi_a\) appear inside measurements, but “\(\hat\Pi_a\ket\psi\)” is only the unnormalised post-measurement state.

4.2 Single-qubit unitaries

Every \(2\times 2\) unitary can be written as \[\hat U = e^{i\alpha}\,\hat R_{\vec n}(\theta), \qquad \hat R_{\vec n}(\theta) = e^{-i\frac\theta2\,\vec n\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma}} = \cos\frac\theta2\,\hat{\mathbb 1} - i\sin\frac\theta2\,\vec n\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma},\] where the closed form follows from \((\vec n\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma})^2 = \hat{\mathbb 1}\). The global phase \(e^{i\alpha}\) is unobservable, and \(\hat R_{\vec n}(\theta)\) acts on the Bloch sphere as a rotation by angle \(\theta\) about the axis \(\vec n\). The “half angle” \(\theta/2\) is the same doubling met in Chapter 1: \(\hat R_{\vec n}(2\pi) = -\hat{\mathbb 1}\), a global phase.

Equivalently, any single-qubit unitary decomposes (Euler) as \(\hat U = e^{i\alpha}\hat R_z(\beta)\hat R_y(\gamma)\hat R_z(\delta)\). Familiar examples: \(\hat\sigma_x = i\hat R_x(\pi)\) (bit flip, rotation by \(\pi\) about \(x\)), \(\hat\sigma_z = i\hat R_z(\pi)\) (phase flip), and the Hadamard gate \[\hat H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{\hat\sigma_x + \hat\sigma_z}{\sqrt 2},\] a rotation by \(\pi\) about the axis \((\hat x + \hat z)/\sqrt2\), which swaps \(\ket 0 \leftrightarrow \ket +\) and \(\ket 1 \leftrightarrow \ket -\), i.e. exchanges the \(z\) and \(x\) bases.

4.3 Time evolution

The state of an isolated system evolves according to \[i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)} = \hat H\ket{\psi(t)},\] where the Hamiltonian \(\hat H\) is the observable associated with energy.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian the solution is \[\ket{\psi(t)} = \hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}, \qquad \hat U(t) = e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar} = \sum_n e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}\ket{E_n}\bra{E_n},\] with \(\hat H\ket{E_n} = E_n\ket{E_n}\). Each energy eigenstate only acquires a phase (it is stationary); superpositions evolve because the relative phases change. Since \(\hat H\) is Hermitian, \(\hat U(t)\) is unitary. In density-operator language, \(i\hbar\,\dot{\hat\rho} = [\hat H, \hat\rho]\) (von Neumann equation).

Let \(\hat H = B_0\hat\sigma_z\) (a spin in a magnetic field along \(z\), with constants absorbed in \(B_0\)) and \(\ket{\psi(0)} = \ket +\). Then \[\ket{\psi(t)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\bigl(e^{-iB_0t/\hbar}\ket 0 + e^{iB_0t/\hbar}\ket 1\bigr) \simeq \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(\ket 0 + e^{i\omega t}\ket 1\bigr), \qquad \omega = \frac{2B_0}{\hbar}.\] The Bloch vector rotates around \(z\) at angular frequency \(\omega\): \(\langle\hat\sigma_x\rangle = \cos\omega t\), \(\langle\hat\sigma_y\rangle = \sin\omega t\), \(\langle\hat\sigma_z\rangle = 0\). Indeed \(\hat U(t) = \hat R_z(\omega t)\). Every single-qubit gate is realised physically by switching on a suitable Hamiltonian for a suitable time.

4.4 Incompatible measurements

Two observables \(\hat A, \hat B\) can be measured “simultaneously” — have a common eigenbasis, so that measuring one does not disturb the statistics of the other — iff they commute, \([\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A = 0\). Otherwise they are incompatible: a state cannot have sharp values of both.

For any state and any observables \(\hat A, \hat B\), \[\Delta A\,\Delta B \geq \frac12\bigl|\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle\bigr| .\]

Proof. Let \(\hat a = \hat A - \langle\hat A\rangle\), \(\hat b = \hat B - \langle\hat B\rangle\). By Cauchy–Schwarz, \((\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 = \langle\hat a^2\rangle\langle\hat b^2\rangle \geq |\langle\hat a\hat b\rangle|^2 \geq |\mathrm{Im}\langle\hat a\hat b\rangle|^2 = \tfrac14|\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle|^2\), since \(\hat a\hat b - \hat b\hat a = [\hat A, \hat B]\) and \(\langle \hat a\hat b\rangle^* = \langle\hat b\hat a\rangle\). \(\square\)

\([\hat\sigma_z, \hat\sigma_x] = 2i\hat\sigma_y\), so \(\Delta\sigma_z\,\Delta\sigma_x \geq |\langle\hat\sigma_y\rangle|\). On \(\ket 0\), \(\Delta\sigma_z = 0\) and \(\Delta\sigma_x = 1\): definite in \(z\), maximally uncertain in \(x\) — the Stern–Gerlach result of Chapter 1. For pure qubit states \((\Delta\sigma_j)^2 = 1 - r_j^2\), and the relation reads \((1 - r_z^2)(1 - r_x^2) \geq r_y^2\), true on the sphere.

Incompatibility is a quantum resource: it is what makes eavesdropping detectable in quantum cryptography (Chapter 10) and what Bell’s theorem turns into a proof of nonlocality (Chapter 6). The “quantum cakes” of Chapter 6 are a story about two incompatible properties.

4.5 The postulates for isolated systems

  1. States. The state of an isolated system is a unit vector \(\ket\psi\) in a Hilbert space \(\mathcal H\), defined up to a global phase (more generally a density operator \(\hat\rho\)). Any normalised superposition of states is a state.
  2. Evolution. States evolve according to the Schrödinger equation, \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\psi(t)} = \hat H\ket{\psi(t)}\); for time-independent \(\hat H\), \(\ket{\psi(t)} = e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\ket{\psi(0)}\). Any discrete transformation is a unitary.
  3. Measurement. Observables are Hermitian operators \(\hat A = \sum_a a\,\hat\Pi_a\). The possible outcomes are the eigenvalues \(a\), obtained with probability \(p_a = |\braket{a|\psi}|^2 = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\Pi_a\hat\rho]\).
  4. Collapse. After obtaining \(a\), the state becomes \(\hat\Pi_a\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_a}\).
  5. Composite systems. The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the components’ spaces (Chapter 5).

Chapter 8 generalises postulates 2–4 to systems that interact with an environment.

4.6 Exercises

Show that \(\hat H\hat\sigma_x\hat H = \hat\sigma_z\), \(\hat H\hat\sigma_z\hat H = \hat\sigma_x\) and \(\hat H\hat\sigma_y\hat H = -\hat\sigma_y\). Interpret geometrically.

Solution. Direct multiplication, or use \(\hat H = (\hat\sigma_x + \hat\sigma_z)/\sqrt2\) and the anticommutation rules: \(\hat H\hat\sigma_x\hat H = \tfrac12(\hat\sigma_x + \hat\sigma_z)\hat\sigma_x(\hat\sigma_x + \hat\sigma_z) = \tfrac12(\hat\sigma_x + \hat\sigma_z + \hat\sigma_z\hat\sigma_x\hat\sigma_z + \hat\sigma_z)\), and \(\hat\sigma_z\hat\sigma_x\hat\sigma_z = -\hat\sigma_x\), giving \(\hat\sigma_z\). Geometrically \(\hat H\) is a \(\pi\) rotation about \((\hat x + \hat z)/\sqrt2\), which swaps the \(x\) and \(z\) axes and reverses \(y\).

A qubit starts in \(\ket 0\) and evolves under \(\hat H = \tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\hat\sigma_x\). Find the probability of finding it in \(\ket 1\) at time \(t\).

Solution. \(\hat U(t) = e^{-i\Omega t\hat\sigma_x/2} = \hat R_x(\Omega t)\), so \(\ket{\psi(t)} = \cos\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\ket 0 - i\sin\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\ket 1\) and \(p_1(t) = \sin^2\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\): Rabi oscillations. A pulse with \(\Omega t = \pi\) is a bit flip (up to a phase).

## Time evolution

::: {.definition name="Schrödinger equation"}
The state of an isolated system evolves according to
$$i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)} = \hat H\ket{\psi(t)},$$
where the Hamiltonian $\hat H$ is the observable associated with energy.
:::

$$\ket{\psi(t)} = \hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}, \qquad \hat U(t) = e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar} = \sum_n e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}\ket{E_n}\bra{E_n},$$

:::

## Incompatible measurements

Two observables $\hat A, \hat B$ can be measured "simultaneously" — have a common eigenbasis, so that measuring one does not disturb the statistics of the other — iff they **commute**, $[\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A = 0$. Otherwise they are **incompatible**: a state cannot have sharp values of both.

::: {.theorem name="Robertson uncertainty relation"}
For any state and any observables $\hat A, \hat B$,
$$\Delta A\,\Delta B \geq \frac12\bigl|\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle\bigr| .$$
:::

*Proof.* Let $\hat a = \hat A - \langle\hat A\rangle$, $\hat b = \hat B - \langle\hat B\rangle$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 = \langle\hat a^2\rangle\langle\hat b^2\rangle \geq |\langle\hat a\hat b\rangle|^2 \geq |\mathrm{Im}\langle\hat a\hat b\rangle|^2 = \tfrac14|\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle|^2$, since $\hat a\hat b - \hat b\hat a = [\hat A, \hat B]$ and $\langle \hat a\hat b\rangle^* = \langle\hat b\hat a\rangle$. $\square$

$[\hat\sigma_z, \hat\sigma_x] = 2i\hat\sigma_y$, so $\Delta\sigma_z\,\Delta\sigma_x \geq |\langle\hat\sigma_y\rangle|$. For pure qubit states $(\Delta\sigma_j)^2 = 1 - r_j^2$, and the relation reads $(1 - r_z^2)(1 - r_x^2) \geq r_y^2$, true on the sphere.

## The postulates for isolated systems

1. **States.** The state of an isolated system is a unit vector $\ket\psi$ in a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$, defined up to a global phase (more generally a density operator $\hat\rho$). Any normalised superposition of states is a state.
2. **Evolution.** States evolve according to the Schrödinger equation, $i\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\psi(t)} = \hat H\ket{\psi(t)}$; for time-independent $\hat H$, $\ket{\psi(t)} = e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\ket{\psi(0)}$. Any discrete transformation is a unitary.
3. **Measurement.** Observables are Hermitian operators $\hat A = \sum_a a\,\hat\Pi_a$. The possible outcomes are the eigenvalues $a$, obtained with probability $p_a = |\braket{a|\psi}|^2 = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\Pi_a\hat\rho]$.
4. **Collapse.** After obtaining $a$, the state becomes $\hat\Pi_a\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_a}$.
5. **Composite systems.** The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the components' spaces (Chapter 5).

Chapter 8 generalises postulates 2–4 to systems that interact with an environment.
