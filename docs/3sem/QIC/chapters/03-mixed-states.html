3  Mixed states and density operators

A ket describes a system prepared in a definite quantum state. Often we know less: a source emits different states at random, or the system is part of a larger entangled whole (Chapter 5). Both situations are described by the density operator, which replaces the state vector as the most general description of a quantum state. This chapter defines it, rewrites the Born rule as a trace, distinguishes quantum superpositions from classical mixtures, and extends the Bloch sphere to the Bloch ball.

3.1 Ensembles and the density operator

Suppose a source prepares the state \(\ket{\psi_k}\) with probability \(p_k\) — an ensemble \(\{p_k, \ket{\psi_k}\}\). The probability of outcome \(a\) in a measurement of \(\hat A\) is the average of the Born probabilities: \[p_a = \sum_k p_k\,\braket{\psi_k|\hat\Pi_a|\psi_k} = \mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\hat\Pi_a \sum_k p_k\ket{\psi_k}\bra{\psi_k}\Bigr],\] using \(\braket{\psi|\hat X|\psi} = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat X\ket\psi\bra\psi]\). All predictions depend on the ensemble only through one operator.

The density operator of the ensemble \(\{p_k, \ket{\psi_k}\}\) is \[\hat\rho = \sum_k p_k\,\ket{\psi_k}\bra{\psi_k}.\] A state is pure if \(\hat\rho = \ket\psi\bra\psi\) for some \(\ket\psi\), and mixed otherwise.

An operator \(\hat\rho\) is a density operator of some ensemble iff it is

  1. Hermitian, \(\hat\rho^\dagger = \hat\rho\);
  2. positive semidefinite, \(\braket{\phi|\hat\rho|\phi} \geq 0\) for all \(\ket\phi\);
  3. of unit trace, \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho = 1\).

The trace is basis independent: \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat A = \sum_k \braket{k|\hat A|k}\) for any orthonormal basis, and \(\mathrm{Tr}(\ket a\bra b) = \braket{b|a}\) turns outer products into inner products. Positivity follows from \(\braket{\phi|\hat\rho|\phi} = \sum_k p_k|\braket{\phi|\psi_k}|^2\); the trace from normalisation. Conversely, the spectral decomposition \(\hat\rho = \sum_j \lambda_j\ket j\bra j\) with \(\lambda_j \geq 0\), \(\sum\lambda_j = 1\) is itself an ensemble. Convex combinations of density operators are density operators: preparing \(\hat\rho_1\) with probability \(p_1\) and \(\hat\rho_2\) with \(p_2\) gives \(p_1\hat\rho_1 + p_2\hat\rho_2\).

For a state \(\hat\rho\): \[p_a = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\Pi_a\hat\rho], \qquad \langle\hat A\rangle = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat A\hat\rho], \qquad \hat\rho \ \xrightarrow{\ a\ }\ \frac{\hat\Pi_a\hat\rho\,\hat\Pi_a}{p_a}.\]

3.2 Pure vs. mixed: superposition is not ignorance

Compare \(\ket + = (\ket 0 + \ket 1)/\sqrt2\) with the mixture “\(\ket 0\) or \(\ket 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\)”: \[\hat\rho_{\text{pure}} = \ket+\bra+ = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\1 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat\rho_{\text{mix}} = \frac12\bigl(\ket 0\bra 0 + \ket 1\bra 1\bigr) = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.\] In the computational basis both give \(p_0 = p_1 = \tfrac12\). In the \(\{\ket\pm\}\) basis, the pure state gives \(p_+ = 1\), the mixture still \(\tfrac12\). The off-diagonal elements (coherences) carry the phase information that distinguishes a superposition from a classical coin flip.

\(\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho^2 = \sum_j\lambda_j^2 \leq 1\), with equality iff \(\hat\rho\) is pure. The minimum \(1/d\) is reached by the maximally mixed state \(\hat{\mathbb 1}/d\).

3.3 The Bloch ball

Since \(\{\hat{\mathbb 1}, \hat\sigma_x, \hat\sigma_y, \hat\sigma_z\}\) is a basis of Hermitian \(2\times 2\) matrices and \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho = 1\), \[\hat\rho = \frac12\bigl(\hat{\mathbb 1} + \vec r\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma}\bigr) = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 + r_z & r_x - i r_y \\ r_x + i r_y & 1 - r_z\end{pmatrix}, \qquad r_j = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\sigma_j\hat\rho] = \langle\hat\sigma_j\rangle .\]

The eigenvalues of \(\hat\rho\) are \(\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm |\vec r|)\), with eigenvectors \(\ket{\pm_{\hat r}}\) (the pure states along \(\pm\vec r\)). Hence \(\hat\rho \geq 0\) iff \(|\vec r| \leq 1\): qubit states fill the unit ball. Pure states are on the surface (\(|\vec r| = 1\), \(\mathrm{Tr}\hat\rho^2 = \tfrac12(1 + |\vec r|^2) = 1\)); the maximally mixed state is the centre.

Every mixed state is a convex combination of the two pure states along its axis: \(\hat\rho = \lambda_+\ket{+_{\hat r}}\bra{+_{\hat r}} + \lambda_-\ket{-_{\hat r}}\bra{-_{\hat r}}\). Geometrically, mixing pulls the Bloch vector inside the sphere.

For pure states \(\hat\rho_1 = \tfrac12(\hat{\mathbb 1} + \vec r\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma})\), \(\hat\rho_2 = \tfrac12(\hat{\mathbb 1} + \vec s\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma})\), \[|\braket{\psi|\phi}|^2 = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\rho_1\hat\rho_2] = \frac{1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s}{2}.\] Two pure states are orthogonal iff \(\vec r\cdot\vec s = -1\), i.e. iff they are antipodal.

Proof. Expand the product and use \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\sigma_j = 0\), \(\mathrm{Tr}[\hat\sigma_j\hat\sigma_k] = 2\delta_{jk}\): \(\tfrac14(2 + 2\vec r\cdot\vec s)\). \(\square\)

3.4 Many ensembles, one density matrix

The map from ensembles to density operators is many-to-one, and no measurement can tell the ensembles apart.

Source A emits \(\ket 0\) with probability \(\tfrac34\) and \(\ket 1\) with probability \(\tfrac14\). Source B emits \(\ket{\phi_\pm} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\ket 0 \pm \tfrac12\ket 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Then \[\hat\rho_B = \frac12\sum_\pm\ket{\phi_\pm}\bra{\phi_\pm} = \frac34\ket 0\bra 0 + \frac14\ket 1\bra 1 = \hat\rho_A,\] because the cross terms \(\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(\ket 0\bra 1 + \ket 1\bra 0)\) cancel. On the Bloch ball, \(\vec r = (0, 0, \tfrac12)\) is the midpoint of the segment joining the poles with weights \(\tfrac34, \tfrac14\), and also of the segment joining the Bloch vectors of \(\ket{\phi_\pm}\).

In general, \(\{p_k, \ket{\psi_k}\}\) and \(\{q_j, \ket{\phi_j}\}\) give the same \(\hat\rho\) iff \(\sqrt{p_k}\ket{\psi_k} = \sum_j u_{kj}\sqrt{q_j}\ket{\phi_j}\) for some unitary matrix \(u\) (Hughston–Jozsa–Wootters). The density operator is everything that is physically meaningful about a preparation.

Do not read \(\hat\rho\) as “the system is really in one of the \(\ket{\psi_k}\) and we don’t know which”. The same \(\hat\rho\) arises from incompatible stories. For a subsystem of an entangled state (Chapter 5) there is no such story at all.

3.5 Exercises

A qubit has Bloch vector \(\vec r = r(0, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})\), \(r < 1\). Compute the outcome probabilities when measuring \(\hat\sigma_z\) and \(\hat\sigma_x\).

Solution. \(p_{0,1} = \tfrac12(1 \pm r_z) = \tfrac12(1 \pm r/\sqrt2)\); \(p_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm r_x) = \tfrac12\). (Measuring \(\hat\sigma_y\) would give \(\tfrac12(1 \pm r/\sqrt 2)\).)

Give an example of a Hermitian operator that is not positive, and show that a convex combination of density operators is a density operator.

Solution. Any Pauli matrix, e.g. \(\hat\sigma_z\) with eigenvalue \(-1\). For \(\hat\rho = p_1\hat\rho_1 + p_2\hat\rho_2\): \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho = p_1 + p_2 = 1\); \(\hat\rho^\dagger = \hat\rho\) since \(p_i\) are real; \(\braket{\phi|\hat\rho|\phi} = p_1\braket{\phi|\hat\rho_1|\phi} + p_2\braket{\phi|\hat\rho_2|\phi} \geq 0\). It is prepared by tossing a biased coin and preparing \(\hat\rho_1\) or \(\hat\rho_2\).

::: {.definition name="Density operator"}
The **density operator** of the ensemble $\{p_k, \ket{\psi_k}\}$ is
$$\hat\rho = \sum_k p_k\,\ket{\psi_k}\bra{\psi_k}.$$
A state is **pure** if $\hat\rho = \ket\psi\bra\psi$ for some $\ket\psi$, and **mixed** otherwise.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Characterisation"}
An operator $\hat\rho$ is a density operator of some ensemble iff it is

::: {.definition name="Born rule and expectation values"}
For a state $\hat\rho$:
$$p_a = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\Pi_a\hat\rho], \qquad \langle\hat A\rangle = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat A\hat\rho], \qquad \hat\rho \ \xrightarrow{\ a\ }\ \frac{\hat\Pi_a\hat\rho\,\hat\Pi_a}{p_a}.$$
:::

## Pure vs. mixed: superposition is not ignorance

Compare $\ket + = (\ket 0 + \ket 1)/\sqrt2$ with the mixture "$\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$ with probability $\tfrac12$":
$$\hat\rho_{\text{pure}} = \ket+\bra+ = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\1 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat\rho_{\text{mix}} = \frac12\bigl(\ket 0\bra 0 + \ket 1\bra 1\bigr) = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.$$
In the computational basis both give $p_0 = p_1 = \tfrac12$. In the $\{\ket\pm\}$ basis, the pure state gives $p_+ = 1$, the mixture still $\tfrac12$. The **off-diagonal elements** (coherences) carry the phase information that distinguishes a superposition from a classical coin flip.

::: {.definition name="Purity"}
$\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho^2 = \sum_j\lambda_j^2 \leq 1$, with equality iff $\hat\rho$ is pure. The minimum $1/d$ is reached by the **maximally mixed state** $\hat{\mathbb 1}/d$.
:::

## The Bloch ball

Since $\{\hat{\mathbb 1}, \hat\sigma_x, \hat\sigma_y, \hat\sigma_z\}$ is a basis of Hermitian $2\times 2$ matrices and $\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\rho = 1$,
$$\hat\rho = \frac12\bigl(\hat{\mathbb 1} + \vec r\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma}\bigr) = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 + r_z & r_x - i r_y \\ r_x + i r_y & 1 - r_z\end{pmatrix}, \qquad r_j = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\sigma_j\hat\rho] = \langle\hat\sigma_j\rangle .$$

::: {.proposition name="Bloch ball"}
The eigenvalues of $\hat\rho$ are $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm |\vec r|)$, with eigenvectors $\ket{\pm_{\hat r}}$ (the pure states along $\pm\vec r$). Hence $\hat\rho \geq 0$ iff $|\vec r| \leq 1$: qubit states fill the unit **ball**. Pure states are on the surface ($|\vec r| = 1$, $\mathrm{Tr}\hat\rho^2 = \tfrac12(1 + |\vec r|^2) = 1$); the maximally mixed state is the centre.
:::

Every mixed state is a convex combination of the two pure states along its axis: $\hat\rho = \lambda_+\ket{+_{\hat r}}\bra{+_{\hat r}} + \lambda_-\ket{-_{\hat r}}\bra{-_{\hat r}}$. Geometrically, mixing pulls the Bloch vector inside the sphere.

::: {.proposition name="Overlap of pure states"}
For pure states $\hat\rho_1 = \tfrac12(\hat{\mathbb 1} + \vec r\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma})$, $\hat\rho_2 = \tfrac12(\hat{\mathbb 1} + \vec s\cdot\hat{\vec\sigma})$,
$$|\braket{\psi|\phi}|^2 = \mathrm{Tr}[\hat\rho_1\hat\rho_2] = \frac{1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s}{2}.$$
Two pure states are orthogonal iff $\vec r\cdot\vec s = -1$, i.e. iff they are antipodal.
:::

*Proof.* Expand the product and use $\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat\sigma_j = 0$, $\mathrm{Tr}[\hat\sigma_j\hat\sigma_k] = 2\delta_{jk}$: $\tfrac14(2 + 2\vec r\cdot\vec s)$. $\square$

## Many ensembles, one density matrix

The map from ensembles to density operators is many-to-one, and **no measurement can tell the ensembles apart**.

::: {.example name="Two indistinguishable sources"}
Source A emits $\ket 0$ with probability $\tfrac34$ and $\ket 1$ with probability $\tfrac14$. Source B emits $\ket{\phi_\pm} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\ket 0 \pm \tfrac12\ket 1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Then
$$\hat\rho_B = \frac12\sum_\pm\ket{\phi_\pm}\bra{\phi_\pm} = \frac34\ket 0\bra 0 + \frac14\ket 1\bra 1 = \hat\rho_A,$$
because the cross terms $\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(\ket 0\bra 1 + \ket 1\bra 0)$ cancel. On the Bloch ball, $\vec r = (0, 0, \tfrac12)$ is the midpoint of the segment joining the poles with weights $\tfrac34, \tfrac14$, and also of the segment joining the Bloch vectors of $\ket{\phi_\pm}$.
:::

In general, $\{p_k, \ket{\psi_k}\}$ and $\{q_j, \ket{\phi_j}\}$ give the same $\hat\rho$ iff $\sqrt{p_k}\ket{\psi_k} = \sum_j u_{kj}\sqrt{q_j}\ket{\phi_j}$ for some unitary matrix $u$ (Hughston–Jozsa–Wootters). The density operator is everything that is physically meaningful about a preparation.

::: {.callout-important}
Do not read $\hat\rho$ as "the system is really in one of the $\ket{\psi_k}$ and we don't know which". The same $\hat\rho$ arises from incompatible stories. For a subsystem of an entangled state (Chapter 5) there is no such story at all.
:::
