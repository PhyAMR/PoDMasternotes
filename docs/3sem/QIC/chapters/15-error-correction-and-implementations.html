15  Error correction and physical implementations

Quantum algorithms assume perfect gates on perfectly isolated qubits. Real qubits decohere (Chapter 8) and real gates are imprecise. Without a way to fight errors, quantum computation would be a theoretical curiosity. This chapter shows that errors can be corrected despite no-cloning and measurement disturbance, sketches the threshold theorem, surveys alternative computing paradigms (one-way, adiabatic, quantum simulation, boson sampling), and reviews the main physical platforms.

15.1 Why quantum error correction seems impossible

Classical memories protect bits by redundancy: encode \(0 \to 000\), \(1 \to 111\) and decode by majority vote; if each bit flips independently with probability \(p\), the logical error rate drops to \(3p^2 - 2p^3\). Three obstacles seem to forbid a quantum analogue:

  1. No-cloning: we cannot encode \(\ket\psi \to \ket\psi\ket\psi\ket\psi\).
  2. Continuous errors: errors are not just flips but arbitrary small rotations and decoherence.
  3. Measurement destroys: reading the qubits to diagnose an error collapses the superposition.

All three are circumvented.

15.2 The three-qubit bit-flip code

Encode by entangling, not copying: \[\alpha\ket 0 + \beta\ket 1 \;\longmapsto\; \alpha\ket{000} + \beta\ket{111} = \alpha\ket{0_L} + \beta\ket{1_L}.\]

Figure: encoding circuit of the bit-flip code — two CNOTs, no copying.

Suppose at most one qubit suffers an \(X\) error. Measure the two parity checks \(Z_1Z_2\) and \(Z_2Z_3\) (with ancillas and CNOTs, without measuring individual qubits):

syndrome \((Z_1Z_2, Z_2Z_3)\) diagnosis correction
\((+1, +1)\) no error none
\((-1, +1)\) qubit 1 flipped \(X_1\)
\((-1, -1)\) qubit 2 flipped \(X_2\)
\((+1, -1)\) qubit 3 flipped \(X_3\)

The parities have the same value on \(\ket{000}\) and \(\ket{111}\), so the syndrome measurement reveals the error but nothing about \(\alpha, \beta\): the superposition survives. The logical error rate is \(O(p^2)\).

Continuous errors are discretised. A small rotation \(e^{-i\epsilon X_1} = \cos\epsilon\,\hat{\mathbb 1} - i\sin\epsilon\,X_1\) produces a superposition of “no error” and “\(X_1\) error”. The syndrome measurement collapses it onto one of the two, and the correction fixes either. More generally:

If a code corrects a set of error operators \(\{\hat E_k\}\), it corrects every error that is a linear combination of them — in particular every quantum channel whose Kraus operators lie in their span. Correcting \(X\), \(Y = iXZ\), \(Z\) on each qubit (and \(\hat{\mathbb 1}\)) suffices to correct arbitrary single-qubit errors.

15.3 Phase flips and the Shor code

A phase flip \(Z\) is a bit flip in the \(\{\ket\pm\}\) basis, so the phase-flip code \(\ket{0_L} = \ket{+++}\), \(\ket{1_L} = \ket{---}\) (encode as before, then apply \(H^{\otimes 3}\)) corrects one \(Z\) error with syndromes \(X_1X_2\), \(X_2X_3\).

Shor’s nine-qubit code (1995) concatenates the two: \[\ket{0_L} = \frac{(\ket{000} + \ket{111})^{\otimes 3}}{2\sqrt2}, \qquad \ket{1_L} = \frac{(\ket{000} - \ket{111})^{\otimes 3}}{2\sqrt2}.\] Each block of three corrects a bit flip; the relative signs between blocks correct a phase flip. It corrects an arbitrary error on any single qubit — the first proof that quantum error correction is possible. Smaller codes exist (Steane’s 7-qubit code, the 5-qubit perfect code), and all of them fit the stabilizer formalism: the code space is the joint \(+1\) eigenspace of a group of commuting Pauli products, whose measured eigenvalues form the syndrome.

If every physical component (gate, preparation, measurement, idle step) fails independently with probability below a threshold \(p_{\text{th}}\), then arbitrarily long quantum computations can be performed reliably, with overhead polylogarithmic in the size of the computation, using concatenated fault-tolerant codes.

Thresholds range from \(\sim 10^{-4}\) for concatenated codes to \(\sim 1\%\) for the surface code, a two-dimensional lattice code with only nearest-neighbour parity checks — the current front-runner, whose below-threshold operation has been demonstrated on superconducting processors. The overhead is still large: thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit for algorithms like Shor’s.

15.4 Other paradigms of quantum computation

One-way (measurement-based) computing. Prepare a highly entangled cluster state (qubits in \(\ket +\) entangled by CZ gates on a lattice), then compute by single-qubit measurements only, choosing each measurement basis from earlier outcomes (feed-forward). Teleportation-like corrections propagate the logical state; the entanglement of the cluster is the resource consumed. It is natural for photons, where entangling gates are probabilistic but measurements are easy.

Adiabatic quantum computing. Encode the answer in the ground state of a problem Hamiltonian \(\hat H_P\); start in the easy ground state of \(\hat H_0\) and slowly interpolate \(\hat H(t) = (1 - t/T)\hat H_0 + (t/T)\hat H_P\). By the adiabatic theorem the system stays in the ground state if \(T = O(1/g^2)\), where \(g\) is the minimum spectral gap along the path. It is polynomially equivalent to the circuit model; the hard instances are those with exponentially small gaps. Quantum annealing (D-Wave) is its noisy, finite-temperature heuristic version for optimisation problems, using quantum fluctuations to escape local minima — sometimes outperforming simulated annealing, with no proven general advantage.

Quantum simulation. Feynman’s original motivation: use a controllable quantum system to simulate another. Digital simulation decomposes \(e^{-i\hat Ht}\) into gates (Trotter formulas); analog simulation engineers a device whose Hamiltonian mimics the target. Ultracold atoms in optical lattices realise the Bose–Hubbard model \[\hat H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j + \frac U2\sum_i\hat n_i(\hat n_i - 1),\] (\(J\) hopping between neighbouring sites, \(U\) on-site interaction), observing the superfluid–Mott-insulator transition.

Boson sampling and quantum advantage. Sending \(n\) indistinguishable photons through a random \(m\)-mode linear interferometer and sampling the output pattern is believed classically hard (the amplitudes are matrix permanents, which are #P-hard; Aaronson–Arkhipov 2011), yet it needs no entangling gates. Boson sampling and random-circuit sampling (Google’s Sycamore, 2019) were the first experiments claiming quantum computational advantage — on tasks of no practical use, and with classical simulation algorithms continually narrowing the gap. Reprogrammable linear-optical circuits can implement any linear-optical protocol up to their size.

15.5 Physical implementations

DiVincenzo’s criteria for a quantum computer: (1) scalable, well-characterised qubits; (2) initialisation to a simple state; (3) coherence times much longer than gate times; (4) a universal gate set; (5) qubit-specific measurement (plus, for networks, interconversion with flying qubits and their faithful transmission). Main platforms:

  • Photons: long coherence, room temperature, natural for communication; two-photon gates are probabilistic (KLM scheme with linear optics, ancillas and feed-forward, 2001) — hence measurement-based and fusion-based architectures.
  • Trapped ions: qubits in hyperfine or optical levels, gates via laser-driven collective motion; record fidelities and all-to-all connectivity; slower gates, scaling via shuttling or photonic links.
  • Neutral atoms in optical lattices or tweezer arrays: large, reconfigurable arrays, Rydberg-mediated gates, natural for analog simulation.
  • NMR: nuclear spins in molecules, manipulated by RF pulses; the first demonstrations of quantum algorithms (Shor’s factoring of 15 in 2001) but not scalable (ensemble, pseudo-pure states).
  • Quantum dots: electron spins in semiconductors, compatible with silicon fabrication.
  • Superconducting qubits: Josephson-junction circuits (transmons) at millikelvin, microwave control and readout, fast gates; the basis of IBM’s cloud-accessible processors (programmable through the IBM Quantum Composer) and of Google’s devices.
  • Topological qubits (pursued by Microsoft): information stored non-locally in exotic quasiparticles, intrinsically protected from local noise — still at an early experimental stage.

Today’s processors belong to the NISQ era (noisy intermediate-scale quantum): tens to hundreds of qubits without full error correction, used for variational algorithms, simulation and benchmarking while fault tolerance is developed.

15.6 Exercises

For the bit-flip code with independent flip probability \(p\) per qubit and perfect correction of single flips, show that the logical error probability is \(3p^2 - 2p^3\) and find when encoding helps.

Solution. The code fails iff two or three qubits flip: \(3p^2(1 - p) + p^3 = 3p^2 - 2p^3\). This is smaller than \(p\) iff \(3p - 2p^2 < 1\), i.e. \(p < \tfrac12\).

Show that the syndrome measurement of \(Z_1Z_2\) does not disturb \(\alpha\ket{000} + \beta\ket{111}\), while measuring \(Z_1\) would.

Solution. Both \(\ket{000}\) and \(\ket{111}\) are \(+1\) eigenstates of \(Z_1Z_2\), so the encoded state is an eigenstate and the measurement leaves it unchanged. \(Z_1\) has eigenvalue \(+1\) on \(\ket{000}\) and \(-1\) on \(\ket{111}\): measuring it collapses the state to \(\ket{000}\) or \(\ket{111}\), destroying the superposition.
