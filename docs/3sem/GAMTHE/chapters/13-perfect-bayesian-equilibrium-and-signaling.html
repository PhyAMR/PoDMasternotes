13  Perfect Bayesian equilibrium and signaling

Static Bayesian games are solved by Bayesian Nash equilibrium; dynamic games of complete information by subgame-perfect equilibrium. When both features are present — a player with private information moves, and others react after observing the move — neither concept is enough: Nature’s initial move merges all subtrees into a single subgame, so subgame perfection loses its bite. This chapter introduces beliefs at every information set and the resulting perfect Bayesian equilibrium, then applies it to signaling games, reputation building, and cheap talk.

13.1 Why SPE is not enough

Game Solution concept
static, complete information Nash equilibrium
dynamic, complete information subgame-perfect equilibrium (backward induction if perfect information)
static, incomplete information Bayesian Nash equilibrium
dynamic, incomplete information perfect Bayesian equilibrium

Entry with an uncertain entrant. Let the entrant now have two types: Competitive (probability \(p = \tfrac12\)), with the payoffs of Chapter 12 (entry is met by Accept \((1,1)\) or Fight \((-1,-1)\)), or Weak, for whom entering is always bad: Accept gives \((-1, 1)\), Fight \((-2, 0)\). Staying out gives \((0,2)\) to either type. The incumbent observes entry but not the type, so it cannot apply backward induction. The entrant’s strategies are \(OO, OE, EO, EE\) (action of C, action of W):

1 \ 2 F A
OO 0, 2 0, 2
OE −1, 1 −½, 3/2
EO −½, ½ ½, 3/2
EE −3/2, −½ 0, 1

There are two pure NE: \((EO, A)\) — only the competitive entrant enters and the incumbent accepts — and \((OO, F)\) — nobody enters because the incumbent threatens to fight. The second is not sequentially rational: whoever entered, fighting is worse than accepting for the incumbent (\(-1 < 1\) against C, \(0 < 1\) against W). Yet \((OO, F)\) is an SPE, because the only subgame is the whole game. We need a refinement that can evaluate behaviour at information sets that are off the equilibrium path.

13.2 Beliefs and perfect Bayesian equilibrium

Given a BNE, an information set is on the equilibrium path if it is reached with positive probability (given the type distribution), and off the path otherwise. In \((OO, F)\) the incumbent’s information set is off the path.

A system of beliefs \(\mu\) assigns to every information set a probability distribution over its nodes: \(\mu(x)\) is the probability that the player believes they are at node \(x\) when the information set \(h(x)\) is reached.

On the path, beliefs should be conditional probabilities, \(\mu(x) = \Pr[x] / \Pr[h(x)]\) by Bayes’ rule.

A perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE) is a pair \((s^*, \mu)\) of a strategy profile and a system of beliefs such that:

  1. every player has beliefs at every information set where they move;
  2. on the equilibrium path, beliefs are derived from \(s^*\) and the prior by Bayes’ rule;
  3. off the equilibrium path, beliefs are arbitrary;
  4. given the beliefs, strategies are sequentially rational: at every information set the player’s continuation strategy is a best response.

A PBE is not just a strategy profile: the beliefs are part of it.

Let \(x_1\) (\(x_2\)) be the incumbent’s node after entry by C (by W), and let the types enter with probabilities \(q_C\), \(q_W\). By Bayes’ rule \[\mu(x_1) = \frac{p\,q_C}{p\,q_C + (1-p)\,q_W}.\] \((EO, A)\) is a PBE with \(\mu(x_1) = 1\): the incumbent knows that an entrant is competitive and accepts. \((OO, F)\) is not: Bayes’ rule cannot be applied (\(q_C = q_W = 0\)), but for any belief Fight is not a best response, so requirement 4 fails. Compare with off-path choices in SPE.

If a profile \(s^*\) (possibly mixed) reaches every information set with positive probability, then \(s^*\) together with the beliefs derived by Bayes’ rule is a PBE if and only if \(s^*\) is a BNE.

13.3 Refinements: sequential equilibrium

The definition above is sometimes called weak PBE because requirement 3 lets off-path beliefs be anything, even inconsistent with the structure of the game.

Nature picks node \(x_1\) or \(x_2\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each; player 1 does not observe it and chooses U (payoff \(1\) for player 1, game over) or D. After D, player 2 (who doesn’t observe Nature either) chooses L, giving \((2, 2)\) at both nodes, or R, giving \((0, 3)\) at \(x_1\) and \((0, 0)\) at \(x_2\). If player 1 plays D, Bayes’ rule gives \(\mu(x_1) = \tfrac12\); R is then worth \(1.5 < 2\), so L, and D is optimal for player 1: the PBE \((D, L)\). But if player 1 never plays D, any belief is admissible; with \(\mu(x_1) > \tfrac23\), R is optimal, and against R player 1 prefers U. So \((U, R)\) is also a (weak) PBE — supported by the belief that player 1’s non-move somehow reveals Nature’s choice, which player 1 does not even observe.

An assessment \((s^*, \mu)\) is consistent if it is the limit of a sequence \((s^k, \mu^k)\) of fully mixed strategy profiles \(s^k\) and the beliefs \(\mu^k\) derived from them by Bayes’ rule. A sequential equilibrium is a consistent, sequentially rational assessment.

In the odd example, every fully mixed profile gives \(\mu^k(x_1) = \tfrac12\), so consistency forces \(\mu(x_1) = \tfrac12\) and eliminates \((U, R)\). Sequential equilibrium can still be insufficient and is much harder to check; in practice the solution concept is chosen case by case — these distinctions are also useful to argue about how “rational” human behaviour is.

13.4 Signaling games

When the typed player moves first, the move itself may reveal information. If instead only the second mover has types, the first can only guess the reaction from the prior: a screening game (think of a quiz show with a prize behind a screen), where SPE is enough.

A two-player dynamic Bayesian game in which:

  1. Nature chooses the type of player 1 (the sender);
  2. player 1 observes the type and chooses an action (the signal), with at least as many actions as types;
  3. player 2 (the receiver) observes the signal, not the type, updates beliefs, and acts;
  4. player 2 cares about player 1’s type (common values).

In a separating equilibrium different types send different signals, so the signal reveals the type. In a pooling equilibrium all types send the same signal, which carries no information. Hybrid (semi-separating, partially pooling) equilibria have some types randomising so that some signals are partially informative.

Separating equilibria have easy beliefs (each signal points to one type); in pooling equilibria the on-path signal leaves the prior unchanged, while the unused signal is off the path and its beliefs must be chosen to support the equilibrium.

13.4.1 A recipe for pure PBE in signaling games

With two sender types and two messages, there are only four sender strategies to try:

  1. Fix a candidate: pooling (both types send the same message) or separating (they send different ones).
  2. Beliefs on the path follow from Bayes’ rule: after a separating message the type is known; after a pooling message the belief is the prior.
  3. Beliefs off the path (after a message nobody sends) are free in a PBE. Find the range of beliefs that makes the receiver’s prescribed response optimal.
  4. Receiver: check that the response to each message is a best reply to the belief.
  5. Sender: check that no type gains by switching message, given the receiver’s responses.

A pooling candidate survives if some off-path belief deters deviation. A separating candidate needs no off-path beliefs, but each type must prefer its own message.

Revealing (verifiable) actions. Some messages cannot lie: a medical test, a public audit, a costly campaign that exposes the product. After such an action the receiver’s information set is a singleton and backward induction applies directly. Only the non-revealing message needs a belief. The game then shrinks: the receiver has one real decision, and the sender asks whether revealing is worth its cost for each type. Typically the “good” types reveal and the “bad” types stay silent, so silence itself becomes informative (unraveling).

13.5 Worked example: a coffee for Brian

Brian is invited for coffee by his colleague Zoe. His partner Ann is Jealous with probability \(0.8\) or Easygoing with probability \(0.2\). Ann may stay Silent or Trash Zoe; Brian observes the signal and Comes to the coffee or Declines.

Figure: the signaling game in “butterfly” form; payoffs are (Ann, Brian). Brian’s information sets join the nodes after the same signal.

Jealous Ann is deeply hurt if Brian goes; easygoing Ann is only mildly annoyed; trashing Zoe costs Ann \(2\); Brian enjoys the coffee (\(2\)) only if Ann is easygoing and suffers (\(-3\)) if she is jealous.

Step 1: the normal form. Ann’s strategy lists the signal of each type (\(ST\): silent if jealous, trash if easygoing); Brian’s lists the reply to each signal (\(CD\): come after S, decline after T). Careful: Ann’s pair is indexed by types, Brian’s by signals, so filling e.g. \((TS, CD)\) requires matching Brian’s reply to the signal each type sends.

Ann \ Brian CC CD DC DD
SS −5, −2 −5, −2 3, 0 3, 0
ST −5, −2 −4.6, −2.4 2.2, 0.4 2.6, 0
TS −5, −2 0.6, 0.4 −4.2, −2.4 1.4, 0
TT −5, −2 1, 0 −5, −2 1, 0

There are five Nash equilibria: three pure, \((SS, DD)\), \((SS, DC)\), \((TT, CD)\), and two mixed, \((TT, \tfrac12 CD + \tfrac12 DD)\) and \((\tfrac16 SS + \tfrac56 TS,\ \tfrac29 CD + \tfrac79 DD)\).

Step 2: beliefs. Let \(\mu_S\) (\(\mu_T\)) be Brian’s belief that Ann is jealous after S (after T). Coming is worth \(-3\mu + 2(1-\mu)\) to Brian, declining \(0\): he comes iff \(\mu \leq 0.4\).

  • No separating PBE. If Ann played \(ST\), beliefs would be \(\mu_S = 1\), \(\mu_T = 0\) and Brian would reply \(DC\); but against \(DC\) Ann prefers \(SS\). Similarly \(TS\) induces \(CD\), against which Ann prefers \(TT\).
  • PBE 1: \((SS, DD)\) with \(\mu_S = 0.8\) (the prior, since both types are silent) and off-path \(\mu_T \geq 0.4\) (so that declining after T is optimal). Ann has no reason to trash Zoe since Brian declines anyway.
  • PBE 2: \((SS, DC)\) with \(\mu_S = 0.8\), \(\mu_T \leq 0.4\): Brian still declines in practice, but would come if Ann trashed Zoe, because he would read it as a sign that she is easygoing.
  • PBE 3: \((TT, CD)\) with \(\mu_T = 0.8\) and off-path \(\mu_S \leq 0.4\).
  • PBE 4: Ann plays \(TT\); Brian declines after T (\(\mu_T = 0.8\)); off the path, Brian believes \(\mu_S = 0.4\) — which makes him indifferent — and comes with probability \(q\) after S. Ann must not want to deviate to S: the easygoing type requires \(-q + 3(1-q) \leq 1\), i.e. \(q \geq \tfrac12\). So this is a continuum of PBE (with \(q = 1\) recovering PBE 3); note that \((TT, DD)\) itself is not an equilibrium.
  • PBE 5 (semi-separating): easygoing Ann is always silent; jealous Ann is silent with probability \(\tfrac16\). T is sent only by the jealous type, so \(\mu_T = 1\) and Brian declines. After S, Bayes’ rule gives \[\mu_S = \frac{0.8 \cdot \tfrac16}{0.8 \cdot \tfrac16 + 0.2} = 0.4,\] exactly the value that makes Brian indifferent, so he can mix. He comes with probability \(\tfrac29\) after S, which makes jealous Ann indifferent between S (\(-6 \cdot \tfrac29 + 3 \cdot \tfrac79 = 1\)) and T (\(1\)).

The recipe for signaling games: (1) write the normal form, minding the type/signal indexing; (2) find all NE; (3) for each, compute on-path beliefs by Bayes’ rule and find off-path beliefs that make the receiver’s prescribed replies optimal — if none exist, the NE is not a PBE.

13.6 Education as a signal and the intuitive criterion

Spence’s (1973) job-market model shows how a costly action can transmit information even when it is useless in itself. A worker is of High productivity (\(2\)) with probability \(p\) or Low (\(1\)). Before being hired, the worker chooses an education level \(e \geq 0\), which costs \(e\) to a Low type and \(e/2\) to a High type. Competitive employers pay the expected productivity given their belief after observing \(e\).

  • Separating PBE. High chooses \(e^*\) and Low chooses \(0\); the wage is \(2\) after \(e^*\) and \(1\) otherwise. Low must not mimic, \(1 \geq 2 - e^*\); High must prefer to signal, \(2 - e^*/2 \geq 1\). Any \(e^* \in [1, 2]\) works, supported by the belief “anyone with \(e < e^*\) is Low”.
  • Pooling PBE. Both choose \(e = 0\) and receive \(\bar w = 1 + p\), with pessimistic beliefs off the path.

Perfect Bayesian equilibrium allows any belief after an unexpected action, hence the multiplicity. The intuitive criterion (Cho and Kreps, 1987) asks: could this deviation possibly benefit this type? If a deviation can only benefit one type, the responder should believe it comes from that type.

  • In the separating family, an education level \(e \in (1, e^*)\) would never pay for a Low worker (even with wage \(2\), \(2 - e < 1\)), but would pay for a High one. So the beliefs “\(e < e^*\) means Low” are unreasonable, and only the least-cost separating equilibrium \(e^* = 1\) survives (the Riley outcome).
  • The pooling equilibrium always fails the criterion (for \(p < 1\)). Any \(e \in (1 - p,\ 2(1 - p))\) cannot benefit Low even at wage \(2\) (\(2 - e < 1 + p\)), but if read as High it gives High \(2 - e/2 > 1 + p\).

The lessons generalise: signals work because they are costlier for the types you want to separate from (single crossing), and equilibrium refinements select the cheapest credible signal. In networks the same logic underlies proof-of-work, costly authentication and reputation deposits.

13.7 Reputation building

In a finitely repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma the unique SPE is to defect always. A little uncertainty about types changes this dramatically (Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts and Wilson, 1982).

Stage payoffs are \((C,C) = (1,1)\), \((C,D) = (-1,2)\), \((D,C) = (2,-1)\), \((D,D) = (0,0)\). Player B is strategic. Player A is a grim-trigger type with probability \(p\) (cooperates until B defects, then defects forever) or strategic with probability \(1 - p\).

  • Two rounds. In round 2 strategic players defect. In round 1 strategic A defects, and B, cooperating, gets \(p(1) + (1-p)(-1)\) in round 1 plus \(2p\) in round 2 (exploiting the grim-trigger type, who still cooperates): total \(4p - 1\). Defecting gives \(2p\) (round 1) and \(0\) after. B cooperates in round 1 iff \(p > \tfrac12\).
  • Three rounds. If \(p > \tfrac12\), the unique PBE has both B and the strategic A cooperating in round 1. Strategic A pools with the grim-trigger type: by cooperating he keeps B’s belief at \(p > \tfrac12\), so B keeps cooperating in round 2, and A earns \(1 + 2 + 0\) instead of \(2 + 0 + 0\) from revealing himself. B, in turn, cooperates in round 1 even if he expected the strategic A to defect, since \(5p - 1 > 2p\) for \(p > \tfrac13\).

Strategic players build a reputation: they defect at the end, but to an outside observer they cooperate. In general, in a \(T\)-stage game the number of rounds in which a strategic player defects is bounded by a constant depending on \(p\) but not on \(T\).

The tough negotiator. In an ultimatum game the proposer offers a High share (payoffs \((2,1)\) if accepted) or a Moderate one (\((1,2)\)); refusal gives \((0,0)\). The unique SPE is \((H, \text{always accept})\). Now let the responder be a “jerk” with probability \(p\), who finds H worse than nothing (payoff \(-1\)). In one round the proposer offers H iff \(2(1-p) > 1\), i.e. \(p < \tfrac12\). With a second round (after a refusal the proposer offers again, payoffs discounted by \(\delta\)) the game becomes intractable in normal form — the responder has \(2^{12}\) strategies — but sequential rationality reduces it. With \(\delta = 0.9\) and \(p = 0.1\) the unique PBE is semi-separating: H is always offered first; the jerk always refuses; a normal responder refuses with probability \(\tfrac19\) (so that after a refusal \(\mu_H = \Pr[\text{jerk} \mid \text{refusal}] = \tfrac12\), making the proposer indifferent between H, worth \(2\delta(1-\mu_H)\), and M, worth \(\delta\)); in round 2 the proposer repeats H with probability \(\tfrac89\) (making the normal responder indifferent: \(\delta[\tfrac89 \cdot 1 + \tfrac19 \cdot 2] = 1\)). Even an unlikely “tough” type gives the responder bargaining power: a normal responder sometimes mimics it.

13.8 Cheap talk

In cheap talk (Crawford and Sobel, 1982), Nature reveals a state \(t\) to player 1 only; player 1 sends a message that costs nothing and has no direct effect on payoffs; player 2 then acts. The question is how much information costless messages can transmit when interests are not aligned.

Player 2 found a job in city A and chooses where to live, \(a \in \{1, \dots, 5\}\) (1 = city A, 5 = city B where player 1 lives). The best location depends on traffic conditions \(t \in \{1, 3, 5\}\), equally likely and known only to player 1. Player 2’s payoff is \(5 - (t - a)^2\); player 1 is biased towards having the friend close: \(5 - (t + b - a)^2\) with \(b = 1.1\).

  1. No truthful PBE. If messages were believed, the type \(t = 3\) would claim “5”: the friend would choose \(a = 5\), giving player 1 \(5 - 0.81 = 4.19\) instead of \(5 - 1.21 = 3.79\).
  2. A babbling PBE always exists: messages are uninformative and player 2 chooses \(a = 3\) from the prior.
  3. A partially informative PBE: player 1 reports \(t = 1\) truthfully but pools \(t \in \{3, 5\}\) into “high”. Player 2 then chooses \(a = 1\) or \(a = 4\). Type 1 prefers the truth (\(3.79\) vs. \(1.39\)); types 3 and 5 prefer “high”.

With a continuous state \(t \in [0, 1]\) and quadratic losses, there is never a truthful equilibrium, there is always a babbling one, and a two-message (threshold) equilibrium exists iff the bias is small enough (\(b < \tfrac14\) for uniform \(t\)). Applications include legislative committees and expert advisers: with the decision maker’s payoff \(-(t - a)^2\), \(t \sim \mathcal U[-w, w]\), and the adviser’s \(-(t + b - a)^2\), informative advice (under open or closed rules) is possible only if \(b \leq w\).

The “negative” results of cheap talk justify a common engineering practice: protocols exchange coarse information — congested / not congested, low / high battery — rather than exact values. With slightly misaligned incentives, more detail would not be credible anyway.

13.9 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 6.

(BM 6.6) Mark buys trousers Immediately at €50 or Waits, in which case Nadia Keeps the price or Lowers it to €30 at the end of the week. Mark values them at €100. Late transactions are discounted by \(\delta = 0.8\). Nadia’s cost is €20 with probability \(0.75\) (and lowering the price then also brings €20 of goodwill, not discounted) or €35. Find the BNE and the SPE.

Solution. Nadia’s strategies are (cost 20, cost 35). Waiting gives Mark \(40\) (K) or \(56\) (L). It gives Nadia \(24\) (K) or \(28\) (L) at cost 20, and \(12\) (K) or \(-4\) (L) at cost 35. Buying immediately gives Mark \(50\) and Nadia \(30\) or \(15\). Normal form, Mark W against KK, KL, LK, LL: \((40, 21)\), \((44, 17)\), \((52, 24)\), \((56, 20)\); Mark I: \((50, 26.25)\). BNE: \((I, KK)\), \((I, KL)\), \((W, LK)\). Sequential rationality requires L at cost 20 and K at cost 35, so Nadia plays LK. Then Mark waits (\(52 > 50\)). The unique SPE is \((W, LK)\). (The printed payoffs contain small arithmetic slips; the equilibria are unchanged.)

(BM 6.7) Janine brings food from Home (payoff \(2\); stand \(0\)) or Eats at the stand. The worker there is Alfred (\(0.8\)) or Brianna (\(0.2\)) and Gives a sandwich (Janine \(5\), worker \(1\)) or Denies it (Janine \(0\), worker \(0\); but Brianna, who dislikes Janine, gets \(5\)). Find the BNE and the SPE.

Solution. The stand’s strategies are (Alfred, Brianna). E against GG, GD, DG, DD gives \((5, 1)\), \((4, 1.8)\), \((1, 0.2)\), \((0, 1)\); H gives \((2, 0)\). BNE: \((E, GD)\), \((H, DG)\), \((H, DD)\). Sequential rationality: Alfred gives, Brianna denies, so GD, and Janine eats (\(4 > 2\)). SPE \((E, GD)\); the other two rest on Alfred threatening to deny. (The printed expected payoffs of the stand are slightly off: \(1.8\), not \(2.8\).)

(BM 6.8) Riccardo is infected with probability \(0.2\). At the border he takes a Swab (revealing his status; cost \(10\)) or Talks. The officer Admits or Denies. Riccardo gets \(20\) if admitted, \(0\) otherwise. The officer gets \(5\) for admitting a healthy traveller, \(-50\) for an infected one, \(0\) for denying. Find the PBE and classify it.

Solution. After a swab the officer knows the type: he admits the healthy (Riccardo \(10\)) and denies the infected (\(-10\)). After T, let \(\mu\) be the officer’s belief that Riccardo is infected; admitting pays \(5 - 55\mu\), so he admits iff \(\mu \leq 1/11\). The infected type never swabs (\(-10 < 0\)). If the healthy type talked too (pooling), \(\mu = 0.2 > 1/11\) and the officer would deny, so the healthy type would rather swab (\(10 > 0\)). PBE: infected Talks, healthy Swabs, the officer believes \(\mu(T) = 1\) and Denies after T, and admits after a negative swab. It is separating: the costly swab is the signal only the healthy type is willing to send.

(BM 6.9) John and Mary exchange gifts at Christmas and on Valentine’s Day (two rounds, \(\delta = 1\)): a gift costs the giver \(5\) and is worth \(12\) to the receiver. Mary is strategic. With probability \(p\) John is a tit-for-tat type (gives at Christmas, then copies Mary); otherwise he is strategic. (a) Payoffs if \(p = 0\)? (b) Probability that John does not give at Christmas? (c) When does Mary give at Christmas?

Solution.

  1. A twice-repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma: \((N, N)\) twice, payoffs \(0\). (b) Only the strategic John withholds at Christmas: probability \(1 - p\). (c) Mary never gives on Valentine’s Day (the last round). If she withholds at Christmas she gets \(12p\) (the tit-for-tat John’s Christmas gift). If she gives, she gets \(p(12 - 5 + 12) - 5(1 - p) = 24p - 5\): tit-for-tat John rewards her on Valentine’s Day. She gives iff \(24p - 5 > 12p\), i.e. \(p > 5/12\). A modest probability of a reciprocating type makes even a selfish player cooperate early: the seed of the reputation results of Kreps–Milgrom–Roberts–Wilson.

(BM 6.10) A clerk Stays put (bandit \(100\), clerk \(0\)) or Punches a bandit whose gun is fake with probability \(p\). After a punch the bandit Reacts or Flees. Fake gun and react: both \(-10\). Real gun and react: clerk \(-100\), bandit \(80\). Flee: clerk \(10\), bandit \(0\). Find the BNE as a function of \(p\) and the SPE.

Solution. The bandit’s strategies are (fake, real). P against RR, RF, FR, FF: clerk \(90p - 100\), \(10 - 20p\), \(110p - 100\), \(10\). S gives \(0\) against everything. Sequential rationality: flee with a fake gun (\(0 > -10\)) and react with a real one (\(80 > 0\)), i.e. FR. The clerk punches iff \(110p - 100 \geq 0\), i.e. \(p \geq 10/11\). BNE: \((S, RR)\) for all \(p\); \((S, RF)\) for \(p \geq 1/2\); \((S, FR)\) for \(p \leq 10/11\); \((P, FR)\) for \(p \geq 10/11\). Only those with FR are SPE. The clerk should play the hero only if he is almost sure the gun is fake.

(BM 6.12) A start-up’s gadget is Effective or Useless (probability \(\tfrac12\) each). It runs a Campaign (cost \(20\), reveals the type) or keeps a Low profile. The incumbent Fights or Yields. Yield: \(50\) each. Fight: \((80, 0)\) if Effective, \((0, 80)\) if Useless. Find the PBE.

Solution. After a campaign the incumbent yields to an effective gadget (\(50 > 0\)) and fights a useless one (\(80 > 50\)), so C yields \(30\) (effective) or \(-20\) (useless). After L, with belief \(\mu = \Pr(\text{effective})\), fighting pays \(80(1 - \mu)\) against \(50\) for yielding. Normal form (start-up strategies (effective, useless), incumbent’s answer to L): CC \((5, 65)\) for both answers; CL \((15, 65)\) / \((40, 50)\); LC \((30, 40)\) / \((15, 65)\); LL \((40, 40)\) / \((50, 50)\). LL is strictly dominant. With \(\mu = 1/2\) the incumbent yields (\(50 > 40\)). Pooling PBE: both types keep a low profile, the incumbent believes the prior after L and yields. The campaign is too expensive to be worth revealing even good news.

(BM 6.16) A student’s green pass is valid (\(0.9\)) or fake (\(0.1\)). The student Displays or Hides it; the professor Admits or sends Online, unable to tell fakes apart. Valid: admitted \((10, 10)\); displayed but sent online: professor \(-50\); otherwise \(0\). Fake: admitted after display \((-100, -100)\); admitted without display \((-50, -200)\); displayed and sent online: student \(-200\); otherwise \(0\). Find the pure PBE.

Solution. Student strategies (valid, fake), professor strategies (after D, after H). The pure NE are \((DH, AO)\) and \((HH, OO)\). Separating \((DH, AO)\): valid passes are displayed, fakes hidden. Beliefs \(\mu(\text{valid} \mid D) = 1\) and \(\mu(\text{valid} \mid H) = 0\); admitting after D and sending online after H are optimal. Pooling \((HH, OO)\): nobody displays, and \(\mu(\text{valid} \mid H) = 0.9\) makes O optimal (\(0 > 0.9\cdot10 - 0.1\cdot200 = -11\)). D is off the path, so its belief is free. Admitting after D pays \(110\mu - 100\) and online pays \(-50\mu\), so O after D is optimal iff \(\mu(\text{valid} \mid D) \leq 5/8\). The pooling PBE needs pessimistic off-path beliefs. By the intuitive criterion, displaying could only benefit the valid type, so the pooling equilibrium is not reasonable.

From the course material:

A young cowboy Y, insulted by an outlaw O, can let it slide (\((-20, 0)\)) or challenge O. O can apologise (\((10, -10)\)) or duel. If O is a sharpshooter (prior \(p\)) the duel gives \((-100, 20)\); otherwise each wins with probability \(\tfrac12\), the winner getting \(20\) and the loser \(-100\). Find the PBE for \(p = 0.2\).

Solution. For a non-sharpshooter the duel is worth \(\tfrac12 \cdot 20 + \tfrac12(-100) = -40\) to both players. Sequential rationality: a sharpshooter duels (\(20 > -10\)); a fake apologises (\(-10 > -40\)). Then challenging gives Y \(p(-100) + (1-p)(10) = 0.2(-100) + 0.8 \cdot 10 = -12 > -20\), so Y challenges. The unique PBE (indeed the unique SPE of the type-agent tree) is: Y challenges; a sharpshooter O duels, a fake O apologises. Y would let it slide only if \(-100p + 10(1-p) < -20\), i.e. \(p > 3/11 \approx 0.27\). This is a screening game — the uninformed player moves first and O knows his own type — so beliefs are trivial and SPE suffices; in normal form there is also a NE in which Y lets it slide and O threatens to duel whatever his type, but it is not sequentially rational.

In the coffee game, check that \((TT, DD)\) is not a Nash equilibrium and explain in words why PBE 4 needs Brian to come after S with probability at least \(\tfrac12\).

Solution. Against \(DD\), Ann gets \(3\) from \(SS\) and \(1\) from \(TT\): she deviates to silence. In PBE 4 Brian’s reply to the off-path signal S must deter both types from switching to S. The jealous type gets \(-6q + 3(1-q) \leq 1\) iff \(q \geq \tfrac29\); the easygoing type gets \(-q + 3(1-q) \leq 1\) iff \(q \geq \tfrac12\). The binding constraint is the easygoing type’s, hence \(q \geq \tfrac12\).

title: "Perfect Bayesian equilibrium and signaling"
---

Static Bayesian games are solved by Bayesian Nash equilibrium; dynamic games of complete information by subgame-perfect equilibrium. When both features are present — a player with private information moves, and others react after observing the move — neither concept is enough: Nature's initial move merges all subtrees into a single subgame, so subgame perfection loses its bite. This chapter introduces **beliefs** at every information set and the resulting **perfect Bayesian equilibrium**, then applies it to signaling games, reputation building, and cheap talk.

## Why SPE is not enough

| Game | Solution concept |
|:--|:--|
| static, complete information | Nash equilibrium |
| dynamic, complete information | subgame-perfect equilibrium (backward induction if perfect information) |
| static, incomplete information | Bayesian Nash equilibrium |
| dynamic, incomplete information | **perfect Bayesian equilibrium** |

**Entry with an uncertain entrant.** Let the *entrant* now have two types: **C**ompetitive (probability $p = \tfrac12$), with the payoffs of Chapter 12 (entry is met by Accept $(1,1)$ or Fight $(-1,-1)$), or **W**eak, for whom entering is always bad: Accept gives $(-1, 1)$, Fight $(-2, 0)$. Staying out gives $(0,2)$ to either type. The incumbent observes entry but not the type, so it cannot apply backward induction. The entrant's strategies are $OO, OE, EO, EE$ (action of C, action of W):

| 1 \\ 2 | F | A |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **OO** | 0, 2 | 0, 2 |
| **OE** | −1, 1 | −½, 3/2 |
| **EO** | −½, ½ | ½, 3/2 |
| **EE** | −3/2, −½ | 0, 1 |

There are two pure NE: $(EO, A)$ — only the competitive entrant enters and the incumbent accepts — and $(OO, F)$ — nobody enters because the incumbent threatens to fight. The second is not sequentially rational: *whoever* entered, fighting is worse than accepting for the incumbent ($-1 < 1$ against C, $0 < 1$ against W). Yet $(OO, F)$ is an SPE, because the only subgame is the whole game. We need a refinement that can evaluate behaviour at information sets that are off the equilibrium path.

## Beliefs and perfect Bayesian equilibrium

::: {.definition name="Equilibrium path in Bayesian games"}
Given a BNE, an information set is **on the equilibrium path** if it is reached with positive probability (given the type distribution), and **off the path** otherwise. In $(OO, F)$ the incumbent's information set is off the path.
:::

::: {.definition name="System of beliefs"}
A **system of beliefs** $\mu$ assigns to every information set a probability distribution over its nodes: $\mu(x)$ is the probability that the player believes they are at node $x$ when the information set $h(x)$ is reached.
:::

On the path, beliefs should be conditional probabilities, $\mu(x) = \Pr[x] / \Pr[h(x)]$ by Bayes' rule.

::: {.definition name="Perfect Bayesian equilibrium"}
A **perfect Bayesian equilibrium** (PBE) is a pair $(s^*, \mu)$ of a strategy profile and a system of beliefs such that:

1. every player has beliefs at every information set where they move;
2. on the equilibrium path, beliefs are derived from $s^*$ and the prior by Bayes' rule;
3. off the equilibrium path, beliefs are arbitrary;
4. given the beliefs, strategies are **sequentially rational**: at every information set the player's continuation strategy is a best response.
:::

A PBE is not just a strategy profile: the beliefs are part of it.

::: {.example name="Entry, solved"}
Let $x_1$ ($x_2$) be the incumbent's node after entry by C (by W), and let the types enter with probabilities $q_C$, $q_W$. By Bayes' rule
$$\mu(x_1) = \frac{p\,q_C}{p\,q_C + (1-p)\,q_W}.$$
$(EO, A)$ is a PBE with $\mu(x_1) = 1$: the incumbent knows that an entrant is competitive and accepts. $(OO, F)$ is not: Bayes' rule cannot be applied ($q_C = q_W = 0$), but for *any* belief Fight is not a best response, so requirement 4 fails. Compare with off-path choices in SPE.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Fully reached information sets"}
If a profile $s^*$ (possibly mixed) reaches every information set with positive probability, then $s^*$ together with the beliefs derived by Bayes' rule is a PBE if and only if $s^*$ is a BNE.
:::

## Refinements: sequential equilibrium

The definition above is sometimes called **weak PBE** because requirement 3 lets off-path beliefs be anything, even inconsistent with the structure of the game.

::: {.example name="An odd PBE"}
Nature picks node $x_1$ or $x_2$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each; player 1 *does not observe it* and chooses U (payoff $1$ for player 1, game over) or D. After D, player 2 (who doesn't observe Nature either) chooses L, giving $(2, 2)$ at both nodes, or R, giving $(0, 3)$ at $x_1$ and $(0, 0)$ at $x_2$. If player 1 plays D, Bayes' rule gives $\mu(x_1) = \tfrac12$; R is then worth $1.5 < 2$, so L, and D is optimal for player 1: the PBE $(D, L)$. But if player 1 never plays D, any belief is admissible; with $\mu(x_1) > \tfrac23$, R is optimal, and against R player 1 prefers U. So $(U, R)$ is also a (weak) PBE — supported by the belief that player 1's non-move somehow reveals Nature's choice, which player 1 does not even observe.
:::

::: {.definition name="Sequential equilibrium (Kreps–Wilson, 1982)"}
An assessment $(s^*, \mu)$ is **consistent** if it is the limit of a sequence $(s^k, \mu^k)$ of *fully mixed* strategy profiles $s^k$ and the beliefs $\mu^k$ derived from them by Bayes' rule. A **sequential equilibrium** is a consistent, sequentially rational assessment.
:::

In the odd example, every fully mixed profile gives $\mu^k(x_1) = \tfrac12$, so consistency forces $\mu(x_1) = \tfrac12$ and eliminates $(U, R)$. Sequential equilibrium can still be insufficient and is much harder to check; in practice the solution concept is chosen case by case — these distinctions are also useful to argue about how "rational" human behaviour is.

## Signaling games

When the typed player moves first, the move itself may reveal information. If instead only the second mover has types, the first can only guess the reaction from the prior: a **screening** game (think of a quiz show with a prize behind a screen), where SPE is enough.

::: {.definition name="Signaling game"}
A two-player dynamic Bayesian game in which:

1. Nature chooses the type of player 1 (the **sender**);
2. player 1 observes the type and chooses an action (the **signal**), with at least as many actions as types;
3. player 2 (the **receiver**) observes the signal, not the type, updates beliefs, and acts;
4. player 2 cares about player 1's type (common values).
:::

::: {.definition name="Separating, pooling and hybrid equilibria"}
In a **separating** equilibrium different types send different signals, so the signal reveals the type. In a **pooling** equilibrium all types send the same signal, which carries no information. **Hybrid** (semi-separating, partially pooling) equilibria have some types randomising so that some signals are partially informative.
:::

Separating equilibria have easy beliefs (each signal points to one type); in pooling equilibria the on-path signal leaves the prior unchanged, while the unused signal is off the path and its beliefs must be chosen to support the equilibrium.

### A recipe for pure PBE in signaling games

With two sender types and two messages, there are only four sender strategies to try:

1. **Fix a candidate**: pooling (both types send the same message) or separating (they send different ones).
2. **Beliefs on the path** follow from Bayes' rule: after a separating message the type is known; after a pooling message the belief is the prior.
3. **Beliefs off the path** (after a message nobody sends) are free in a PBE. Find the range of beliefs that makes the receiver's prescribed response optimal.
4. **Receiver**: check that the response to each message is a best reply to the belief.
5. **Sender**: check that no type gains by switching message, given the receiver's responses.

A pooling candidate survives if *some* off-path belief deters deviation. A separating candidate needs no off-path beliefs, but each type must prefer its own message.

**Revealing (verifiable) actions.** Some messages cannot lie: a medical test, a public audit, a costly campaign that exposes the product. After such an action the receiver's information set is a singleton and backward induction applies directly. Only the non-revealing message needs a belief. The game then shrinks: the receiver has one real decision, and the sender asks whether revealing is worth its cost for each type. Typically the "good" types reveal and the "bad" types stay silent, so silence itself becomes informative (*unraveling*).

## Worked example: a coffee for Brian

Brian is invited for coffee by his colleague Zoe. His partner Ann is **J**ealous with probability $0.8$ or **E**asygoing with probability $0.2$. Ann may stay **S**ilent or **T**rash Zoe; Brian observes the signal and **C**omes to the coffee or **D**eclines.

*Figure: the signaling game in "butterfly" form; payoffs are (Ann, Brian). Brian's information sets join the nodes after the same signal.*

Jealous Ann is deeply hurt if Brian goes; easygoing Ann is only mildly annoyed; trashing Zoe costs Ann $2$; Brian enjoys the coffee ($2$) only if Ann is easygoing and suffers ($-3$) if she is jealous.

**Step 1: the normal form.** Ann's strategy lists the signal of each type ($ST$: silent if jealous, trash if easygoing); Brian's lists the reply to each signal ($CD$: come after S, decline after T). Careful: Ann's pair is indexed by *types*, Brian's by *signals*, so filling e.g. $(TS, CD)$ requires matching Brian's reply to the signal each type sends.

| Ann \\ Brian | CC | CD | DC | DD |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **SS** | −5, −2 | −5, −2 | 3, 0 | 3, 0 |
| **ST** | −5, −2 | −4.6, −2.4 | 2.2, 0.4 | 2.6, 0 |
| **TS** | −5, −2 | 0.6, 0.4 | −4.2, −2.4 | 1.4, 0 |
| **TT** | −5, −2 | 1, 0 | −5, −2 | 1, 0 |

There are five Nash equilibria: three pure, $(SS, DD)$, $(SS, DC)$, $(TT, CD)$, and two mixed, $(TT, \tfrac12 CD + \tfrac12 DD)$ and $(\tfrac16 SS + \tfrac56 TS,\ \tfrac29 CD + \tfrac79 DD)$.

**Step 2: beliefs.** Let $\mu_S$ ($\mu_T$) be Brian's belief that Ann is jealous after S (after T). Coming is worth $-3\mu + 2(1-\mu)$ to Brian, declining $0$: he comes iff $\mu \leq 0.4$.

- **No separating PBE.** If Ann played $ST$, beliefs would be $\mu_S = 1$, $\mu_T = 0$ and Brian would reply $DC$; but against $DC$ Ann prefers $SS$. Similarly $TS$ induces $CD$, against which Ann prefers $TT$.
- **PBE 1: $(SS, DD)$** with $\mu_S = 0.8$ (the prior, since both types are silent) and off-path $\mu_T \geq 0.4$ (so that declining after T is optimal). Ann has no reason to trash Zoe since Brian declines anyway.
- **PBE 2: $(SS, DC)$** with $\mu_S = 0.8$, $\mu_T \leq 0.4$: Brian still declines in practice, but would come if Ann trashed Zoe, because he would read it as a sign that she is easygoing.
- **PBE 3: $(TT, CD)$** with $\mu_T = 0.8$ and off-path $\mu_S \leq 0.4$.
- **PBE 4:** Ann plays $TT$; Brian declines after T ($\mu_T = 0.8$); off the path, Brian believes $\mu_S = 0.4$ — which makes him indifferent — and comes with probability $q$ after S. Ann must not want to deviate to S: the easygoing type requires $-q + 3(1-q) \leq 1$, i.e. $q \geq \tfrac12$. So this is a continuum of PBE (with $q = 1$ recovering PBE 3); note that $(TT, DD)$ itself is *not* an equilibrium.
- **PBE 5 (semi-separating):** easygoing Ann is always silent; jealous Ann is silent with probability $\tfrac16$. T is sent only by the jealous type, so $\mu_T = 1$ and Brian declines. After S, Bayes' rule gives
$$\mu_S = \frac{0.8 \cdot \tfrac16}{0.8 \cdot \tfrac16 + 0.2} = 0.4,$$
exactly the value that makes Brian indifferent, so he can mix. He comes with probability $\tfrac29$ after S, which makes jealous Ann indifferent between S ($-6 \cdot \tfrac29 + 3 \cdot \tfrac79 = 1$) and T ($1$).

::: {.callout-tip}
The recipe for signaling games: (1) write the normal form, minding the type/signal indexing; (2) find all NE; (3) for each, compute on-path beliefs by Bayes' rule and find off-path beliefs that make the receiver's prescribed replies optimal — if none exist, the NE is not a PBE.
:::

## Education as a signal and the intuitive criterion

Spence's (1973) job-market model shows how a costly action can transmit information even when it is useless in itself. A worker is of High productivity ($2$) with probability $p$ or Low ($1$). Before being hired, the worker chooses an education level $e \geq 0$, which costs $e$ to a Low type and $e/2$ to a High type. Competitive employers pay the expected productivity given their belief after observing $e$.

- **Separating PBE.** High chooses $e^*$ and Low chooses $0$; the wage is $2$ after $e^*$ and $1$ otherwise. Low must not mimic, $1 \geq 2 - e^*$; High must prefer to signal, $2 - e^*/2 \geq 1$. Any $e^* \in [1, 2]$ works, supported by the belief "anyone with $e < e^*$ is Low".
- **Pooling PBE.** Both choose $e = 0$ and receive $\bar w = 1 + p$, with pessimistic beliefs off the path.

Perfect Bayesian equilibrium allows any belief after an unexpected action, hence the multiplicity. The **intuitive criterion** (Cho and Kreps, 1987) asks: *could this deviation possibly benefit this type?* If a deviation can only benefit one type, the responder should believe it comes from that type.

- In the separating family, an education level $e \in (1, e^*)$ would never pay for a Low worker (even with wage $2$, $2 - e < 1$), but would pay for a High one. So the beliefs "$e < e^*$ means Low" are unreasonable, and only the **least-cost separating** equilibrium $e^* = 1$ survives (the Riley outcome).
- The pooling equilibrium always fails the criterion (for $p < 1$). Any $e \in (1 - p,\ 2(1 - p))$ cannot benefit Low even at wage $2$ ($2 - e < 1 + p$), but if read as High it gives High $2 - e/2 > 1 + p$.

The lessons generalise: signals work because they are **costlier for the types you want to separate from** (single crossing), and equilibrium refinements select the cheapest credible signal. In networks the same logic underlies proof-of-work, costly authentication and reputation deposits.

## Reputation building

In a finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma the unique SPE is to defect always. A little uncertainty about types changes this dramatically (Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts and Wilson, 1982).

Stage payoffs are $(C,C) = (1,1)$, $(C,D) = (-1,2)$, $(D,C) = (2,-1)$, $(D,D) = (0,0)$. Player B is strategic. Player A is a **grim-trigger** type with probability $p$ (cooperates until B defects, then defects forever) or strategic with probability $1 - p$.

- **Two rounds.** In round 2 strategic players defect. In round 1 strategic A defects, and B, cooperating, gets $p(1) + (1-p)(-1)$ in round 1 plus $2p$ in round 2 (exploiting the grim-trigger type, who still cooperates): total $4p - 1$. Defecting gives $2p$ (round 1) and $0$ after. B cooperates in round 1 iff $p > \tfrac12$.
- **Three rounds.** If $p > \tfrac12$, the unique PBE has *both* B and the strategic A cooperating in round 1. Strategic A pools with the grim-trigger type: by cooperating he keeps B's belief at $p > \tfrac12$, so B keeps cooperating in round 2, and A earns $1 + 2 + 0$ instead of $2 + 0 + 0$ from revealing himself. B, in turn, cooperates in round 1 even if he expected the strategic A to defect, since $5p - 1 > 2p$ for $p > \tfrac13$.

Strategic players **build a reputation**: they defect at the end, but to an outside observer they cooperate. In general, in a $T$-stage game the number of rounds in which a strategic player defects is bounded by a constant depending on $p$ but not on $T$.

**The tough negotiator.** In an ultimatum game the proposer offers a **H**igh share (payoffs $(2,1)$ if accepted) or a **M**oderate one ($(1,2)$); refusal gives $(0,0)$. The unique SPE is $(H, \text{always accept})$. Now let the responder be a "jerk" with probability $p$, who finds H worse than nothing (payoff $-1$). In one round the proposer offers H iff $2(1-p) > 1$, i.e. $p < \tfrac12$. With a second round (after a refusal the proposer offers again, payoffs discounted by $\delta$) the game becomes intractable in normal form — the responder has $2^{12}$ strategies — but sequential rationality reduces it. With $\delta = 0.9$ and $p = 0.1$ the unique PBE is semi-separating: H is always offered first; the jerk always refuses; a normal responder refuses with probability $\tfrac19$ (so that after a refusal $\mu_H = \Pr[\text{jerk} \mid \text{refusal}] = \tfrac12$, making the proposer indifferent between H, worth $2\delta(1-\mu_H)$, and M, worth $\delta$); in round 2 the proposer repeats H with probability $\tfrac89$ (making the normal responder indifferent: $\delta[\tfrac89 \cdot 1 + \tfrac19 \cdot 2] = 1$). Even an unlikely "tough" type gives the responder bargaining power: a normal responder sometimes mimics it.

## Cheap talk

In **cheap talk** (Crawford and Sobel, 1982), Nature reveals a state $t$ to player 1 only; player 1 sends a message that costs nothing and has no direct effect on payoffs; player 2 then acts. The question is how much information costless messages can transmit when interests are not aligned.

::: {.example name="Where to live"}
Player 2 found a job in city A and chooses where to live, $a \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$ (1 = city A, 5 = city B where player 1 lives). The best location depends on traffic conditions $t \in \{1, 3, 5\}$, equally likely and known only to player 1. Player 2's payoff is $5 - (t - a)^2$; player 1 is biased towards having the friend close: $5 - (t + b - a)^2$ with $b = 1.1$.

1. **No truthful PBE.** If messages were believed, the type $t = 3$ would claim "5": the friend would choose $a = 5$, giving player 1 $5 - 0.81 = 4.19$ instead of $5 - 1.21 = 3.79$.
2. **A babbling PBE** always exists: messages are uninformative and player 2 chooses $a = 3$ from the prior.
3. **A partially informative PBE:** player 1 reports $t = 1$ truthfully but pools $t \in \{3, 5\}$ into "high". Player 2 then chooses $a = 1$ or $a = 4$. Type 1 prefers the truth ($3.79$ vs. $1.39$); types 3 and 5 prefer "high".
:::

With a continuous state $t \in [0, 1]$ and quadratic losses, there is never a truthful equilibrium, there is always a babbling one, and a **two-message (threshold) equilibrium** exists iff the bias is small enough ($b < \tfrac14$ for uniform $t$). Applications include legislative committees and expert advisers: with the decision maker's payoff $-(t - a)^2$, $t \sim \mathcal U[-w, w]$, and the adviser's $-(t + b - a)^2$, informative advice (under open or closed rules) is possible only if $b \leq w$.

::: {.callout-note}
The "negative" results of cheap talk justify a common engineering practice: protocols exchange **coarse** information — congested / not congested, low / high battery — rather than exact values. With slightly misaligned incentives, more detail would not be credible anyway.
:::

## Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 6.

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.6)** Mark buys trousers Immediately at €50 or Waits, in which case Nadia Keeps the price or Lowers it to €30 at the end of the week. Mark values them at €100. Late transactions are discounted by $\delta = 0.8$. Nadia's cost is €20 with probability $0.75$ (and lowering the price then also brings €20 of goodwill, not discounted) or €35. Find the BNE and the SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Nadia's strategies are (cost 20, cost 35). Waiting gives Mark $40$ (K) or $56$ (L). It gives Nadia $24$ (K) or $28$ (L) at cost 20, and $12$ (K) or $-4$ (L) at cost 35. Buying immediately gives Mark $50$ and Nadia $30$ or $15$. Normal form, Mark W against KK, KL, LK, LL: $(40, 21)$, $(44, 17)$, $(52, 24)$, $(56, 20)$; Mark I: $(50, 26.25)$. BNE: $(I, KK)$, $(I, KL)$, $(W, LK)$. Sequential rationality requires L at cost 20 and K at cost 35, so Nadia plays LK. Then Mark waits ($52 > 50$). The unique SPE is $(W, LK)$. (The printed payoffs contain small arithmetic slips; the equilibria are unchanged.)
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.7)** Janine brings food from Home (payoff $2$; stand $0$) or Eats at the stand. The worker there is Alfred ($0.8$) or Brianna ($0.2$) and Gives a sandwich (Janine $5$, worker $1$) or Denies it (Janine $0$, worker $0$; but Brianna, who dislikes Janine, gets $5$). Find the BNE and the SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
The stand's strategies are (Alfred, Brianna). E against GG, GD, DG, DD gives $(5, 1)$, $(4, 1.8)$, $(1, 0.2)$, $(0, 1)$; H gives $(2, 0)$. BNE: $(E, GD)$, $(H, DG)$, $(H, DD)$. Sequential rationality: Alfred gives, Brianna denies, so GD, and Janine eats ($4 > 2$). SPE $(E, GD)$; the other two rest on Alfred threatening to deny. (The printed expected payoffs of the stand are slightly off: $1.8$, not $2.8$.)
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.8)** Riccardo is infected with probability $0.2$. At the border he takes a Swab (revealing his status; cost $10$) or Talks. The officer Admits or Denies. Riccardo gets $20$ if admitted, $0$ otherwise. The officer gets $5$ for admitting a healthy traveller, $-50$ for an infected one, $0$ for denying. Find the PBE and classify it.
:::

::: {.solution}
After a swab the officer knows the type: he admits the healthy (Riccardo $10$) and denies the infected ($-10$). After T, let $\mu$ be the officer's belief that Riccardo is infected; admitting pays $5 - 55\mu$, so he admits iff $\mu \leq 1/11$. The infected type never swabs ($-10 < 0$). If the healthy type talked too (pooling), $\mu = 0.2 > 1/11$ and the officer would deny, so the healthy type would rather swab ($10 > 0$). PBE: infected Talks, healthy Swabs, the officer believes $\mu(T) = 1$ and Denies after T, and admits after a negative swab. It is **separating**: the costly swab is the signal only the healthy type is willing to send.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.9)** John and Mary exchange gifts at Christmas and on Valentine's Day (two rounds, $\delta = 1$): a gift costs the giver $5$ and is worth $12$ to the receiver. Mary is strategic. With probability $p$ John is a tit-for-tat type (gives at Christmas, then copies Mary); otherwise he is strategic. (a) Payoffs if $p = 0$? (b) Probability that John does not give at Christmas? (c) When does Mary give at Christmas?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) A twice-repeated Prisoner's Dilemma: $(N, N)$ twice, payoffs $0$. (b) Only the strategic John withholds at Christmas: probability $1 - p$. (c) Mary never gives on Valentine's Day (the last round). If she withholds at Christmas she gets $12p$ (the tit-for-tat John's Christmas gift). If she gives, she gets $p(12 - 5 + 12) - 5(1 - p) = 24p - 5$: tit-for-tat John rewards her on Valentine's Day. She gives iff $24p - 5 > 12p$, i.e. $p > 5/12$. A modest probability of a reciprocating type makes even a selfish player cooperate early: the seed of the reputation results of Kreps–Milgrom–Roberts–Wilson.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.10)** A clerk Stays put (bandit $100$, clerk $0$) or Punches a bandit whose gun is fake with probability $p$. After a punch the bandit Reacts or Flees. Fake gun and react: both $-10$. Real gun and react: clerk $-100$, bandit $80$. Flee: clerk $10$, bandit $0$. Find the BNE as a function of $p$ and the SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
The bandit's strategies are (fake, real). P against RR, RF, FR, FF: clerk $90p - 100$, $10 - 20p$, $110p - 100$, $10$. S gives $0$ against everything. Sequential rationality: flee with a fake gun ($0 > -10$) and react with a real one ($80 > 0$), i.e. FR. The clerk punches iff $110p - 100 \geq 0$, i.e. $p \geq 10/11$. BNE: $(S, RR)$ for all $p$; $(S, RF)$ for $p \geq 1/2$; $(S, FR)$ for $p \leq 10/11$; $(P, FR)$ for $p \geq 10/11$. Only those with FR are SPE. The clerk should play the hero only if he is almost sure the gun is fake.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.12)** A start-up's gadget is Effective or Useless (probability $\tfrac12$ each). It runs a Campaign (cost $20$, reveals the type) or keeps a Low profile. The incumbent Fights or Yields. Yield: $50$ each. Fight: $(80, 0)$ if Effective, $(0, 80)$ if Useless. Find the PBE.
:::

::: {.solution}
After a campaign the incumbent yields to an effective gadget ($50 > 0$) and fights a useless one ($80 > 50$), so C yields $30$ (effective) or $-20$ (useless). After L, with belief $\mu = \Pr(\text{effective})$, fighting pays $80(1 - \mu)$ against $50$ for yielding. Normal form (start-up strategies (effective, useless), incumbent's answer to L): CC $(5, 65)$ for both answers; CL $(15, 65)$ / $(40, 50)$; LC $(30, 40)$ / $(15, 65)$; LL $(40, 40)$ / $(50, 50)$. LL is strictly dominant. With $\mu = 1/2$ the incumbent yields ($50 > 40$). **Pooling** PBE: both types keep a low profile, the incumbent believes the prior after L and yields. The campaign is too expensive to be worth revealing even good news.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 6.16)** A student's green pass is valid ($0.9$) or fake ($0.1$). The student Displays or Hides it; the professor Admits or sends Online, unable to tell fakes apart. Valid: admitted $(10, 10)$; displayed but sent online: professor $-50$; otherwise $0$. Fake: admitted after display $(-100, -100)$; admitted without display $(-50, -200)$; displayed and sent online: student $-200$; otherwise $0$. Find the pure PBE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Student strategies (valid, fake), professor strategies (after D, after H). The pure NE are $(DH, AO)$ and $(HH, OO)$. **Separating** $(DH, AO)$: valid passes are displayed, fakes hidden. Beliefs $\mu(\text{valid} \mid D) = 1$ and $\mu(\text{valid} \mid H) = 0$; admitting after D and sending online after H are optimal. **Pooling** $(HH, OO)$: nobody displays, and $\mu(\text{valid} \mid H) = 0.9$ makes O optimal ($0 > 0.9\cdot10 - 0.1\cdot200 = -11$). D is off the path, so its belief is free. Admitting after D pays $110\mu - 100$ and online pays $-50\mu$, so O after D is optimal iff $\mu(\text{valid} \mid D) \leq 5/8$. The pooling PBE needs pessimistic off-path beliefs. By the intuitive criterion, displaying could only benefit the valid type, so the pooling equilibrium is not reasonable.
:::

From the course material:

::: {.exercise}
A young cowboy Y, insulted by an outlaw O, can let it slide ($(-20, 0)$) or challenge O. O can apologise ($(10, -10)$) or duel. If O is a sharpshooter (prior $p$) the duel gives $(-100, 20)$; otherwise each wins with probability $\tfrac12$, the winner getting $20$ and the loser $-100$. Find the PBE for $p = 0.2$.
:::

::: {.solution}
For a non-sharpshooter the duel is worth $\tfrac12 \cdot 20 + \tfrac12(-100) = -40$ to both players. Sequential rationality: a sharpshooter duels ($20 > -10$); a fake apologises ($-10 > -40$). Then challenging gives Y $p(-100) + (1-p)(10) = 0.2(-100) + 0.8 \cdot 10 = -12 > -20$, so Y challenges. The unique PBE (indeed the unique SPE of the type-agent tree) is: Y challenges; a sharpshooter O duels, a fake O apologises. Y would let it slide only if $-100p + 10(1-p) < -20$, i.e. $p > 3/11 \approx 0.27$. This is a screening game — the uninformed player moves first and O knows his own type — so beliefs are trivial and SPE suffices; in normal form there is also a NE in which Y lets it slide and O threatens to duel whatever his type, but it is not sequentially rational.
:::

::: {.exercise}
In the coffee game, check that $(TT, DD)$ is not a Nash equilibrium and explain in words why PBE 4 needs Brian to come after S with probability at least $\tfrac12$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Against $DD$, Ann gets $3$ from $SS$ and $1$ from $TT$: she deviates to silence. In PBE 4 Brian's reply to the off-path signal S must deter *both* types from switching to S. The jealous type gets $-6q + 3(1-q) \leq 1$ iff $q \geq \tfrac29$; the easygoing type gets $-q + 3(1-q) \leq 1$ iff $q \geq \tfrac12$. The binding constraint is the easygoing type's, hence $q \geq \tfrac12$.
:::
