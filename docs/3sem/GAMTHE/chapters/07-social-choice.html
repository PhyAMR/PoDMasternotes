7  Social choice and voting

Chapter 1 showed that a single rational decision maker has a utility function. Can a group of rational individuals be summarised by a rational collective preference? The question matters well beyond elections: scheduling packets under several criteria, choosing among solutions of a multi-objective optimisation, or allocating resources by competing priorities are all “votes”. The answer, in the form of Arrow’s impossibility theorem, is essentially negative, and the paradoxes of voting systems show why.

7.1 Constitutions

Let \(\mathcal R(A)\) be the set of rational preferences on a set of alternatives \(A\), and let \(n\) individuals have preferences \((\succeq_1, \dots, \succeq_n)\), a profile.

A constitution (social welfare function) is a map \(f : \mathcal R(A)^n \to \mathcal R(A)\) turning every profile into a social preference \(\succeq = f(\succeq_1, \dots, \succeq_n)\). For \(Y \subseteq A\), \(\succeq|_Y = \succeq \cap (Y \times Y)\) denotes the restriction to \(Y\).

  • Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA): if two profiles agree on the pair \(\{a, b\}\) for every individual, the social preferences agree on \(\{a, b\}\). Adding or removing other alternatives does not change how society ranks \(a\) against \(b\).
  • Pareto efficiency: if \(a \succeq_i b\) for every \(i\), then \(a \succeq b\).
  • Dictatorship: there is an individual \(i\) such that \(a \succeq_i b\) implies \(a \succeq b\) for every profile.
  • Monotonicity: if one individual raises an alternative in their ranking, society does not rank it lower.
  • Non-imposition: every rational preference is the output of some profile (\(f\) is surjective).

With at least three alternatives, no constitution is simultaneously non-dictatorial, monotonic, IIA and non-imposed. Equivalently (1963 version): every Pareto-efficient constitution satisfying IIA is a dictatorship.

7.2 Majority rule and Condorcet cycles

With two candidates, majority rule is unproblematic. With three it can fail spectacularly:

voter 1 2 3
best A C B
B A C
worst C B A

Pairwise, \(A\) beats \(B\) (voters 1, 2), \(B\) beats \(C\) (voters 1, 3), and \(C\) beats \(A\) (voters 2, 3). Majority preference is not transitive.

A candidate beating every other in pairwise majority contests is a Condorcet winner; one beaten by every other is a Condorcet loser. If no Condorcet winner exists, majority preference contains a Condorcet cycle.

Three-candidate profiles arise from two-candidate ones by inserting the third candidate somewhere in each voter’s ranking; depending on where it goes, the newcomer can become a Condorcet winner, a Condorcet loser, or close a cycle. Cycles cannot occur with two alternatives, but with random preferences their probability grows with the number of candidates:

candidates \ voters 3 5 7 9 \(\infty\)
3 5.6% 6.9% 7.5% 7.8% 8.8%
5 16.0% 20.0% 21.5% 23.0% 25.1%
7 23.9% 29.9% 30.5% 34.2% 36.9%
\(\infty\) 100% 100% 100% 100% 100%

Real examples abound: fiscal policies ranked by liberals, anti-deficit voters and conservatives over combinations of taxes and spending; or traffic classes (voice over IP, video streaming, best-effort data) ranked by delay, loss and fairness criteria, each criterion being a “voter”.

7.3 Electoral systems and their paradoxes

Plurality voting: the candidate with the most first places wins. With \(9\) voters

voters 4 3 2
best A B C
B C B
worst C A A

\(A\) wins with \(4\) first places, yet a majority prefers \(B\) to \(A\) and \(C\) to \(A\), and \(B\) is the Condorcet winner.

Two-round run-off: the top two meet in a ballot. With

voters 4 3 2
best A B C
C C B
worst B A A

\(A\) and \(B\) reach the ballot and \(B\) wins \(5\)–\(4\), while the Condorcet winner \(C\) does not even make it to the second round. Plurality and run-off favour polarised candidates over compromise ones.

Borda count: with \(M\) candidates each voter gives \(M-1\) points to the first, \(M-2\) to the second, …, \(0\) to the last. With

voters 5 3 1
best A B C
B C B
worst C A A

\(A\) gets \(10\), \(B\) \(12\), \(C\) \(5\): \(B\) wins although \(A\) is the Condorcet winner. Borda also violates IIA dramatically. With four candidates

voters 5 2 2
best D A A
C D B
B B D
worst A C C

the ranking is \(D\) (21), \(A\) (12), \(B\) (11), \(C\) (10). If \(D\) withdraws, the scores become \(C\) 10, \(B\) 9, \(A\) 8: the order of the remaining three is entirely reversed.

Approval voting: each voter approves \(N\) candidates, one point each (\(N = 1\) is plurality). With

voters 3 3 2 1
best A D B A
C B D B
D C C C
worst B A A D

top-2 approval elects \(B\) (A 4, B 6, C 3, D 5), top-3 approval elects \(C\) (A 4, B 6, C 9, D 8), plurality elects \(A\), and Borda elects \(D\) (16 vs. 14, 12, 12). Four systems, four winners.

Instant run-off: voters rank all candidates; the candidate with the fewest first places is repeatedly eliminated and their votes transferred. With

voters 6 5 4 2
best A C B B
B A C A
worst C B A C

\(C\) is eliminated first and \(A\) wins with \(11\) votes. If the last two voters raise \(A\) to first place, \(B\) is eliminated first, its four votes go to \(C\), and \(C\) beats \(A\) \(9\)–\(8\). \(A\) loses because of increased support — a violation of monotonicity.

7.4 Agenda setting and manipulation

Sequential pairwise votes look fair but are not under a Condorcet cycle. With \(A > B > C > A\), “first \(A\) vs. \(B\), then the winner vs. \(C\)” elects \(C\); “first \(B\) vs. \(C\), then the winner vs. \(A\)” elects \(A\). Whoever sets the agenda chooses the winner. The power is not unlimited: a Condorcet loser \(D\) can never win, and a Condorcet winner always does.

Voters can also misreport. With

voters 4 3 2
best A B C
D A B
C D D
worst B C A

\(A\) wins under plurality. Voters 8–9, for whom \(A\) is the worst, can report \(B\) first and make \(B\) win. Voters 1–4 can pre-empt this by reporting \(C\), who then wins with only two sincere supporters; \(B\)’s supporters can in turn switch to \(A\). The outcome depends on who cheats first.

A social choice rule is strategy-proof (non-manipulable) if for every profile no individual \(j\) obtains an outcome they strictly prefer by reporting \(\succeq_j'\) instead of their true \(\succeq_j\).

With at least three alternatives, every strategy-proof social choice rule whose range includes every alternative is a dictatorship.

7.5 Ways out

No universally good system exists, but there are escape routes:

  • Weaken the axioms (e.g. drop IIA, as Borda does).
  • Restrict the domain of admissible profiles. Majority rule, \(a \succeq b \iff |\{i : a \succeq_i b\}| \geq |\{i : b \succeq_i a\}|\), is Pareto efficient, IIA and non-dictatorial, but in general not transitive because of Condorcet cycles — so it is not a constitution. On domains where cycles cannot occur it becomes one: Black (1948) showed this for single-peaked preferences (alternatives on a line, each voter preferring options closer to their ideal point), Sen (1966) gave more general conditions.
  • Change the ballot: approval voting with free choice of \(N\), or voting “for” and “against”.

For engineering, the lesson is that aggregating several performance criteria into a single ranking is never neutral. Every scalarisation (weighted sums are Borda-like) embodies choices that can reverse rankings when an irrelevant option is added or removed.

7.6 Voting as a game

An election can itself be modelled as a static game: voters are players, ballots are strategies, and the rule maps profiles to outcomes. Three observations follow.

  • Two candidates: voting sincerely is weakly dominant, since your ballot matters only when it is pivotal, and then you want your favourite to win. The median voter result of Chapter 4 describes the candidates’ side of this game.
  • Three or more candidates: sincere voting is generally not an equilibrium. Under plurality, supporters of a hopeless candidate prefer to vote for the lesser evil among the front-runners, which is why plurality systems tend to two parties (Duverger’s law). Run-off systems invite subtler manipulation: supporting a weak opponent in the first round to meet them in the second.
  • Equilibrium is too permissive: with many voters no single ballot is pivotal, so every profile in which a candidate wins by two or more votes is a Nash equilibrium, including everyone voting for their least favourite. One must first delete weakly dominated ballots, or study coalitions of like-minded voters, as in the exercises.

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem guarantees that some profile always rewards misreporting. The useful question for a given rule is therefore who can manipulate, and whether the others can counter it. A Condorcet winner, when it exists, is robust: any coalition preferring another candidate is a minority in the head-to-head comparison.

7.7 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 4.

(BM 4.8) Thirteen students elect a class representative by plurality among A, B, C. Preferences: students 1–5: \(A \succ B \succ C\); 6–9: \(B \succ C \succ A\); 10–12: \(C \succ B \succ A\); 13: \(C \succ A \succ B\). (a) Who wins with sincere voting? (b) How can students 10–12 manipulate the result? (c) Can A’s supporters counter?

Solution.

  1. A \(5\), B \(4\), C \(4\): A wins. (b) Students 10–12 prefer B to A: voting B gives B \(7\) votes and B wins. (c) A’s supporters could only block B by voting C, which would at best tie and which they dislike more than B. They have no counter. B is the Condorcet winner (B beats A \(7\)–\(6\) and C \(9\)–\(4\)), so the manipulation actually restores the majority’s choice.

(BM 4.9) A two-round run-off among A (42%, \(B \succ C \succ D\) after A), B (11%, \(A \succ C \succ D\)), C (27%, \(B \succ D \succ A\)), D (20%, \(C \succ B \succ A\)). (a) Sincere outcome? (b) Best strategic vote for D’s supporters? (c) Who wins then, and can A’s supporters respond?

Solution.

  1. A and C reach the ballot; B’s voters prefer A, so A wins \(53\)–\(47\). (b) D’s supporters prefer B to A: voting B in round one sends A and B to the ballot (B \(31\%\), C \(27\%\)). (c) B wins the ballot with \(58\%\) (everyone but A’s supporters): B is the Condorcet winner despite the fewest first preferences. A’s supporters cannot beat B head-to-head, so they must keep B out of the ballot. If \(7\) of their \(42\) points vote C in round one (A \(35\), C \(34\), B \(31\)), A meets C again and wins \(53\)–\(47\). Manipulation invites counter-manipulation, and the outcome depends on who coordinates better.

From the course material:

Verify that in the instant run-off example the two voters who switch to \(A > B > C\) cause \(A\) to lose, and compute the Condorcet winner (if any) in both profiles.

Solution. Original: first places \(A\) 6, \(C\) 5, \(B\) 6 → \(C\) out, its 5 votes go to \(A\) → \(A\) 11. Pairwise: \(A\) vs \(B\): \(6 + 5 = 11\) prefer \(A\); \(A\) vs \(C\): \(6 + 2 = 8\) prefer \(A\) vs \(9\) prefer \(C\), so \(C\) beats \(A\); \(B\) vs \(C\): \(6+4+2=12\) prefer \(B\). \(B > C > A\) and \(A > B\): a cycle, no Condorcet winner. Modified profile: first places \(A\) 8, \(C\) 5, \(B\) 4 → \(B\) out, its 4 votes go to \(C\) → \(C\) 9 beats \(A\) 8. Pairwise: \(A > B\) (13–4), \(C > A\) (9–8), \(B > C\) (12–5): again a cycle.

Show that majority rule over three alternatives is transitive whenever all voters’ preferences are single-peaked on the order \(A < B < C\).

Solution. Single-peakedness on \(A<B<C\) forbids \(B\) being ranked last, leaving the orders \(ABC\), \(BAC\), \(BCA\), \(CBA\). A cycle \(A > B > C > A\) would need a strict majority with \(A\) over \(B\) (only \(ABC\) voters) and a strict majority with \(C\) over \(A\) (only \(BCA\), \(CBA\) voters): two disjoint groups, each larger than \(n/2\) — impossible. The reverse cycle \(A > C > B > A\) needs a majority with \(C\) over \(B\) (only \(CBA\)) and one with \(A\) over \(C\) (\(ABC\), \(BAC\)), again disjoint. Hence no cycle exists and majority rule is transitive; in fact the median voter’s ranking decides every pairwise contest.

::: {.solution}
Single-peakedness on $A<B<C$ forbids $B$ being ranked last, leaving the orders $ABC$, $BAC$, $BCA$, $CBA$. A cycle $A > B > C > A$ would need a strict majority with $A$ over $B$ (only $ABC$ voters) *and* a strict majority with $C$ over $A$ (only $BCA$, $CBA$ voters): two disjoint groups, each larger than $n/2$ — impossible. The reverse cycle $A > C > B > A$ needs a majority with $C$ over $B$ (only $CBA$) and one with $A$ over $C$ ($ABC$, $BAC$), again disjoint. Hence no cycle exists and majority rule is transitive; in fact the median voter's ranking decides every pairwise contest.
:::
