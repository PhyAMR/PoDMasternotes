10  Multistage and repeated games

Many interactions consist of several rounds, each producing a partial payoff: tournaments, rounds of cards, partial exams, network sessions. This chapter studies multistage games (a sequence of static games played by the same players) and their most important special case, repeated games (the same stage game over and over). The central message is that the future can discipline the present: threats of punishment and promises of reward make cooperative behaviour — impossible in a one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma — part of a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

10.1 Why exponential discounting

Aggregating payoffs across periods requires a discounting rule. A single-person example shows why it must be exponential.

A player has a budget \(K = 1\) to spend over three periods with per-period utility \(u(x) = \log(1+x)\) and total value \(v = u(x_1) + \delta u(x_2) + \delta^2 u(x_3)\). The first-order conditions \(\frac{1}{1+x_1} = \frac{\delta}{1+x_2} = \frac{\delta^2}{1+x_3}\) give \[x_1 = \frac{3 - \delta - \delta^2}{1 + \delta + \delta^2}, \quad x_2 = \frac{-1 + 3\delta - \delta^2}{1 + \delta + \delta^2}, \quad x_3 = \frac{-1 - \delta + 3\delta^2}{1 + \delta + \delta^2},\] an equal split for \(\delta = 1\) and \((0.6393, 0.3115, 0.0492)\) for \(\delta = 0.8\) (an interior solution requires \(3\delta^2 - \delta - 1 \geq 0\), i.e. \(\delta \gtrsim 0.77\)). At period 2, with \(1 - x_1\) left, the player re-optimises \(u(x_2) + \delta u(x_3)\) and finds \(x_2 = (2 - x_1 - \delta)/(1 + \delta)\): for \(x_1 = 0.6393\) this is again \(0.3115\). Exponential discounting is time consistent — the plan made today is still optimal tomorrow.

Now try quasi-hyperbolic discounting, \(v = u(x_1) + \delta u(x_2) + \delta u(x_3)\) (the future discounted by a single factor). The plan is \(x_1 = (3 - 2\delta)/(1 + 2\delta)\), \(x_2 = x_3 = (2\delta - 1)/(1 + 2\delta)\), i.e. \((0.5385, 0.2308, 0.2308)\) for \(\delta = 0.8\). But at period 2 the player re-optimises with \(u(x_2) + \delta u(x_3)\) and chooses \((0.3675, 0.0940)\): the plan is inconsistent. A fully rational player anticipates this struggle between the present self and the future self; solving it by backward induction leads to \(x_1 = 1\) — overconsumption to prevent future misuse. From now on, discounting is always exponential.

10.2 Multistage games

A multistage game is a finite sequence of \(T\) normal-form stage games \(G_1, \dots, G_T\), played by the same players, with the outcome of each stage observed before the next. Stage payoffs \(u_i^{(t)}\) are aggregated with a common, commonly known discount factor: \[u_i = \sum_{t=1}^T \delta^{t-1} u_i^{(t)}.\]

Each stage is a simultaneous-move game, so the whole is a dynamic game of complete but imperfect information. A strategy specifies the first-stage action and the action at each later stage as a function of the history of outcomes.

Al and Bob first play a Prisoner’s Dilemma; out of jail, each may join a Gang or stay a Loner:

Al \ Bob m f
M 4, 4 −1, 5
F 5, −1 1, 1
Al \ Bob ℓ g
L 0, 0 −4, −1
G −1, −4 −3, −3

The second stage has two NE: the “friendly” \((L, \ell)\) and the “gang” \((G, g)\). Each player already has \(2 \times 2^4 = 32\) pure strategies.

  1. If \(s^{t*}\) is a NE of stage game \(G_t\) for each \(t\), then playing \(s^{1*}, \dots, s^{T*}\) regardless of history is an SPE.
  2. In any NE (even non-subgame-perfect) of a multistage game, a NE of \(G_T\) is played in the last stage.
  3. If every stage game has a unique NE, the multistage game has a unique SPE: play those NE regardless of history.
  1. holds because at the last stage there is no future to influence, so players simply best-respond. Statement (1) removes any strategic link between stages: in Prisoner–Revenge, “\(F\) then \(L\) whatever happened” and “\(F\) then \(G\) whatever happened” are SPE. But if the last stage has multiple equilibria, non-equilibrium play can be sustained earlier.

In Prisoner–Revenge, let both players use “play \(M\) in stage 1; then play Loner if the first outcome was \((M, m)\), otherwise Gang”. This is an SPE iff \(\delta \geq \tfrac13\).

Proof. A NE is played in every second-stage subgame, so it suffices to check the first stage. Against this strategy, \(M\) yields \(4 + 0\cdot\delta\) and \(F\) yields \(5 - 3\delta\); \(M\) is a best response iff \(\delta \geq \tfrac13\). \(\square\)

The construction is carrot and stick: play the desired (non-equilibrium) action now; reward compliance with the good equilibrium (carrot); punish deviation with the bad one (stick). \(\delta\) must be large enough for the difference to matter — for \(\delta = 0\) the stick is not a credible deterrent. The same trick can support any first-stage profile: \((F, m)\) is sustainable if Bob prefers \(-1 + 0\cdot\delta\) to \(1 - 3\delta\), i.e. \(\delta \geq \tfrac23\).

A strategy is optimal if no alternative strategy improves the payoff at any information set; it is one-stage unimprovable if no change of the action at a single information set does. In finite multistage games (and in infinite ones with discounting), a one-stage unimprovable strategy is optimal.

Proof idea. Suppose \(s_i\) is one-stage unimprovable but some \(s_i'\) does better. Take the profitable deviation with the fewest differences and consider its last deviation: in the subgame starting there, \(s_i'\) differs from \(s_i\) at a single information set and improves the payoff — contradiction. \(\square\)

To verify an SPE, it is enough to check one-shot deviations — exactly what backward induction does.

10.3 Finitely repeated games

\(G(T, \delta)\) is the multistage game in which the same stage game \(G\) is played \(T\) times, with discount \(\delta\). For \(T = \infty\) we need \(\delta < 1\); \(\delta\) may also be read as the probability that the game continues one more round.

If \(G\) has a unique NE \(s^*\), then \(G(T, \delta)\) with \(T < \infty\) has a unique SPE: play \(s^*\) at every stage.

In the twice-repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma (with \(4, 0, 5, 1\) and \(\delta = 1\)) the second stage always ends at \((F,F)\), so the first stage is a Prisoner’s Dilemma with every payoff raised by \(1\) — and \((F,F)\) is played twice. Repetitions of games with a unique equilibrium are uninteresting.

Multiple stage equilibria change everything. Add a third strategy:

M F H
M 4, 4 0, 5 0, 0
F 5, 0 1, 1 0, 0
H 0, 0 0, 0 3, 3

Now \((F,F)\) and \((H,H)\) are both NE. Consider “play \(M\); then \(H\) if the first outcome was \((M,M)\), otherwise \(F\)”. The first stage becomes the game with \((M,M)\) raised by \(3\) and everything else by \(1\), in which \((M,M)\), with payoffs \((7,7)\), is a NE (\(7 > 6\)). Cooperation is supported in the first stage even though \((M,M)\) is not an equilibrium of \(G\). Note that this needs no communication — only shared anticipation.

Remark (Punishing the punisher). Punishing with \((F,F)\) instead of \((H,H)\) hurts the punisher too, which invites renegotiation (“let’s forget it and play \((H,H)\)”). The construction is more robust with punishments that benefit the punisher: add strategies \(P\), \(Q\) with \((P,P)\) giving \((4, \tfrac12)\) and \((Q,Q)\) giving \((\tfrac12, 4)\). Then a deviation by Bob is punished by \((P,P)\) — good for Al — and vice versa. Cooperation is easier when punishments exist, and better still when several punishment options are available.

In finitely repeated games cooperation is therefore limited: the last stage is always played selfishly, and cooperative play requires multiple stage equilibria.

10.4 Infinitely repeated games

With \(T = \infty\) there is no last stage and backward induction cannot start. Surprisingly, this leads to a stronger conclusion: no external punishment equilibrium is needed.

In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, the grim-trigger strategy plays \(M\) at stage 1 and at every stage \(t > 1\) plays \(M\) if all previous outcomes were \((M,M)\); otherwise it plays \(F\) forever.

With stage payoffs \((M,M) = (4,4)\), \((M,F) = (0,5)\), \((F,F) = (1,1)\), both players using grim trigger is an SPE of \(G(\infty, \delta)\) iff \(\delta \geq \tfrac14\).

Proof. (NE) Suppose Al uses grim trigger. If Bob ever deviates, Al plays \(F\) forever, so Bob’s best continuation is \(F\) forever too. Deviating now yields \[V_{\text{dev}} = 5 + \delta + \delta^2 + \dots = 5 + \frac{\delta}{1 - \delta},\] while cooperating forever yields \(V_{\text{coop}} = 4/(1-\delta)\). \(V_{\text{coop}} \geq V_{\text{dev}}\) iff \(\delta \geq \tfrac14\). (SPE) Subgames come in two classes: after a history of \((M,M)\) only, the subgame is identical to the whole game, where grim trigger is a NE; after any deviation, grim trigger prescribes \((F,F)\) forever, which is a NE of that subgame. \(\square\)

The argument generalises.

A payoff vector is feasible if it is a convex combination of pure-strategy payoff vectors of \(G\) (achievable by alternating outcomes, or with a public randomisation device).

Let \(G\) be a finite static game of complete information, \((e_1, \dots, e_n)\) the payoffs of a NE of \(G\), and \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\) feasible payoffs with \(x_j > e_j\) for all \(j\). Then for \(\delta\) close enough to \(1\), \(G(\infty, \delta)\) has an SPE with average payoffs \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\).

Figure: feasible payoffs of the Prisoner’s Dilemma (the quadrilateral) and the payoffs above the NE \((1,1)\) that the folk theorem sustains (shaded).

Developments of the theorem:

  • Minimax punishment. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma the NE payoff coincides with the security payoff \(1\), but in general \(\text{maximin} \leq u(\text{NE})\), and harsher (still credible) punishments can push the threshold down to the minimax payoffs \((r_1, \dots, r_n)\).
  • Small \(\delta\). A lower discount factor weakens punishments; in some games (not the Prisoner’s Dilemma) punishments harsher than reverting to the NE are available — the Cournot carrot-and-stick of Chapter 11.

10.5 Tit for tat and reputation

Grim trigger holds a grudge forever. With two actions (cooperate / defect), tit for tat (TFT) plays at stage \(t\) whatever the opponent played at \(t-1\): it punishes immediately but forgives after one round (eye for an eye, live-and-let-live, reciprocal altruism in biology). TFT was the winner of Axelrod’s famous tournaments, but it is not subgame perfect: after a single unilateral deviation two TFT players alternate defection forever (a “death spiral”), which is not a NE of that subgame. Tit for two tats forgives the first defection and punishes the second; it avoids death spirals but is exploited by aggressive strategies.

In a one-shot trust game A either does Not trust (payoffs \((0,0)\)) or Trusts, after which B Collaborates \((1,1)\) or Defects \((-1,2)\). Backward induction predicts no trust. In the repeated game, A can use a grim trigger (“trust as long as B has always collaborated”), and B’s best response is to collaborate if \(\frac{1}{1-\delta} \geq 2\), i.e. \(\delta \geq \tfrac12\).

To strengthen B’s incentives, introduce a guarantor G who holds an insurance of \(2\) from B, keeps it if B defects, and otherwise returns it minus a fee of \(0.1\). With G, defecting no longer pays and trust is the SPE path. Why would G not simply keep the insurance? One-shot, keeping it is dominant. In the repeated game, G also has a reputation to protect: keeping the insurance yields \(2\) once, honest service yields \(0.1/(1-\delta)\), which is better iff \(\delta \geq 0.95\).

10.6 Beyond Nash reversion

Friedman’s theorem punishes deviators by reverting to a stage NE forever. Two refinements widen and sharpen the picture.

Player \(i\)’s minmax (pure-strategy) payoff is \(\underline v_i = \min_{s_{-i}}\max_{s_i}u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\): the lowest payoff the others can hold \(i\) to, if \(i\) best-responds. A payoff vector is individually rational if \(x_i > \underline v_i\) for all \(i\).

No equilibrium of the repeated game can give a player less than \(\underline v_i\) on average: they could always best-respond at every stage. Conversely:

In an infinitely repeated game (satisfying a mild full-dimensionality condition), every feasible and individually rational payoff vector is the average payoff of some SPE, for all \(\delta\) close enough to \(1\).

The gain over Friedman lies between the minmax and the NE payoffs. Punishments harsher than the stage NE are possible, provided they are temporary and the punishers are themselves rewarded for carrying them out (“stick and carrot”, Abreu). With a stronger stick, a lower \(\delta\) suffices.

Checking an equilibrium. By the one-stage deviation principle, a profile is an SPE iff no player gains by deviating once, in any history, and then following the profile. Two kinds of history must be checked:

  • on the path: cooperating gives \(R/(1-\delta)\); the best one-shot deviation gives \(T\) now plus the discounted punishment phase;
  • off the path: the punishment itself must be a best response. Grim trigger passes, because it prescribes a stage NE. Tit for tat fails for some \(\delta\): after a single deviation it prescribes alternating \((C, D)\), \((D, C)\), …, and returning to cooperation may pay more. So tit for tat is a NE but generally not an SPE.

Alternating outcomes. Payoffs outside the pure-outcome set (a fair split of an asymmetric outcome) are reached by alternation. With \(\delta < 1\), the player who is favoured first gets slightly more: \((a + \delta b)/(1 - \delta^2)\) versus \((b + \delta a)/(1 - \delta^2)\).

10.7 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 5. Several printed solutions contain slips; the corrected values are given here.

(BM 5.1) A guitarist and a pianist play two songs; for each song each Sings (S) or just Plays (J). \((S, S) = (5, 5)\); \((S, J) = (1, 6)\); \((J, S) = (7, 1)\); \((J, J) = (0, 0)\) (guitarist first). Payoffs add over the two songs. (a) Find the stage NE. (b) Find the SPE that repeats the same stage outcome and gives the highest total utility. (c) Is there an SPE in which the first song is a duet?

Solution.

  1. Pure NE \((S, J)\) and \((J, S)\), and a mixed one: the guitarist sings with \(1/2\), the pianist with \(1/3\), with payoffs \((7/3, 3)\). (b) Repeating \((J, S)\) gives \((14, 2)\), total \(16\); repeating \((S, J)\) gives \((2, 12)\), total \(14\). So \((J, S)\) twice. (c) No. A deviation from \((S, S)\) is detected and attributed: the guitarist deviating yields \((J, S)\), the pianist \((S, J)\). For the guitarist, \(5 + v_G(\text{reward}) \geq 7 + v_G(\text{punishment})\) needs a gap \(\geq 2\); the only stage NE giving him at least \(3\) is \((J, S)\). For the pianist the gap must be \(\geq 1\), so the reward must give him at least \(2\), which rules out \((J, S)\) (he gets \(1\)). No second-stage NE rewards both. (An exhaustive check over all assignments of the three stage NE confirms it. The printed solution overlooks the guitarist’s own incentive to deviate.)

(BM 5.2) At Christmas Ashley and Brook each give a gift (G, costing \(10\), worth \(100\) to the receiver) or not (N). At New Year’s Eve they choose Downtown or Home: \((D, D) = (50, 50)\), \((H, H) = (0, 0)\), mismatch \((-100, -100)\). The second stage is discounted by \(\delta\). Find a trivial SPE, and the smallest \(\delta\) for an SPE with gifts.

Solution. Stage 1 is a Prisoner’s Dilemma: \((G, G) = (90, 90)\), \((G, N) = (-10, 100)\), NE \((N, N)\). Stage 2 has NE \((D, D)\) and \((H, H)\). Trivial SPE: \((N, N)\) followed by either NE regardless of history. Carrot and stick: play \(G\); then \(D\) if both gave, \(H\) otherwise. Deviating gains \(100 - 90 = 10\) now and loses \(50\delta\) later, so the SPE requires \(\delta \geq 1/5\).

(BM 5.3) Ann and Brian play daily: both do chores \((-1, -1)\); only one does: the cleaner \(-2\), the other \(5\); neither \((0, 0)\); \(\delta = 0.9\). (a) Stage NE? (b) Show that “Ann always cleans” is not sustainable. (c) Show that alternating (Brian on odd days, Ann on even days) is, with a grim trigger.

Solution.

  1. \(N\) is dominant: NE \((N, N)\) with \((0, 0)\), which is also each player’s minmax. (b) Ann would average \(-2 < 0\): not individually rational, so no equilibrium can sustain it. (c) Grim trigger: follow the alternation; after any deviation play \(N\) forever. Values: Ann (lazy first) \(\frac{5 - 2\delta}{1 - \delta^2} = 16.8\), Brian \(\frac{-2 + 5\delta}{1 - \delta^2} = 13.2\). The binding check is whoever must clean today: cleaning gives \(\frac{-2 + 5\delta}{1 - \delta^2}\), deviating gives \(0\) forever, so the alternation is an SPE iff \(\delta \geq 0.4\). It holds at \(\delta = 0.9\). (The printed numerical values use \(1/(1-\delta^2) = 1.2345\) instead of \(5.263\); the conclusion is unaffected.)

(BM 5.4) Carl and Diana either Study or watch Movies in the library: \((S, S) = (10, 10)\), \((S, M) = (0, 18)\), \((M, S) = (15, 0)\), \((M, M) = (7.5, 9)\) (Carl first). (a) NE of \(G(3)\) with \(\delta = 1\)? (b) For which \(\delta\) does grim trigger sustain \((S, S)\) forever? (c) Add a punishment \(P\) giving \((-10, -10)\) whenever used. In \(G'(2)\), when can \((S, S)\) be played?

Solution.

  1. \((M, M)\) is the unique stage NE (dominant), so in \(G(3)\) it is played every night. (b) Diana: \(\frac{10}{1-\delta} \geq 18 + \frac{9\delta}{1-\delta}\), so \(\delta \geq 8/9\). Carl: \(10 \geq 15(1-\delta) + 7.5\delta\), so \(\delta \geq 2/3\). Grim trigger needs \(\delta \geq 8/9\). (c) \(G'\) has NE \((M, M)\) and \((P, P)\) (\(P\) is weakly dominated, but a NE). Play \((S, S)\) in round 1, then \((M, M)\) if round 1 was \((S, S)\) and \((P, P)\) otherwise. Diana: \(10 + 9\delta \geq 18 - 10\delta\), so \(\delta \geq 8/19\); Carl: \(10 + 7.5\delta \geq 15 - 10\delta\), so \(\delta \geq 2/7\). Hence \(\delta \geq 8/19\). Cooperation is possible only in round 1, since the last round must be a stage NE.

(BM 5.5) A warrior and a mage first choose Cast (C) or Draw (D): different actions give \(2\) each, equal actions \(0\), and \(1\) is subtracted from whoever uses their disliked action (C for the warrior, D for the mage). Then a troll appears: both Attack \((5, 5)\), a lone attacker dies (\(-100\)) while the other gets \(10\), both Run \((0, 0)\). Find all SPE.

Solution. Stage 1: \((C, C) = (-1, 0)\), \((C, D) = (1, 1)\), \((D, C) = (2, 2)\), \((D, D) = (0, -1)\). Stage 2 has a dominant action Run, with unique NE \((R, R)\). Since the last stage has a unique NE, nothing can be conditioned on stage 1. The SPE are: any stage-1 NE, then \((R, R)\) after every history. The stage-1 NE are \((C, D)\), \((D, C)\) and the mixed one, in which the warrior Casts with \(3/4\) and the mage Casts with \(1/4\) (each mixture makes the other indifferent; the printed \(1/2\) is a slip).

(BM 5.6) Two chipmunks search an Oak or a Pine: \((O, O) = (2, 2)\), \((O, P) = (3, 5)\), \((P, O) = (5, 3)\), \((P, P) = (1, 1)\). Then a wolf comes: both Help \((-1, -1)\), a lone helper is eaten (\(-100\)) while the other gets \(0\), both Flee \((-4, -4)\). Find all SPE.

Solution. Stage 2: Flee is dominant (\(0 > -1\), \(-4 > -100\)), so \((F, F)\) is the unique NE. Stage 1 NE: \((O, P)\), \((P, O)\), and both playing \(O\) with \(0.4\). SPE: one of these, then \((F, F)\) after every history. As in the previous problem, a unique last-stage NE leaves no room for rewards or punishments.

(BM 5.7) A knight and a thief simultaneously Attack the warlock or Save the princess. Both attack: \((0, 0)\); both save: \((-100, -100)\). Otherwise the hero who attacked may then Grab the wand and kill his ally (G) or be Fair (F). The knight values killing at \(-20\), the thief at \(20\) (the victim gets \(-100\)); a fair split gives \(10\) each. Reduce the game, find the SPE, and discuss \((AG, AG)\).

Solution. At the second move the knight plays \(F\) (\(10 > -20\)) and the thief plays \(G\) (\(20 > 10\)). Reduced game: \((A, A) = (0, 0)\), \((A, S) = (10, 10)\), \((S, A) = (-100, 20)\), \((S, S) = (-100, -100)\). \(A\) is dominant for the knight, and the thief answers \(S\). SPE \((AF, SG)\): the knight slays and is fair, the thief saves, and the thief’s betrayal plan is never used. \((AG, AG)\) is a NE: both attack, and the princess burns. Switching to \(S\) gives \(-100\) because of the other’s announced \(G\), and that threat is not credible for the knight.

(BM 5.8) In the infinitely repeated game \((M, M) = (1, 1)\), \((M, F) = (-2, 3)\), \((F, F) = (0, 0)\), describe tit for tat, find when it sustains \((M, M)\), and say whether it is an SPE.

Solution. Tit for tat: play \(M\) first, then copy the opponent’s previous move. Against tit for tat, defecting once and returning gives \(3 - 2\delta\) over two periods versus \(1 + \delta\); defecting forever gives \(3\) versus \(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\). Both require \(\delta \geq 2/3\). It is not an SPE in general: after a single deviation it locks the players into alternating \((M, F)\), \((F, M)\), The punisher’s echo is worth \(\frac{3 - 2\delta}{1-\delta^2}\), while forgiving restores \(\frac{1}{1-\delta} = \frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta^2}\). For \(\delta > 2/3\) forgiving is better, so tit for tat fails the one-shot deviation test off the path exactly when it works on the path (except at \(\delta = 2/3\)).

(BM 5.9) Stage game (rows/columns M, F, H): \((7,7)\) \((-2,9)\) \((-1,-1)\) / \((9,-2)\) \((2,2)\) \((0,0)\) / \((-1,-1)\) \((0,0)\) \((6,6)\), repeated twice. (a) Stage NE? (b) An SPE that plays \((M, M)\) first? (c) The condition on \(\delta\)?

Solution.

  1. \(M\) is strictly dominated by \(F\). Pure NE \((F, F)\) and \((H, H)\); mixed: each plays \(F\) with \(3/4\) and \(H\) with \(1/4\), with payoff \(1.5\) (the printed \(\tfrac14F + \tfrac34H\), payoff \(5\), inverts the weights). (b) Play \(M\); then \((H, H)\) if the first outcome was \((M, M)\), and \((F, F)\) otherwise. (c) Deviating to \(F\) gains \(2\) now and loses \(6 - 2 = 4\) later: \(9 + 2\delta \leq 7 + 6\delta\), so \(\delta \geq 1/2\).

(BM 5.10) Siblings play every afternoon: \((F, F) = (1, 1)\), \((B, F) = (3, -3)\), \((B, B) = (0, 0)\). Find strategies sustaining friendship forever. Do they work for \(\delta = 0.6\)?

Solution. Grim trigger: cooperating gives \(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\), the best deviation \(3 + 0\), so \(\delta \geq 2/3\). Tit for tat: a one-period deviation needs \(1 + \delta \geq 3 - 3\delta\) (\(\delta \geq 1/2\)), but permanent defection gives \(3\), so again \(\delta \geq 2/3\). At \(\delta = 0.6\) neither sustains cooperation (\(\frac{1}{0.4} = 2.5 < 3\)). The printed solution’s conclusion for \(\delta = 0.6\) is wrong. For \(\delta \geq 2/3\), grim trigger is an SPE; tit for tat is only a NE.

From the course material:

Consider the stage game below repeated twice with \(\delta = 1\). Find its NE, and an SPE of the repeated game in which \((M, M)\) is played in the first stage. For which \(\delta\) does it remain an SPE?

M F H
M 7, 7 −2, 9 −1, −1
F 9, −2 2, 2 0, 0
H −1, −1 0, 0 6, 6

Solution. Pure NE of the stage game: \((F,F)\) and \((H,H)\) (check: against \(F\), \(F\) gives 2 > −2, 0; against \(H\), \(H\) gives 6 > −1, 0). Strategy: play \(M\); then \(H\) if the first outcome was \((M,M)\), else \(F\). Deviating from \(M\) to \(F\) in stage 1 yields \(9 + 2\delta\); complying yields \(7 + 6\delta\). The strategy is an SPE iff \(7 + 6\delta \geq 9 + 2\delta\), i.e. \(\delta \geq \tfrac12\); with \(\delta = 1\) it holds.

In the infinitely repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with stage payoffs \((M,M) = (R,R)\), \((F,M) = (T, S)\), \((F,F) = (P,P)\) with \(T > R > P > S\), find the threshold on \(\delta\) for grim trigger to be an SPE.

Solution. Cooperating: \(R/(1-\delta)\). Deviating: \(T + \delta P/(1-\delta)\). Grim trigger is an SPE iff \(R \geq (1-\delta)T + \delta P\), i.e. \(\delta \geq \dfrac{T - R}{T - P}\). With \(T=5\), \(R=4\), \(P=1\) this gives \(\tfrac14\).

title: "Multistage and repeated games"

Many interactions consist of several rounds, each producing a partial payoff: tournaments, rounds of cards, partial exams, network sessions. This chapter studies **multistage games** (a sequence of static games played by the same players) and their most important special case, **repeated games** (the same stage game over and over). The central message is that the future can discipline the present: threats of punishment and promises of reward make cooperative behaviour — impossible in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma — part of a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

## Why exponential discounting

Aggregating payoffs across periods requires a discounting rule. A single-person example shows why it must be exponential.

A player has a budget $K = 1$ to spend over three periods with per-period utility $u(x) = \log(1+x)$ and total value $v = u(x_1) + \delta u(x_2) + \delta^2 u(x_3)$. The first-order conditions $\frac{1}{1+x_1} = \frac{\delta}{1+x_2} = \frac{\delta^2}{1+x_3}$ give
$$x_1 = \frac{3 - \delta - \delta^2}{1 + \delta + \delta^2}, \quad x_2 = \frac{-1 + 3\delta - \delta^2}{1 + \delta + \delta^2}, \quad x_3 = \frac{-1 - \delta + 3\delta^2}{1 + \delta + \delta^2},$$
an equal split for $\delta = 1$ and $(0.6393, 0.3115, 0.0492)$ for $\delta = 0.8$ (an interior solution requires $3\delta^2 - \delta - 1 \geq 0$, i.e. $\delta \gtrsim 0.77$). At period 2, with $1 - x_1$ left, the player re-optimises $u(x_2) + \delta u(x_3)$ and finds $x_2 = (2 - x_1 - \delta)/(1 + \delta)$: for $x_1 = 0.6393$ this is again $0.3115$. Exponential discounting is **time consistent** — the plan made today is still optimal tomorrow.

Now try **quasi-hyperbolic** discounting, $v = u(x_1) + \delta u(x_2) + \delta u(x_3)$ (the future discounted by a single factor). The plan is $x_1 = (3 - 2\delta)/(1 + 2\delta)$, $x_2 = x_3 = (2\delta - 1)/(1 + 2\delta)$, i.e. $(0.5385, 0.2308, 0.2308)$ for $\delta = 0.8$. But at period 2 the player re-optimises with $u(x_2) + \delta u(x_3)$ and chooses $(0.3675, 0.0940)$: the plan is **inconsistent**. A fully rational player anticipates this struggle between the present self and the future self; solving it by backward induction leads to $x_1 = 1$ — overconsumption to prevent future misuse. From now on, discounting is always exponential.

## Multistage games

::: {.definition name="Multistage game"}
A **multistage game** is a finite sequence of $T$ normal-form **stage games** $G_1, \dots, G_T$, played by the same players, with the outcome of each stage observed before the next. Stage payoffs $u_i^{(t)}$ are aggregated with a common, commonly known discount factor:
$$u_i = \sum_{t=1}^T \delta^{t-1} u_i^{(t)}.$$
:::

Each stage is a simultaneous-move game, so the whole is a dynamic game of complete but imperfect information. A strategy specifies the first-stage action and the action at each later stage *as a function of the history* of outcomes.

::: {.example name="Prisoner–Revenge"}
Al and Bob first play a Prisoner's Dilemma; out of jail, each may join a **G**ang or stay a **L**oner:

| Al \\ Bob | m | f |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **M** | 4, 4 | −1, 5 |
| **F** | 5, −1 | 1, 1 |

| Al \\ Bob | ℓ | g |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **L** | 0, 0 | −4, −1 |
| **G** | −1, −4 | −3, −3 |

The second stage has two NE: the "friendly" $(L, \ell)$ and the "gang" $(G, g)$. Each player already has $2 \times 2^4 = 32$ pure strategies.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Equilibria of multistage games"}
1. If $s^{t*}$ is a NE of stage game $G_t$ for each $t$, then playing $s^{1*}, \dots, s^{T*}$ regardless of history is an SPE.
2. In any NE (even non-subgame-perfect) of a multistage game, a NE of $G_T$ is played in the last stage.
3. If every stage game has a unique NE, the multistage game has a unique SPE: play those NE regardless of history.
:::

(2) holds because at the last stage there is no future to influence, so players simply best-respond. Statement (1) removes any strategic link between stages: in Prisoner–Revenge, "$F$ then $L$ whatever happened" and "$F$ then $G$ whatever happened" are SPE. But if the last stage has **multiple** equilibria, non-equilibrium play can be sustained earlier.

::: {.proposition name="Strategic connection"}
In Prisoner–Revenge, let both players use "play $M$ in stage 1; then play Loner if the first outcome was $(M, m)$, otherwise Gang". This is an SPE iff $\delta \geq \tfrac13$.
:::

*Proof.* A NE is played in every second-stage subgame, so it suffices to check the first stage. Against this strategy, $M$ yields $4 + 0\cdot\delta$ and $F$ yields $5 - 3\delta$; $M$ is a best response iff $\delta \geq \tfrac13$. $\square$

The construction is **carrot and stick**: play the desired (non-equilibrium) action now; reward compliance with the good equilibrium (carrot); punish deviation with the bad one (stick). $\delta$ must be large enough for the difference to matter — for $\delta = 0$ the stick is not a credible deterrent. The same trick can support *any* first-stage profile: $(F, m)$ is sustainable if Bob prefers $-1 + 0\cdot\delta$ to $1 - 3\delta$, i.e. $\delta \geq \tfrac23$.

::: {.theorem name="One-stage deviation principle"}
A strategy is *optimal* if no alternative strategy improves the payoff at any information set; it is *one-stage unimprovable* if no change of the action at a *single* information set does. In finite multistage games (and in infinite ones with discounting), **a one-stage unimprovable strategy is optimal**.
:::

*Proof idea.* Suppose $s_i$ is one-stage unimprovable but some $s_i'$ does better. Take the profitable deviation with the fewest differences and consider its *last* deviation: in the subgame starting there, $s_i'$ differs from $s_i$ at a single information set and improves the payoff — contradiction. $\square$

To verify an SPE, it is enough to check one-shot deviations — exactly what backward induction does.

## Finitely repeated games

::: {.definition name="Repeated game"}
$G(T, \delta)$ is the multistage game in which the same stage game $G$ is played $T$ times, with discount $\delta$. For $T = \infty$ we need $\delta < 1$; $\delta$ may also be read as the probability that the game continues one more round.
:::

::: {.corollary name="Unique stage equilibrium"}
If $G$ has a unique NE $s^*$, then $G(T, \delta)$ with $T < \infty$ has a unique SPE: play $s^*$ at every stage.
:::

In the twice-repeated Prisoner's Dilemma (with $4, 0, 5, 1$ and $\delta = 1$) the second stage always ends at $(F,F)$, so the first stage is a Prisoner's Dilemma with every payoff raised by $1$ — and $(F,F)$ is played twice. Repetitions of games with a unique equilibrium are uninteresting.

**Multiple stage equilibria change everything.** Add a third strategy:

| | M | F | H |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **M** | 4, 4 | 0, 5 | 0, 0 |
| **F** | 5, 0 | 1, 1 | 0, 0 |
| **H** | 0, 0 | 0, 0 | 3, 3 |

Now $(F,F)$ and $(H,H)$ are both NE. Consider "play $M$; then $H$ if the first outcome was $(M,M)$, otherwise $F$". The first stage becomes the game with $(M,M)$ raised by $3$ and everything else by $1$, in which $(M,M)$, with payoffs $(7,7)$, is a NE ($7 > 6$). Cooperation is supported in the first stage even though $(M,M)$ is not an equilibrium of $G$. Note that this needs no communication — only shared anticipation.

::: {.remark name="Punishing the punisher"}
Punishing with $(F,F)$ instead of $(H,H)$ hurts the punisher too, which invites renegotiation ("let's forget it and play $(H,H)$"). The construction is more robust with punishments that *benefit* the punisher: add strategies $P$, $Q$ with $(P,P)$ giving $(4, \tfrac12)$ and $(Q,Q)$ giving $(\tfrac12, 4)$. Then a deviation by Bob is punished by $(P,P)$ — good for Al — and vice versa. Cooperation is easier when punishments exist, and better still when several punishment options are available.
:::

In finitely repeated games cooperation is therefore limited: the last stage is always played selfishly, and cooperative play requires multiple stage equilibria.

## Infinitely repeated games

With $T = \infty$ there is no last stage and backward induction cannot start. Surprisingly, this leads to a *stronger* conclusion: no external punishment equilibrium is needed.

::: {.definition name="Grim trigger"}
In the Prisoner's Dilemma, the **grim-trigger** strategy plays $M$ at stage 1 and at every stage $t > 1$ plays $M$ if all previous outcomes were $(M,M)$; otherwise it plays $F$ forever.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Cooperation in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma"}
With stage payoffs $(M,M) = (4,4)$, $(M,F) = (0,5)$, $(F,F) = (1,1)$, both players using grim trigger is an SPE of $G(\infty, \delta)$ iff $\delta \geq \tfrac14$.
:::

*Proof.* (NE) Suppose Al uses grim trigger. If Bob ever deviates, Al plays $F$ forever, so Bob's best continuation is $F$ forever too. Deviating now yields
$$V_{\text{dev}} = 5 + \delta + \delta^2 + \dots = 5 + \frac{\delta}{1 - \delta},$$
while cooperating forever yields $V_{\text{coop}} = 4/(1-\delta)$. $V_{\text{coop}} \geq V_{\text{dev}}$ iff $\delta \geq \tfrac14$. (SPE) Subgames come in two classes: after a history of $(M,M)$ only, the subgame is identical to the whole game, where grim trigger is a NE; after any deviation, grim trigger prescribes $(F,F)$ forever, which is a NE of that subgame. $\square$

The argument generalises.

::: {.definition name="Feasible and individually rational payoffs"}
A payoff vector is **feasible** if it is a convex combination of pure-strategy payoff vectors of $G$ (achievable by alternating outcomes, or with a public randomisation device).
:::

::: {.theorem name="Friedman's folk theorem"}
Let $G$ be a finite static game of complete information, $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ the payoffs of a NE of $G$, and $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ feasible payoffs with $x_j > e_j$ for all $j$. Then for $\delta$ close enough to $1$, $G(\infty, \delta)$ has an SPE with average payoffs $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$.
:::

Developments of the theorem:

- **Minimax punishment.** In the Prisoner's Dilemma the NE payoff coincides with the security payoff $1$, but in general $\text{maximin} \leq u(\text{NE})$, and harsher (still credible) punishments can push the threshold down to the minimax payoffs $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$.
- **Small $\delta$.** A lower discount factor weakens punishments; in some games (not the Prisoner's Dilemma) punishments harsher than reverting to the NE are available — the Cournot carrot-and-stick of Chapter 11.

## Tit for tat and reputation

Grim trigger holds a grudge forever. With two actions (cooperate / defect), **tit for tat** (TFT) plays at stage $t$ whatever the opponent played at $t-1$: it punishes immediately but forgives after one round (eye for an eye, live-and-let-live, reciprocal altruism in biology). TFT was the winner of Axelrod's famous tournaments, but it is not subgame perfect: after a single unilateral deviation two TFT players alternate defection forever (a "death spiral"), which is not a NE of that subgame. *Tit for two tats* forgives the first defection and punishes the second; it avoids death spirals but is exploited by aggressive strategies.

::: {.example name="Trust and certified reputation"}
In a one-shot trust game A either does **N**ot trust (payoffs $(0,0)$) or **T**rusts, after which B **C**ollaborates $(1,1)$ or **D**efects $(-1,2)$. Backward induction predicts no trust. In the repeated game, A can use a grim trigger ("trust as long as B has always collaborated"), and B's best response is to collaborate if $\frac{1}{1-\delta} \geq 2$, i.e. $\delta \geq \tfrac12$.

To strengthen B's incentives, introduce a **guarantor** G who holds an insurance of $2$ from B, keeps it if B defects, and otherwise returns it minus a fee of $0.1$. With G, defecting no longer pays and trust is the SPE path. Why would G not simply keep the insurance? One-shot, keeping it is dominant. In the repeated game, G also has a reputation to protect: keeping the insurance yields $2$ once, honest service yields $0.1/(1-\delta)$, which is better iff $\delta \geq 0.95$.
:::

## Beyond Nash reversion

Friedman's theorem punishes deviators by reverting to a stage NE forever. Two refinements widen and sharpen the picture.

::: {.definition name="Minmax payoff"}
Player $i$'s **minmax** (pure-strategy) payoff is $\underline v_i = \min_{s_{-i}}\max_{s_i}u_i(s_i, s_{-i})$: the lowest payoff the others can hold $i$ to, if $i$ best-responds. A payoff vector is **individually rational** if $x_i > \underline v_i$ for all $i$.
:::

No equilibrium of the repeated game can give a player less than $\underline v_i$ on average: they could always best-respond at every stage. Conversely:

::: {.theorem name="Folk theorem (Fudenberg and Maskin, 1986)"}
In an infinitely repeated game (satisfying a mild full-dimensionality condition), every feasible and individually rational payoff vector is the average payoff of some SPE, for all $\delta$ close enough to $1$.
:::

The gain over Friedman lies between the minmax and the NE payoffs. Punishments harsher than the stage NE are possible, provided they are temporary and the punishers are themselves rewarded for carrying them out ("stick and carrot", Abreu). With a stronger stick, a lower $\delta$ suffices.

**Checking an equilibrium.** By the one-stage deviation principle, a profile is an SPE iff no player gains by deviating once, in any history, and then following the profile. Two kinds of history must be checked:

- **on the path:** cooperating gives $R/(1-\delta)$; the best one-shot deviation gives $T$ now plus the discounted punishment phase;
- **off the path:** the punishment itself must be a best response. Grim trigger passes, because it prescribes a stage NE. Tit for tat fails for some $\delta$: after a single deviation it prescribes alternating $(C, D)$, $(D, C)$, …, and returning to cooperation may pay more. So tit for tat is a NE but generally not an SPE.

**Alternating outcomes.** Payoffs outside the pure-outcome set (a fair split of an asymmetric outcome) are reached by alternation. With $\delta < 1$, the player who is favoured first gets slightly more: $(a + \delta b)/(1 - \delta^2)$ versus $(b + \delta a)/(1 - \delta^2)$.

## Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 5. Several printed solutions contain slips; the corrected values are given here.

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.1)** A guitarist and a pianist play two songs; for each song each Sings (S) or just Plays (J). $(S, S) = (5, 5)$; $(S, J) = (1, 6)$; $(J, S) = (7, 1)$; $(J, J) = (0, 0)$ (guitarist first). Payoffs add over the two songs. (a) Find the stage NE. (b) Find the SPE that repeats the same stage outcome and gives the highest total utility. (c) Is there an SPE in which the first song is a duet?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Pure NE $(S, J)$ and $(J, S)$, and a mixed one: the guitarist sings with $1/2$, the pianist with $1/3$, with payoffs $(7/3, 3)$. (b) Repeating $(J, S)$ gives $(14, 2)$, total $16$; repeating $(S, J)$ gives $(2, 12)$, total $14$. So $(J, S)$ twice. (c) **No.** A deviation from $(S, S)$ is detected and attributed: the guitarist deviating yields $(J, S)$, the pianist $(S, J)$. For the guitarist, $5 + v_G(\text{reward}) \geq 7 + v_G(\text{punishment})$ needs a gap $\geq 2$; the only stage NE giving him at least $3$ is $(J, S)$. For the pianist the gap must be $\geq 1$, so the reward must give him at least $2$, which rules out $(J, S)$ (he gets $1$). No second-stage NE rewards both. (An exhaustive check over all assignments of the three stage NE confirms it. The printed solution overlooks the guitarist's own incentive to deviate.)
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.2)** At Christmas Ashley and Brook each give a gift (G, costing $10$, worth $100$ to the receiver) or not (N). At New Year's Eve they choose Downtown or Home: $(D, D) = (50, 50)$, $(H, H) = (0, 0)$, mismatch $(-100, -100)$. The second stage is discounted by $\delta$. Find a trivial SPE, and the smallest $\delta$ for an SPE with gifts.
:::

::: {.solution}
Stage 1 is a Prisoner's Dilemma: $(G, G) = (90, 90)$, $(G, N) = (-10, 100)$, NE $(N, N)$. Stage 2 has NE $(D, D)$ and $(H, H)$. Trivial SPE: $(N, N)$ followed by either NE regardless of history. Carrot and stick: play $G$; then $D$ if both gave, $H$ otherwise. Deviating gains $100 - 90 = 10$ now and loses $50\delta$ later, so the SPE requires $\delta \geq 1/5$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.3)** Ann and Brian play daily: both do chores $(-1, -1)$; only one does: the cleaner $-2$, the other $5$; neither $(0, 0)$; $\delta = 0.9$. (a) Stage NE? (b) Show that "Ann always cleans" is not sustainable. (c) Show that alternating (Brian on odd days, Ann on even days) is, with a grim trigger.
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $N$ is dominant: NE $(N, N)$ with $(0, 0)$, which is also each player's minmax. (b) Ann would average $-2 < 0$: not individually rational, so no equilibrium can sustain it. (c) Grim trigger: follow the alternation; after any deviation play $N$ forever. Values: Ann (lazy first) $\frac{5 - 2\delta}{1 - \delta^2} = 16.8$, Brian $\frac{-2 + 5\delta}{1 - \delta^2} = 13.2$. The binding check is whoever must clean today: cleaning gives $\frac{-2 + 5\delta}{1 - \delta^2}$, deviating gives $0$ forever, so the alternation is an SPE iff $\delta \geq 0.4$. It holds at $\delta = 0.9$. (The printed numerical values use $1/(1-\delta^2) = 1.2345$ instead of $5.263$; the conclusion is unaffected.)
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.4)** Carl and Diana either Study or watch Movies in the library: $(S, S) = (10, 10)$, $(S, M) = (0, 18)$, $(M, S) = (15, 0)$, $(M, M) = (7.5, 9)$ (Carl first). (a) NE of $G(3)$ with $\delta = 1$? (b) For which $\delta$ does grim trigger sustain $(S, S)$ forever? (c) Add a punishment $P$ giving $(-10, -10)$ whenever used. In $G'(2)$, when can $(S, S)$ be played?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $(M, M)$ is the unique stage NE (dominant), so in $G(3)$ it is played every night. (b) Diana: $\frac{10}{1-\delta} \geq 18 + \frac{9\delta}{1-\delta}$, so $\delta \geq 8/9$. Carl: $10 \geq 15(1-\delta) + 7.5\delta$, so $\delta \geq 2/3$. Grim trigger needs $\delta \geq 8/9$. (c) $G'$ has NE $(M, M)$ and $(P, P)$ ($P$ is weakly dominated, but a NE). Play $(S, S)$ in round 1, then $(M, M)$ if round 1 was $(S, S)$ and $(P, P)$ otherwise. Diana: $10 + 9\delta \geq 18 - 10\delta$, so $\delta \geq 8/19$; Carl: $10 + 7.5\delta \geq 15 - 10\delta$, so $\delta \geq 2/7$. Hence $\delta \geq 8/19$. Cooperation is possible only in round 1, since the last round must be a stage NE.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.5)** A warrior and a mage first choose Cast (C) or Draw (D): different actions give $2$ each, equal actions $0$, and $1$ is subtracted from whoever uses their disliked action (C for the warrior, D for the mage). Then a troll appears: both Attack $(5, 5)$, a lone attacker dies ($-100$) while the other gets $10$, both Run $(0, 0)$. Find all SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Stage 1: $(C, C) = (-1, 0)$, $(C, D) = (1, 1)$, $(D, C) = (2, 2)$, $(D, D) = (0, -1)$. Stage 2 has a dominant action Run, with unique NE $(R, R)$. Since the last stage has a unique NE, nothing can be conditioned on stage 1. The SPE are: any stage-1 NE, then $(R, R)$ after every history. The stage-1 NE are $(C, D)$, $(D, C)$ and the mixed one, in which the warrior Casts with $3/4$ and the mage Casts with $1/4$ (each mixture makes the *other* indifferent; the printed $1/2$ is a slip).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.6)** Two chipmunks search an Oak or a Pine: $(O, O) = (2, 2)$, $(O, P) = (3, 5)$, $(P, O) = (5, 3)$, $(P, P) = (1, 1)$. Then a wolf comes: both Help $(-1, -1)$, a lone helper is eaten ($-100$) while the other gets $0$, both Flee $(-4, -4)$. Find all SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Stage 2: Flee is dominant ($0 > -1$, $-4 > -100$), so $(F, F)$ is the unique NE. Stage 1 NE: $(O, P)$, $(P, O)$, and both playing $O$ with $0.4$. SPE: one of these, then $(F, F)$ after every history. As in the previous problem, a unique last-stage NE leaves no room for rewards or punishments.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.7)** A knight and a thief simultaneously Attack the warlock or Save the princess. Both attack: $(0, 0)$; both save: $(-100, -100)$. Otherwise the hero who attacked may then Grab the wand and kill his ally (G) or be Fair (F). The knight values killing at $-20$, the thief at $20$ (the victim gets $-100$); a fair split gives $10$ each. Reduce the game, find the SPE, and discuss $(AG, AG)$.
:::

::: {.solution}
At the second move the knight plays $F$ ($10 > -20$) and the thief plays $G$ ($20 > 10$). Reduced game: $(A, A) = (0, 0)$, $(A, S) = (10, 10)$, $(S, A) = (-100, 20)$, $(S, S) = (-100, -100)$. $A$ is dominant for the knight, and the thief answers $S$. SPE $(AF, SG)$: the knight slays and is fair, the thief saves, and the thief's betrayal plan is never used. $(AG, AG)$ is a NE: both attack, and the princess burns. Switching to $S$ gives $-100$ because of the other's announced $G$, and that threat is not credible for the knight.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.8)** In the infinitely repeated game $(M, M) = (1, 1)$, $(M, F) = (-2, 3)$, $(F, F) = (0, 0)$, describe tit for tat, find when it sustains $(M, M)$, and say whether it is an SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Tit for tat: play $M$ first, then copy the opponent's previous move. Against tit for tat, defecting once and returning gives $3 - 2\delta$ over two periods versus $1 + \delta$; defecting forever gives $3$ versus $\frac{1}{1-\delta}$. Both require $\delta \geq 2/3$. It is not an SPE in general: after a single deviation it locks the players into alternating $(M, F)$, $(F, M)$, The punisher's echo is worth $\frac{3 - 2\delta}{1-\delta^2}$, while forgiving restores $\frac{1}{1-\delta} = \frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta^2}$. For $\delta > 2/3$ forgiving is better, so tit for tat fails the one-shot deviation test off the path exactly when it works on the path (except at $\delta = 2/3$).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.9)** Stage game (rows/columns M, F, H): $(7,7)$ $(-2,9)$ $(-1,-1)$ / $(9,-2)$ $(2,2)$ $(0,0)$ / $(-1,-1)$ $(0,0)$ $(6,6)$, repeated twice. (a) Stage NE? (b) An SPE that plays $(M, M)$ first? (c) The condition on $\delta$?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) $M$ is strictly dominated by $F$. Pure NE $(F, F)$ and $(H, H)$; mixed: each plays $F$ with $3/4$ and $H$ with $1/4$, with payoff $1.5$ (the printed $\tfrac14F + \tfrac34H$, payoff $5$, inverts the weights). (b) Play $M$; then $(H, H)$ if the first outcome was $(M, M)$, and $(F, F)$ otherwise. (c) Deviating to $F$ gains $2$ now and loses $6 - 2 = 4$ later: $9 + 2\delta \leq 7 + 6\delta$, so $\delta \geq 1/2$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 5.10)** Siblings play every afternoon: $(F, F) = (1, 1)$, $(B, F) = (3, -3)$, $(B, B) = (0, 0)$. Find strategies sustaining friendship forever. Do they work for $\delta = 0.6$?
:::

::: {.solution}
Grim trigger: cooperating gives $\frac{1}{1-\delta}$, the best deviation $3 + 0$, so $\delta \geq 2/3$. Tit for tat: a one-period deviation needs $1 + \delta \geq 3 - 3\delta$ ($\delta \geq 1/2$), but permanent defection gives $3$, so again $\delta \geq 2/3$. At $\delta = 0.6$ **neither** sustains cooperation ($\frac{1}{0.4} = 2.5 < 3$). The printed solution's conclusion for $\delta = 0.6$ is wrong. For $\delta \geq 2/3$, grim trigger is an SPE; tit for tat is only a NE.
:::

From the course material:

::: {.exercise}
Consider the stage game below repeated twice with $\delta = 1$. Find its NE, and an SPE of the repeated game in which $(M, M)$ is played in the first stage. For which $\delta$ does it remain an SPE?

| | M | F | H |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **M** | 7, 7 | −2, 9 | −1, −1 |
| **F** | 9, −2 | 2, 2 | 0, 0 |
| **H** | −1, −1 | 0, 0 | 6, 6 |
:::

::: {.solution}
Pure NE of the stage game: $(F,F)$ and $(H,H)$ (check: against $F$, $F$ gives 2 > −2, 0; against $H$, $H$ gives 6 > −1, 0). Strategy: play $M$; then $H$ if the first outcome was $(M,M)$, else $F$. Deviating from $M$ to $F$ in stage 1 yields $9 + 2\delta$; complying yields $7 + 6\delta$. The strategy is an SPE iff $7 + 6\delta \geq 9 + 2\delta$, i.e. $\delta \geq \tfrac12$; with $\delta = 1$ it holds.
:::

::: {.exercise}
In the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with stage payoffs $(M,M) = (R,R)$, $(F,M) = (T, S)$, $(F,F) = (P,P)$ with $T > R > P > S$, find the threshold on $\delta$ for grim trigger to be an SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Cooperating: $R/(1-\delta)$. Deviating: $T + \delta P/(1-\delta)$. Grim trigger is an SPE iff $R \geq (1-\delta)T + \delta P$, i.e. $\delta \geq \dfrac{T - R}{T - P}$. With $T=5$, $R=4$, $P=1$ this gives $\tfrac14$.
:::
