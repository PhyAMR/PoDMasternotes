14  Auctions and mechanism design

So far the game was given and we predicted its outcome. Mechanism design reverses the question: a designer wants a certain outcome — the good goes to whoever values it most, a protocol allocates bandwidth efficiently — and must design the game so that self-interested players with private information produce it in equilibrium. Auctions are the most important example, and the second-price auction shows that a well-designed game can make telling the truth a dominant strategy. The revelation principle then tells us where to look for good mechanisms.

14.1 Sealed-bid auctions

One seller and \(N\) bidders; bidder \(i\) privately values the object \(v_i \geq 0\). Each bidder submits a bid \(b_i \geq 0\) without seeing the others. The highest bid wins; ties are broken by sharing (or by a coin flip).

14.1.1 First-price auction

The winner pays their own bid. With \(W\) tied winners, \[u_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - b_i & \text{if } i \text{ is the only highest bidder}, \\ (v_i - b_i)/W & \text{if } i \text{ is among } W > 1 \text{ highest bidders}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\]

  • Bidding \(b_i \geq v_i\) always yields \(u_i \leq 0\), so it is weakly dominated by bidding below \(v_i\).
  • Bidding \(b_i = 0\) wins only if everyone bids \(0\), and is weakly dominated by a small positive bid.
  • Between two bids \(0 < b_i < b_i' < v_i\) nothing can be said in general: against opponents bidding \(0\) the lower bid is better, against opponents bidding \((b_i + b_i')/2\) the higher one is.

Rational bidders shade their bids below their value. With incomplete information one computes the Bayesian Nash equilibrium: for two bidders with values independent and uniform on \([0, 1]\), the symmetric BNE is \(b(v) = v/2\). Indeed, if the opponent bids \(v_j/2\), bidding \(b\) wins with probability \(\Pr[v_j/2 < b] = 2b\), and \(\max_b (v - b)\,2b\) gives \(b = v/2\). (With \(N\) bidders, \(b(v) = \tfrac{N-1}{N} v\).)

14.1.2 Second-price (Vickrey) auction

Proposed by Vickrey (1961): the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid \(S = \max_{j \neq i} b_j\): \[u_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - S & \text{if } b_i > S, \\ (v_i - S)/W & \text{if } b_i = S \text{ with } W \text{ tied winners}, \\ 0 & \text{if } b_i < S. \end{cases}\]

In a second-price auction, bidding \(b_i = v_i\) weakly dominates every other bid.

Proof. Compare \(v_i\) with a lower bid \(b_i < v_i\), for each value of \(S\):

  • \(S < b_i\): both bids win alone and pay \(S\): same payoff \(v_i - S\).
  • \(S = b_i\): \(b_i\) ties and gets \((v_i - S)/W\); \(v_i\) wins alone and gets \(v_i - S > (v_i - S)/W\).
  • \(b_i < S < v_i\): \(b_i\) loses (\(0\)), \(v_i\) wins (\(v_i - S > 0\)).
  • \(S \geq v_i\): \(b_i\) loses (\(0\)); \(v_i\) loses or ties at zero surplus (\(0\)).

Now a higher bid \(b_i > v_i\):

  • \(S < v_i\): both win and pay \(S\): same payoff.
  • \(S = v_i\): both get \(0\).
  • \(v_i < S\): \(v_i\) loses (\(0\)); \(b_i\) either loses (\(0\)) or wins/ties paying \(S > v_i\) (negative payoff).

In every case \(v_i\) is at least as good, and strictly better in some. \(\square\)

So \((v_1, \dots, v_n)\) is a Nash equilibrium (in dominant strategies — no beliefs needed), and the second-price auction makes bidders reveal their true values. In a first-price auction, by contrast, bidding \(v_i\) always gives \(0\) and is dominated.

Efficiency. Social surplus is maximised if the object goes to the bidder with the highest value. In the second-price auction this bidder wins and pays \(S\): the bidder gets \(v - S\), the seller \(S\), total \(v\) — Pareto efficient. For the first-price auction with symmetric bidders the BNE is also efficient (bids are increasing in values), and with independent private values the two formats yield the same expected revenue: for two uniform bidders, \(\mathbb E[\max_i v_i]/2 = \tfrac13 = \mathbb E[\min_i v_i]\) (the revenue equivalence theorem).

14.2 Revenue equivalence and reserve prices

First-price bidders shade their bids and second-price bidders do not, but the winner of a second-price auction pays only the second-highest value. Which raises more money? With \(N\) bidders and i.i.d. values uniform on \([0, 1]\), both give expected revenue \(\frac{N-1}{N+1}\). This is no coincidence:

With risk-neutral bidders and independent private values from a common continuous distribution, every auction format in which (i) the highest-value bidder always wins and (ii) a bidder with the lowest possible value expects zero payment yields the same expected revenue and the same expected payment for each type.

The reason is the envelope theorem. A type’s equilibrium utility grows with its value at a rate equal to its winning probability, which is fixed by (i). Payments are then pinned down by (ii). First-price, second-price, English, Dutch, all-pay and even third-price auctions (where bidders bid above their values) all raise \(\frac{N-1}{N+1}\) in the uniform case. Revenue equivalence fails with risk-averse bidders (first-price then raises more, as shading is risky), with asymmetric bidders, and with correlated or common values.

Reserve prices. The seller can beat every efficient format by refusing to sell below a reserve \(r\). With two uniform bidders and a second-price auction, revenue is \(R(r) = \frac{1 + 3r^2 - 4r^3}{3}\), maximised at \(r^* = \tfrac12\) with \(R = \tfrac5{12} > \tfrac13\). The seller gives up some efficient trades (when both values are below \(\tfrac12\)) to extract more from a lone high bidder. In general \(r^*\) solves \(r - \frac{1 - F(r)}{f(r)} = 0\) (zero virtual value), independently of the number of bidders.

14.3 Common values and the winner’s curse

When the object has the same, unknown value for everyone (an oil tract, a spectrum licence), each bidder holds a noisy estimate. Winning is bad news: it means your estimate was the most optimistic. A bidder who bids as if their own estimate were the truth overpays systematically — the winner’s curse. Rational bidders condition on winning, \(\mathbb E[V \mid \text{my signal}, \text{I win}]\), and shade more as the number of competitors grows. Revenue equivalence breaks down here: open ascending auctions, which reveal others’ information as bidders drop out, raise more revenue than sealed bids (the linkage principle).

14.4 Vickrey–Clarke–Groves auctions

A VCG auction extends the second-price idea to several items. Each bidder submits a value for every combination of items; the seller chooses the allocation maximising the total declared value (possibly a hard combinatorial problem); then each winner pays for the harm they cause to the others: \[p_i = \underbrace{V(\mathcal N \setminus \{i\})}_{\substack{\text{others' best total value}\\ \text{without } i}} \;-\; \underbrace{V_{-i}(x^*)}_{\substack{\text{others' value in the}\\ \text{chosen allocation}}} .\]

A bids \(5\) for a single item, B bids \(4\) for a single item, C bids \(7\) for the pair only. The optimal allocation gives one item to A and one to B (total \(9 > 7\)). Without A, the best the others could do is C taking both (\(7\)); in the chosen allocation the others get \(4\) (B). So A pays \(7 - 4 = 3\). Symmetrically B pays \(7 - 5 = 2\), and C, who wins nothing and harms nobody, pays \(0\).

VCG mechanisms are truthful: bidding one’s true valuations is weakly dominant, for the same reason as in the Vickrey auction — a bidder’s report affects only the allocation, never the price conditional on the allocation, and the allocation maximises the bidder’s own utility plus a term they cannot affect. Yet VCG is rare in practice: revenue can be low (even zero), it is non-monotone (adding bidders can lower revenue), and it is vulnerable to collusion and shill bidding.

14.5 Mechanism design

Players \(1, \dots, n\) must choose among public alternatives \(X\). Their preferences depend on private types \(t = (t_1, \dots, t_n) \in T = T_1 \times \dots \times T_n\), the state of the world; types are private, the prior is common knowledge. Payoffs are quasi-linear in money, \[v_i(x, t_i, m_i) = u_i(x, t_i) + m_i ,\] where \(m_i\) is a monetary transfer.

A choice rule is a map \(f : T \to Y\), with \(Y = \{(x, m_1, \dots, m_n) : x \in X,\ \sum_i m_i \leq 0\}\). It consists of a decision rule \(x(t)\) and a transfer rule \(m(t)\); the budget constraint says the designer does not inject money.

The designer would like to implement \(f\), but does not know \(t\), and players might lie.

A mechanism \(\Gamma = (A_1, \dots, A_n; g)\) specifies an action set \(A_i\) for each player and an outcome function \(g : A_1 \times \dots \times A_n \to Y\). In the induced Bayesian game a pure strategy is \(s_i : T_i \to A_i\). The mechanism implements \(f\) (in BNE) if it has a Bayesian Nash equilibrium \(s^*\) with \(g(s^*(t)) = f(t)\) for every \(t \in T\).

Figure: a mechanism implements \(f\) if equilibrium play \(s^*\) followed by the outcome function \(g\) reproduces \(f\).

14.6 The revelation principle

A mechanism is a direct revelation mechanism if \(A_i = T_i\) for every \(i\) and \(g = f\): the designer simply asks players to report their types and applies the choice rule. \(f\) is truthfully implementable in BNE if truth-telling, \(s_i^*(t_i) = t_i\) for all \(i\) and \(t_i\), is a BNE of its direct revelation mechanism.

A choice rule \(f\) is implementable by some mechanism if and only if it is truthfully implementable in Bayesian Nash equilibrium.

Proof. (\(\Leftarrow\)) The direct mechanism is a mechanism. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Let \(\Gamma\) implement \(f\) through the equilibrium \(s^*\). Build a direct mechanism that, on reports \(\hat t\), computes \(g(s^*(\hat t))\): it “plays the equilibrium on behalf of” each player. If some type gained by misreporting \(\hat t_i\), the same type would have gained in \(\Gamma\) by playing \(s_i^*(\hat t_i)\) instead of \(s_i^*(t_i)\), contradicting that \(s^*\) is an equilibrium. \(\square\)

The consequence is powerful: to know whether any clever mechanism can achieve \(f\), it suffices to check whether truth-telling is an equilibrium of the direct one. If the direct mechanism fails, no other mechanism will work. VCG mechanisms are exactly direct mechanisms in which truth-telling is even dominant.

In ICT, “mechanisms” are protocols: a scheduler asking users to declare their traffic demand, a spectrum auction, a crowdsensing platform paying for reported measurements. The revelation principle says one can restrict attention to protocols that ask for the private information directly and pay so that reporting it honestly is in each user’s interest.

14.7 Exercises

Badia and Marchioro’s handbook has no auction problems; these are from Tadelis (T), Chapters 13–14.

(T 13.2) In a second-price auction with complete information (everyone knows all values), is bidding one’s value still weakly dominant? Are there other NE?

Solution. Yes: dominance compares payoffs profile by profile, so information about the others is irrelevant. But there are many other NE. For example, the bidder with the lowest value bids above the highest value and everyone else bids \(0\). The low bidder wins at price \(0\); anyone else could win only by outbidding, paying more than their value. Such equilibria are in weakly dominated strategies and are inefficient.

(T 13.3) Two bidders with values in \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) (equally likely, independent) face a second-price auction with reserve \(r\): if only one bid reaches \(r\) that bidder pays \(r\); if none does, the good is not sold. (a) Is truthful bidding still dominant? (b)–(c) Expected revenue for \(r = 0\) and \(r = 1\)? (d) The optimal \(r\)?

Solution.

  1. Yes: the reserve acts like an extra bid by the seller. (b) Revenue is the lower value: \(\mathbb E[\min] = 1\cdot\tfrac39 + 2\cdot\tfrac19 = \tfrac59\). (c) With \(r = 1\): if both values are \(\geq 1\) (probability \(4/9\)), the minimum is paid, contributing \(\tfrac59\); if exactly one is \(\geq 1\) (\(4/9\)), that bidder pays \(1\), contributing \(\tfrac49\). Total \(1\). (d) For \(r \in (1, 2]\) revenue is \(\tfrac19\cdot2 + \tfrac49 r\), maximised at \(r = 2\): \(\tfrac{10}9\), double the no-reserve revenue. (Above \(2\) nothing sells.) The reserve extracts the full value from a lone high bidder, at the cost of never selling to two low ones.

(T 13.4) Two bidders with values uniform on \([0, 1]\); second-price auction with reserve \(r\). Show that \(r = 0\) is not optimal and find the optimal \(r\).

Solution. Revenue: if both values exceed \(r\) the seller gets \(\min(v_1, v_2)\); if exactly one does, \(r\). Integrating, \(R(r) = \frac{1 + 3r^2 - 4r^3}{3}\). \(R'(r) = 2r - 4r^2 > 0\) for \(0 < r < \tfrac12\), so a small reserve raises revenue (the loss, not selling when both values are tiny, is second order). The optimum is \(r^* = \tfrac12\) with \(R = \tfrac5{12}\), versus \(\tfrac13\) without a reserve; a simulation with \(10^6\) draws gives \(0.4167\).

(T 13.5) First-price auction, two bidders with values \(4\) or \(8\) (probability \(\tfrac12\) each). Show that the low type bids \(4\) and the high type mixes on \([4, 6]\); find the mixture and the seller’s revenue, and compare with an English auction.

Solution. A low type bidding below \(4\) would be overbid by the other low type at no cost, so \(b_L = 4\) (zero surplus). The high type wins against a low rival by bidding just above \(4\), earning \(8 - 4 = 4\) with probability \(\tfrac12\), so its equilibrium payoff is \(2\). A bid \(b\) must give the same: \((8 - b)\bigl(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12F(b)\bigr) = 2\), so \[F(b) = \frac{b - 4}{8 - b}, \qquad b \in [4, 6].\] The support ends where \(F = 1\), at \(b = 6\). (Tadelis states “uniformly”, but uniform bids are not optimal: e.g. \(b = 5\) would give \(2.25\).) Expected high bid \(8 - 4\ln2 = 5.23\), expected maximum of two high bids \(5.55\). Revenue: \(\tfrac14\cdot4 + \tfrac12\cdot5.23 + \tfrac14\cdot5.55 = 5\). An English auction sells at the second-highest value: \(\tfrac34\cdot4 + \tfrac14\cdot8 = 5\). Revenue equivalence holds even with discrete types here.

(T 13.6) In a third-price auction (\(n \geq 3\) bidders, values uniform on \([0, 1]\); the highest bid wins and pays the third-highest bid), verify that \(s(\theta) = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\theta\) is a symmetric BNE, explain overbidding, and compute the revenue.

Solution. Bidding above value is harmless in the event that matters: winning is good whenever the price (the third-highest bid) is below your value. The second-highest bid is irrelevant to the price, which raises the value of winning. Checking the first-order condition of the expected payoff under the candidate strategy confirms \(s(\theta) = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\theta\). Revenue: \(\frac{n-1}{n-2}\,\mathbb E[\theta_{(3)}] = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\cdot\frac{n-2}{n+1} = \frac{n-1}{n+1}\), the same as first- and second-price auctions, as revenue equivalence predicts.

(T 14.1, Brothers) Brother 1 ranks \(A \succ B \succ C\). Brother 2 is type \(\theta'\) (\(C \succ B \succ A\)) or \(\theta''\) (\(B \succ A \succ C\)). The parents ask brother 2 to report his type and implement \(f(\theta') = B\), \(f(\theta'') = A\). Is truth-telling dominant? What if \(f(\theta') = C\), \(f(\theta'') = A\)? Or \(f \equiv B\)?

Solution.

  1. Type \(\theta''\) gets \(A\) by telling the truth but \(B\), which he prefers, by lying. Not incentive compatible. (b) \(\theta'\) gets his favourite \(C\); \(\theta''\) gets \(A\) truthfully versus \(C\) by lying, and prefers \(A\). Truth-telling is dominant. (c) A constant choice is trivially incentive compatible, but it ignores the information. This is the revelation principle in miniature: a social choice function is implementable iff no type gains by imitating another.

From the course material:

Three bidders with values \(10\), \(7\), \(4\) participate in a second-price auction. What are the dominant-strategy outcome and the seller’s revenue? Show that bidder 2 cannot gain by bidding \(12\).

Solution. Truthful bids: bidder 1 wins and pays \(7\); revenue \(7\), bidder 1’s surplus \(3\). If bidder 2 bids \(12\), he wins and pays \(10 > 7 = v_2\): payoff \(-3 < 0\).

In a first-price auction with \(N\) bidders whose values are i.i.d. uniform on \([0,1]\), verify that \(b(v) = \frac{N-1}{N} v\) is a symmetric BNE and compute the expected revenue.

Solution. If the others bid \(\frac{N-1}{N} v_j\), bidding \(b\) wins iff all \(v_j < \frac{N}{N-1} b\), with probability \(\bigl(\frac{N b}{N-1}\bigr)^{N-1}\). Maximising \((v - b) b^{N-1}\) gives \((N-1)(v - b) = b\), i.e. \(b = \frac{N-1}{N} v\). Revenue: \(\frac{N-1}{N}\,\mathbb E[\max v] = \frac{N-1}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{N+1} = \frac{N-1}{N+1}\), equal to the expected second-highest value — revenue equivalence.

So far the game was given and we predicted its outcome. **Mechanism design** reverses the question: a designer wants a certain outcome — the good goes to whoever values it most, a protocol allocates bandwidth efficiently — and must *design the game* so that self-interested players with private information produce it in equilibrium. Auctions are the most important example, and the second-price auction shows that a well-designed game can make telling the truth a dominant strategy. The revelation principle then tells us where to look for good mechanisms.

## Sealed-bid auctions

::: {.definition name="Sealed-bid auction"}
One seller and $N$ bidders; bidder $i$ privately values the object $v_i \geq 0$. Each bidder submits a bid $b_i \geq 0$ without seeing the others. The highest bid wins; ties are broken by sharing (or by a coin flip).
:::

### First-price auction

The winner pays their own bid. With $W$ tied winners,
$$u_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - b_i & \text{if } i \text{ is the only highest bidder}, \\ (v_i - b_i)/W & \text{if } i \text{ is among } W > 1 \text{ highest bidders}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

- Bidding $b_i \geq v_i$ always yields $u_i \leq 0$, so it is weakly dominated by bidding below $v_i$.
- Bidding $b_i = 0$ wins only if everyone bids $0$, and is weakly dominated by a small positive bid.
- Between two bids $0 < b_i < b_i' < v_i$ nothing can be said in general: against opponents bidding $0$ the lower bid is better, against opponents bidding $(b_i + b_i')/2$ the higher one is.

Rational bidders **shade** their bids below their value. With incomplete information one computes the Bayesian Nash equilibrium: for two bidders with values independent and uniform on $[0, 1]$, the symmetric BNE is $b(v) = v/2$. Indeed, if the opponent bids $v_j/2$, bidding $b$ wins with probability $\Pr[v_j/2 < b] = 2b$, and $\max_b (v - b)\,2b$ gives $b = v/2$. (With $N$ bidders, $b(v) = \tfrac{N-1}{N} v$.)

### Second-price (Vickrey) auction

Proposed by Vickrey (1961): the highest bidder wins but pays the **second-highest** bid $S = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$:
$$u_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - S & \text{if } b_i > S, \\ (v_i - S)/W & \text{if } b_i = S \text{ with } W \text{ tied winners}, \\ 0 & \text{if } b_i < S. \end{cases}$$

::: {.theorem name="Truthful bidding is weakly dominant"}
In a second-price auction, bidding $b_i = v_i$ weakly dominates every other bid.
:::

*Proof.* Compare $v_i$ with a lower bid $b_i < v_i$, for each value of $S$:

- $S < b_i$: both bids win alone and pay $S$: same payoff $v_i - S$.
- $S = b_i$: $b_i$ ties and gets $(v_i - S)/W$; $v_i$ wins alone and gets $v_i - S > (v_i - S)/W$.
- $b_i < S < v_i$: $b_i$ loses ($0$), $v_i$ wins ($v_i - S > 0$).
- $S \geq v_i$: $b_i$ loses ($0$); $v_i$ loses or ties at zero surplus ($0$).

Now a higher bid $b_i > v_i$:

- $S < v_i$: both win and pay $S$: same payoff.
- $S = v_i$: both get $0$.
- $v_i < S$: $v_i$ loses ($0$); $b_i$ either loses ($0$) or wins/ties paying $S > v_i$ (negative payoff).

In every case $v_i$ is at least as good, and strictly better in some. $\square$

So $(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ is a Nash equilibrium (in dominant strategies — no beliefs needed), and the second-price auction makes bidders reveal their true values. In a first-price auction, by contrast, bidding $v_i$ always gives $0$ and is dominated.

**Efficiency.** Social surplus is maximised if the object goes to the bidder with the highest value. In the second-price auction this bidder wins and pays $S$: the bidder gets $v - S$, the seller $S$, total $v$ — Pareto efficient. For the first-price auction with symmetric bidders the BNE is also efficient (bids are increasing in values), and with independent private values the two formats yield the same expected revenue: for two uniform bidders, $\mathbb E[\max_i v_i]/2 = \tfrac13 = \mathbb E[\min_i v_i]$ (the **revenue equivalence theorem**).

## Revenue equivalence and reserve prices

First-price bidders shade their bids and second-price bidders do not, but the winner of a second-price auction pays only the second-highest value. Which raises more money? With $N$ bidders and i.i.d. values uniform on $[0, 1]$, both give expected revenue $\frac{N-1}{N+1}$. This is no coincidence:

::: {.theorem name="Revenue equivalence (Myerson, 1981; Riley and Samuelson, 1981)"}
With risk-neutral bidders and independent private values from a common continuous distribution, every auction format in which (i) the highest-value bidder always wins and (ii) a bidder with the lowest possible value expects zero payment yields the **same expected revenue** and the same expected payment for each type.
:::

The reason is the envelope theorem. A type's equilibrium utility grows with its value at a rate equal to its winning probability, which is fixed by (i). Payments are then pinned down by (ii). First-price, second-price, English, Dutch, all-pay and even third-price auctions (where bidders bid *above* their values) all raise $\frac{N-1}{N+1}$ in the uniform case. Revenue equivalence fails with risk-averse bidders (first-price then raises more, as shading is risky), with asymmetric bidders, and with correlated or common values.

**Reserve prices.** The seller can beat every efficient format by refusing to sell below a reserve $r$. With two uniform bidders and a second-price auction, revenue is $R(r) = \frac{1 + 3r^2 - 4r^3}{3}$, maximised at $r^* = \tfrac12$ with $R = \tfrac5{12} > \tfrac13$. The seller gives up some efficient trades (when both values are below $\tfrac12$) to extract more from a lone high bidder. In general $r^*$ solves $r - \frac{1 - F(r)}{f(r)} = 0$ (zero *virtual value*), independently of the number of bidders.

## Common values and the winner's curse

When the object has the same, unknown value for everyone (an oil tract, a spectrum licence), each bidder holds a noisy estimate. Winning is bad news: it means your estimate was the most optimistic. A bidder who bids as if their own estimate were the truth overpays systematically — the **winner's curse**. Revenue equivalence breaks down here: open ascending auctions, which reveal others' information as bidders drop out, raise more revenue than sealed bids (the *linkage principle*).

## Vickrey–Clarke–Groves auctions

A **VCG auction** extends the second-price idea to several items. Each bidder submits a value for every *combination* of items; the seller chooses the allocation maximising the total declared value (possibly a hard combinatorial problem); then each winner pays for the harm they cause to the others:
$$p_i = \underbrace{V(\mathcal N \setminus \{i\})}_{\substack{\text{others' best total value}\\ \text{without } i}} \;-\; \underbrace{V_{-i}(x^*)}_{\substack{\text{others' value in the}\\ \text{chosen allocation}}} .$$

::: {.example name="Two items, three bidders"}
A bids $5$ for a single item, B bids $4$ for a single item, C bids $7$ for the pair only. The optimal allocation gives one item to A and one to B (total $9 > 7$). Without A, the best the others could do is C taking both ($7$); in the chosen allocation the others get $4$ (B). So A pays $7 - 4 = 3$. Symmetrically B pays $7 - 5 = 2$, and C, who wins nothing and harms nobody, pays $0$.
:::

VCG mechanisms are truthful: bidding one's true valuations is weakly dominant, for the same reason as in the Vickrey auction — a bidder's report affects only the allocation, never the price conditional on the allocation, and the allocation maximises the bidder's own utility plus a term they cannot affect. Yet VCG is rare in practice: revenue can be low (even zero), it is non-monotone (adding bidders can lower revenue), and it is vulnerable to collusion and shill bidding.

## Mechanism design

Players $1, \dots, n$ must choose among public alternatives $X$. Their preferences depend on private types $t = (t_1, \dots, t_n) \in T = T_1 \times \dots \times T_n$, the **state of the world**; types are private, the prior is common knowledge. Payoffs are quasi-linear in money,
$$v_i(x, t_i, m_i) = u_i(x, t_i) + m_i ,$$
where $m_i$ is a monetary transfer.

::: {.definition name="Choice rule"}
A **choice rule** is a map $f : T \to Y$, with $Y = \{(x, m_1, \dots, m_n) : x \in X,\ \sum_i m_i \leq 0\}$. It consists of a **decision rule** $x(t)$ and a **transfer rule** $m(t)$; the budget constraint says the designer does not inject money.
:::

The designer would like to implement $f$, but does not know $t$, and players might lie.

::: {.definition name="Mechanism and implementation"}
A **mechanism** $\Gamma = (A_1, \dots, A_n; g)$ specifies an action set $A_i$ for each player and an outcome function $g : A_1 \times \dots \times A_n \to Y$. In the induced Bayesian game a pure strategy is $s_i : T_i \to A_i$. The mechanism **implements** $f$ (in BNE) if it has a Bayesian Nash equilibrium $s^*$ with $g(s^*(t)) = f(t)$ for every $t \in T$.
:::

```{=latex}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small, line width=0.4pt, >=stealth]
  \node (T) at (0,0) {types $T$};
  \node (Y) at (5,0) {outcomes $Y$};
  \node (A) at (2.5,-1.6) {actions $A$};
  \draw[->] (T) -- node[above] {choice rule $f$} (Y);
  \draw[->] (T) -- node[below left] {$s^*$} (A);
  \draw[->] (A) -- node[below right] {$g$} (Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
```

*Figure: a mechanism implements $f$ if equilibrium play $s^*$ followed by the outcome function $g$ reproduces $f$.*

## The revelation principle

::: {.definition name="Direct revelation mechanism"}
A mechanism is a **direct revelation mechanism** if $A_i = T_i$ for every $i$ and $g = f$: the designer simply asks players to report their types and applies the choice rule. $f$ is **truthfully implementable** in BNE if truth-telling, $s_i^*(t_i) = t_i$ for all $i$ and $t_i$, is a BNE of its direct revelation mechanism.
:::

::: {.theorem name="Revelation principle"}
A choice rule $f$ is implementable by some mechanism if and only if it is truthfully implementable in Bayesian Nash equilibrium.
:::

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) The direct mechanism is a mechanism. ($\Rightarrow$) Let $\Gamma$ implement $f$ through the equilibrium $s^*$. Build a direct mechanism that, on reports $\hat t$, computes $g(s^*(\hat t))$: it "plays the equilibrium on behalf of" each player. If some type gained by misreporting $\hat t_i$, the same type would have gained in $\Gamma$ by playing $s_i^*(\hat t_i)$ instead of $s_i^*(t_i)$, contradicting that $s^*$ is an equilibrium. $\square$

The consequence is powerful: to know whether *any* clever mechanism can achieve $f$, it suffices to check whether truth-telling is an equilibrium of the direct one. If the direct mechanism fails, no other mechanism will work. VCG mechanisms are exactly direct mechanisms in which truth-telling is even dominant.

::: {.callout-tip}
In ICT, "mechanisms" are protocols: a scheduler asking users to declare their traffic demand, a spectrum auction, a crowdsensing platform paying for reported measurements. The revelation principle says one can restrict attention to protocols that ask for the private information directly and pay so that reporting it honestly is in each user's interest.
:::

## Exercises

Badia and Marchioro's handbook has no auction problems; these are from Tadelis (T), Chapters 13–14.

::: {.exercise}
**(T 13.2)** In a second-price auction with complete information (everyone knows all values), is bidding one's value still weakly dominant? Are there other NE?
:::

::: {.solution}
Yes: dominance compares payoffs profile by profile, so information about the others is irrelevant. But there are many other NE. For example, the bidder with the *lowest* value bids above the highest value and everyone else bids $0$. The low bidder wins at price $0$; anyone else could win only by outbidding, paying more than their value. Such equilibria are in weakly dominated strategies and are inefficient.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 13.3)** Two bidders with values in $\{0, 1, 2\}$ (equally likely, independent) face a second-price auction with reserve $r$: if only one bid reaches $r$ that bidder pays $r$; if none does, the good is not sold. (a) Is truthful bidding still dominant? (b)–(c) Expected revenue for $r = 0$ and $r = 1$? (d) The optimal $r$?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Yes: the reserve acts like an extra bid by the seller. (b) Revenue is the lower value: $\mathbb E[\min] = 1\cdot\tfrac39 + 2\cdot\tfrac19 = \tfrac59$. (c) With $r = 1$: if both values are $\geq 1$ (probability $4/9$), the minimum is paid, contributing $\tfrac59$; if exactly one is $\geq 1$ ($4/9$), that bidder pays $1$, contributing $\tfrac49$. Total $1$. (d) For $r \in (1, 2]$ revenue is $\tfrac19\cdot2 + \tfrac49 r$, maximised at $r = 2$: $\tfrac{10}9$, double the no-reserve revenue. (Above $2$ nothing sells.) The reserve extracts the full value from a lone high bidder, at the cost of never selling to two low ones.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 13.4)** Two bidders with values uniform on $[0, 1]$; second-price auction with reserve $r$. Show that $r = 0$ is not optimal and find the optimal $r$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Revenue: if both values exceed $r$ the seller gets $\min(v_1, v_2)$; if exactly one does, $r$. Integrating, $R(r) = \frac{1 + 3r^2 - 4r^3}{3}$. $R'(r) = 2r - 4r^2 > 0$ for $0 < r < \tfrac12$, so a small reserve raises revenue (the loss, not selling when both values are tiny, is second order). The optimum is $r^* = \tfrac12$ with $R = \tfrac5{12}$, versus $\tfrac13$ without a reserve; a simulation with $10^6$ draws gives $0.4167$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 13.5)** First-price auction, two bidders with values $4$ or $8$ (probability $\tfrac12$ each). Show that the low type bids $4$ and the high type mixes on $[4, 6]$; find the mixture and the seller's revenue, and compare with an English auction.
:::

::: {.solution}
A low type bidding below $4$ would be overbid by the other low type at no cost, so $b_L = 4$ (zero surplus). The high type wins against a low rival by bidding just above $4$, earning $8 - 4 = 4$ with probability $\tfrac12$, so its equilibrium payoff is $2$. A bid $b$ must give the same: $(8 - b)\bigl(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12F(b)\bigr) = 2$, so
$$F(b) = \frac{b - 4}{8 - b}, \qquad b \in [4, 6].$$
The support ends where $F = 1$, at $b = 6$. (Tadelis states "uniformly", but uniform bids are not optimal: e.g. $b = 5$ would give $2.25$.) Expected high bid $8 - 4\ln2 = 5.23$, expected maximum of two high bids $5.55$. Revenue: $\tfrac14\cdot4 + \tfrac12\cdot5.23 + \tfrac14\cdot5.55 = 5$. An English auction sells at the second-highest value: $\tfrac34\cdot4 + \tfrac14\cdot8 = 5$. Revenue equivalence holds even with discrete types here.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 13.6)** In a third-price auction ($n \geq 3$ bidders, values uniform on $[0, 1]$; the highest bid wins and pays the third-highest bid), verify that $s(\theta) = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\theta$ is a symmetric BNE, explain overbidding, and compute the revenue.
:::

::: {.solution}
Bidding above value is harmless in the event that matters: winning is good whenever the price (the third-highest bid) is below your value. The second-highest bid is irrelevant to the price, which raises the value of winning. Checking the first-order condition of the expected payoff under the candidate strategy confirms $s(\theta) = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\theta$. Revenue: $\frac{n-1}{n-2}\,\mathbb E[\theta_{(3)}] = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\cdot\frac{n-2}{n+1} = \frac{n-1}{n+1}$, the same as first- and second-price auctions, as revenue equivalence predicts.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 14.1, Brothers)** Brother 1 ranks $A \succ B \succ C$. Brother 2 is type $\theta'$ ($C \succ B \succ A$) or $\theta''$ ($B \succ A \succ C$). The parents ask brother 2 to report his type and implement $f(\theta') = B$, $f(\theta'') = A$. Is truth-telling dominant? What if $f(\theta') = C$, $f(\theta'') = A$? Or $f \equiv B$?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Type $\theta''$ gets $A$ by telling the truth but $B$, which he prefers, by lying. Not incentive compatible. (b) $\theta'$ gets his favourite $C$; $\theta''$ gets $A$ truthfully versus $C$ by lying, and prefers $A$. Truth-telling is dominant. (c) A constant choice is trivially incentive compatible, but it ignores the information. This is the revelation principle in miniature: a social choice function is implementable iff no type gains by imitating another.
:::

From the course material:

::: {.exercise}
Three bidders with values $10$, $7$, $4$ participate in a second-price auction. What are the dominant-strategy outcome and the seller's revenue? Show that bidder 2 cannot gain by bidding $12$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Truthful bids: bidder 1 wins and pays $7$; revenue $7$, bidder 1's surplus $3$. If bidder 2 bids $12$, he wins and pays $10 > 7 = v_2$: payoff $-3 < 0$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
In a first-price auction with $N$ bidders whose values are i.i.d. uniform on $[0,1]$, verify that $b(v) = \frac{N-1}{N} v$ is a symmetric BNE and compute the expected revenue.
:::

::: {.solution}
If the others bid $\frac{N-1}{N} v_j$, bidding $b$ wins iff all $v_j < \frac{N}{N-1} b$, with probability $\bigl(\frac{N b}{N-1}\bigr)^{N-1}$. Maximising $(v - b) b^{N-1}$ gives $(N-1)(v - b) = b$, i.e. $b = \frac{N-1}{N} v$. Revenue: $\frac{N-1}{N}\,\mathbb E[\max v] = \frac{N-1}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{N+1} = \frac{N-1}{N+1}$, equal to the expected second-highest value — revenue equivalence.
:::
