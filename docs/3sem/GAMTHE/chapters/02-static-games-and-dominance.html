2  Static games and dominance

The simplest genuinely multi-player interaction is a static game of complete information: everyone chooses once, without seeing the others’ choices, and everyone knows everyone’s payoffs. This chapter sets up the normal-form representation, meets the canonical examples that recur throughout the course, and develops the first two solution ideas — eliminating dominated strategies, and best-responding to beliefs.

2.1 Static games of complete information

  • Static: every player chooses an action without knowledge of the others’ actions. Moves need not be literally simultaneous — what matters is the information.
  • Complete information: every player’s payoff function is common knowledge.

Two physically different procedures are both “simultaneous”: (A) two players write their choices on opposite sides of a board at the same time; (B) player 1 writes first while player 2 is blindfolded, then the board is turned and player 2 writes. Only the information available at decision time matters.

A fact \(E\) is common knowledge if everybody knows \(E\), everybody knows that everybody knows \(E\), and so on ad infinitum.

Complete information means that all possible actions, all resulting outcomes and all players’ preferences over outcomes are common knowledge. We also assume that rationality is common knowledge: everybody maximises their payoff, everybody knows that everybody does, and so on. This is a powerful and far from innocuous assumption.

2.2 Normal-form representation

The ingredients of a static game are actions, outcomes and utilities. It is convenient to speak of strategies rather than actions: a strategy is a complete plan of action (“if these conditions hold, play \(a\); otherwise \(a'\)”). For now we need only deterministic plans.

A pure strategy is a deterministic plan of action. A game in normal form is \[G = \{S_1, \dots, S_n;\ u_1, \dots, u_n\},\] where \(S_i\) is the set of pure strategies of player \(i\) and \(u_i : S_1 \times \dots \times S_n \to \mathbb R\) is the payoff of player \(i\) for each strategy profile \(s = (s_1, \dots, s_n)\).

With two players and finite strategy sets, \(G\) is a bi-matrix: rows are player 1’s strategies, columns player 2’s, and each cell contains the pair \((u_1, u_2)\).

2.4 Pareto efficiency

A profile \(s\) is Pareto dominated by \(s'\) if \(u_i(s') \geq u_i(s)\) for every player \(i\) and \(u_i(s') > u_i(s)\) for at least one. A profile not Pareto dominated by any other is Pareto efficient (Pareto optimal).

There are usually several Pareto-efficient profiles, none dominating the others. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, \((M,M)\), \((M,F)\) and \((F,M)\) are Pareto efficient while \((F,F)\) is not — it is dominated by \((M,M)\).

2.5 Strict dominance and IESDS

In \(G = \{S_1, \dots, S_n; u_1, \dots, u_n\}\), strategy \(s_i \in S_i\) is strictly dominated by \(s_i' \in S_i\) if \[u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i} = \textstyle\prod_{j \neq i} S_j .\]

A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy: whatever the others do, there is something strictly better. If rationality is common knowledge, everybody can delete everybody’s dominated strategies, and the deletion can be iterated on the smaller game.

Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies repeatedly removes, for every player, the strategies that are strictly dominated in the game that survives the previous rounds, until no more can be removed.

\(D\) is strictly dominated by \(M\) (\(1 > 0\), \(4 > 2\)). With \(D\) gone, \(L\) is strictly dominated by \(R\) for player B (\(5 > 0\), \(3 > 0\)). With \(L\) gone, \(U\) is strictly dominated by \(M\) (\(4 > 0\)). The unique survivor is \((M, R)\) with payoffs \((4, 3)\).

\(F\) strictly dominates \(M\) for both players (\(0 > -1\) and \(-6 > -9\)). IESDS leaves only \((F,F)\), which is Pareto dominated by \((M,M)\). Individually rational behaviour produces a collectively bad outcome — hence the “dilemma”.

Remark (Order irrelevance). For strict dominance in finite games, the final result of IESDS does not depend on the order in which strategies are removed. (This fails for weak dominance, see Chapter 3.)

IESDS often fails to say anything. In Example 2 nothing is dominated, yet \((D,R)\) looks compelling. In Odds & Evens neither strategy dominates the other. In the Battle of the Sexes both \((R,R)\) and \((S,S)\) look sensible but nothing can be removed. We need a sharper concept.

2.6 Best responses and beliefs

For single-agent problems, the solution follows from the setup. In games it depends on what the others do — so we make the dependence explicit.

Strategy \(s_i \in S_i\) is a best response to \(s_{-i} \in S_{-i}\) if \[u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, s_i' \in S_i .\]

There may be several best responses, all with the same payoff. In the game

L C R
U 3, 3 5, 1 6, 2
M 4, 1 8, 4 3, 6
D 4, 0 9, 6 6, 8

both \(U\) and \(D\) are best responses of player A to \(R\) (payoff \(6\)), and both \(M\) and \(D\) to \(L\) (payoff \(4\)).

A belief of player \(i\) is a profile of opponents’ strategies \(s_{-i} \in S_{-i}\) that \(i\) conjectures will be played. The best-response correspondence \(BR_i : S_{-i} \rightrightarrows S_i\) maps each belief to the set of best responses to it. It is single-valued when the best response is unique.

A rational player who holds belief \(s_{-i}\) always picks some \(s_i \in BR_i(s_{-i})\).

If \(s_i\) is strictly dominated, it is not a best response to any \(s_{-i} \in S_{-i}\).

Proof. If \(s_i'\) strictly dominates \(s_i\), then \(u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\) for every \(s_{-i}\), violating the best-response inequality. \(\square\)

Iterating “never a best response” instead of “strictly dominated” gives rationalizability: the strategies that survive are those a rational player could play for some belief consistent with common knowledge of rationality. For two players the two procedures coincide once mixed strategies are allowed (Chapter 5).

Dominance arguments only use the first half of what we will need: that each player best-responds to some belief. Nash equilibrium adds the second half — that beliefs are correct.

2.7 Rationalizability and common knowledge

The solution concepts of this chapter rest on two assumptions with different strength.

A fact \(E\) is common knowledge if everybody knows \(E\), everybody knows that everybody knows \(E\), and so on ad infinitum.

A game of complete information requires the players, strategy sets and payoffs to be common knowledge. Rationality alone justifies deleting strictly dominated strategies once; each further round of IESDS requires one more level of “I know that you know that I am rational”. IESDS to the end needs common knowledge of rationality.

\(s_i^D\) is a strictly dominant strategy if it strictly dominates every other \(s_i \in S_i\). If every player has one, \(s^D = (s_1^D, \dots, s_n^D)\) is a strictly dominant strategy equilibrium; it is unique, and it is the only profile surviving IESDS.

The Prisoner’s Dilemma has one; most games do not. It needs only rationality, not common knowledge — which makes it the most robust prediction, when it exists.

2.7.1 Beliefs as probabilities

A player who is unsure what others will do holds a probabilistic belief \(\pi_i \in \Delta(S_{-i})\) and maximises expected payoff \(\sum_{s_{-i}}\pi_i(s_{-i})\,u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\). A strategy is a never-best response if it is not optimal for any such belief. Strict dominance by a pure strategy is sufficient, but not necessary:

L R
U 3 0
M 0 3
D 1 1

\(D\) (row player’s payoffs shown) is dominated by neither \(U\) nor \(M\), yet it is never a best response: against belief \(\Pr(L) = q\) it earns \(1 < \max(3q, 3 - 3q)\), which is at least \(1.5\). Equivalently, \(D\) is strictly dominated by the mixed strategy \(\tfrac12U + \tfrac12M\), which earns \(1.5\) against both columns.

In a finite two-player game, a strategy is a never-best response to all beliefs if and only if it is strictly dominated by some (possibly mixed) strategy.

Rationalizability iterates the deletion of never-best responses. By the theorem, in two-player games it coincides with IESDS when mixed dominators are allowed; with more players (correlated beliefs aside) it can be finer. Either way, it is the set of strategies a player can justify by an infinite chain of beliefs, each consistent with the rationality of the others.

2.7.2 IESDS on a continuum: two classic examples

Cournot duopoly (studied in Chapter 4): firms choose \(q_i \geq 0\), price \(a - q_1 - q_2\), unit cost \(c\); the best response is \(q_i = \frac{a - c - q_j}{2}\). Quantities above the monopoly output \(\frac{a-c}{2}\) are dominated. Given that, quantities below \(\frac{a-c}{4}\) are dominated, and so on. The surviving intervals shrink geometrically around \(\frac{a-c}{3}\), so IESDS alone pins down the Cournot outcome.

The \(p\)-beauty contest: \(n\) players pick a number in \([0, 100]\); whoever is closest to \(p = 2/3\) of the average wins. No number above \(66.\overline{6}\) can be a best response; given that, nothing above \(44.\overline{4}\); after \(k\) rounds, nothing above \(100(2/3)^k\). Only \(0\) survives. In experiments the average is around \(20\)–\(35\): subjects behave like level-\(k\) thinkers, performing one to three rounds of reasoning. Common knowledge of rationality is a strong assumption, and the “right” play depends on how rational you believe the others are.

2.8 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Game Theory: A Handbook of Problems and Exercises, Chapters 1 and 4. The equilibrium parts of these problems are in Chapters 3 and 5.

(BM 1.2) Mr. Square (S) and Ms. Triangle (T) each pick a point. Both get revenue \(10\) if the points differ and \(0\) if they coincide, minus a cost \(x + 3y\) for the chosen point \((x, y)\). S chooses among A \((0,0)\), B \((0,4)\), C \((4,4)\), D \((4,0)\); T among A \((0,0)\), E \((2,4)\), D \((4,0)\). Write the normal form and remove the dominated strategies.

Solution.

A D E
A 0, 0 10, 6 10, −4
B −2, 10 −2, 6 −2, −4
C −6, 10 −6, 6 −6, −4
D 6, 10 −4, −4 6, −4

Points with high \(y\) are expensive and never worth it. \(B\) and \(C\) are strictly dominated by \(A\) for S, and \(E\) by \(A\) for T. What remains is the \(2\times2\) anti-coordination game on \(\{A, D\}\), where nothing more is dominated.

(BM 1.5) Alice and Bob each create a character: Human (H), Elf (E), Dwarf (D) or Orc (O), costing \(0\), \(5\), \(4\), \(3\). Humans give nothing to the partner; Orcs give \(-3\); Dwarves give \(+5\); Elves give \(+15\), but only to an Elf partner. Alice gets an extra \(+5\) if she is an Orc and Bob is not. Write the normal form and apply IESDS.

Solution.

H E D O
H 0, 0 0, −5 5, −4 −3, −3
E −5, 0 10, 10 0, −4 −8, −3
D −4, 5 −4, 0 1, 1 −7, 2
O 2, −3 2, −8 2, −7 −6, −6

\(D\) is strictly dominated by \(H\) for both players (a Dwarf pays \(4\) to help the other). Then \(O\) is dominated by \(H\) for Bob (\(0 > -3\), \(0 > -8\), \(-3 > -6\)). Against \(\{H, E\}\), Alice’s \(H\) is dominated by \(O\) (\(2 > 0\), \(2 > 0\)). The survivors are \(\{E, O\} \times \{H, E\}\), where nothing else is dominated.

(BM 1.6) Two teams choose Aggressive, Spectacular or Relaxed. Aggressive always gives \(1\); Spectacular gives \(3\) and adds \(1\) to the opponent; Relaxed gives \(0\), unless both relax, in which case both get \(5\). Write the normal form and simplify it.

Solution.

A S R
A 1, 1 2, 3 1, 0
S 3, 2 4, 4 3, 1
R 0, 1 1, 3 5, 5

\(A\) is strictly dominated by \(S\) (\(3 > 1\), \(4 > 2\), \(3 > 1\)). The reduced game on \(\{S, R\}\) is a coordination game (a Stag Hunt: \((R, R)\) is Pareto dominant but risky), so no further elimination is possible.

(BM 4.5) Two knights choose Axe, Mace or Sword. Same weapon: White wins with probability \(0.6\). Axe against Mace: Axe wins with \(0.9\). Black with the Sword (White without): Black wins with \(0.8\). White with the Sword (Black without): White wins with \(0.7\). Winning is worth \(10\), dying \(-100\). Write the expected payoffs and solve by IESDS.

Solution. With winning probability \(\pi\) the expected utility is \(10\pi - 100(1 - \pi) = 110\pi - 100\) (White’s payoff first, White chooses the row):

A M S
A −34, −56 −1, −89 −78, −12
M −89, −1 −34, −56 −78, −12
S −23, −67 −23, −67 −34, −56

Every cell sums to \(-90\): a constant-sum game. \(M\) is strictly dominated by \(S\) for both. In the remaining \(\{A, S\}\) game \(A\) is dominated by \(S\) for both. IESDS leaves \((S, S)\), the unique NE.

(BM 4.4) Two friends choose a course, G, H or I. If they choose different courses both get \(0\). If they choose the same course \(j\), it turns out good with probability \(\rho_G = 0.4\), \(\rho_H = 0.8\), \(\rho_I = 0.5\). A good course gives Anastasia \(1\) and Bruno \(3\), \(2\), \(-1\) for G, H, I; a bad course gives \(0\). Write the normal form and apply IESDS, allowing domination by mixed strategies.

Solution.

G H I
G 0.4, 1.2 0, 0 0, 0
H 0, 0 0.8, 1.6 0, 0
I 0, 0 0, 0 0.5, −0.5

No pure strategy strictly dominates Bruno’s \(I\): its payoffs \((0, 0, -0.5)\) are only weakly below those of \(G\) and \(H\). The mixture \(\tfrac12G + \tfrac12H\) gives \((0.6, 0.8, 0) > (0, 0, -0.5)\), so \(I\) is strictly dominated. With \(I\) gone, Anastasia’s \(I\) earns \((0, 0)\) against \((G, H)\), strictly below \(\tfrac12G + \tfrac12H = (0.2, 0.4)\). The survivors are \(\{G, H\}^2\): a coordination game. This is Pearce’s theorem at work: these strategies are never best responses.

(BM 1.7) Two firms assign \(0, 1, 2, \dots\) employees (\(100\) k€ each) to a project. Equal staffing pays each \(140\); otherwise the larger team gets \(350\) and the smaller \(160\). (a) Show that nobody assigns more than 2. (b) Write the \(3\times3\) game. (c) Is \(2\) strictly dominated by a mixture of \(0\) and \(1\)?

Solution.

  1. Assigning \(0\) guarantees at least \(140\), while \(k \geq 3\) employees yield at most \(350 - 300 = 50\). (b) Row firm’s payoff (the game is symmetric):
0 1 2
0 140 160 160
1 250 40 60
2 150 150 −60
  1. No. A mixture \(p\cdot0 + (1-p)\cdot1\) beats \(2\) against column \(0\) iff \(140p + 250(1 - p) > 150\), i.e. \(p < 0.909\), and against column \(1\) iff \(160p + 40(1 - p) > 150\), i.e. \(p > 0.917\): incompatible. So \(2\) survives IESDS. (The printed solution claims \(0.95\cdot0 + 0.05\cdot1\) dominates \(2\); it fails against column \(0\), \(145.5 < 150\).) This matters for Chapter 5: \(2\) appears in the support of a mixed equilibrium.

(BM 4.2) Two hunters each choose a Bear, Deer or Wolf. Choosing the same prey scares it away (both get \(0\)). A lone hunter succeeds with probability \(\rho_B = 0.4\), \(\rho_D = 0.9\), \(\rho_W = 0.6\), earning \(+1\), and is otherwise killed (\(-1\)). Write the normal form and eliminate dominated strategies.

Solution. A lone hunt of \(j\) is worth \(2\rho_j - 1\): \(-0.2\), \(0.8\), \(0.2\).

B D W
B 0, 0 −0.2, 0.8 −0.2, 0.2
D 0.8, −0.2 0, 0 0.8, 0.2
W 0.2, −0.2 0.2, 0.8 0, 0

\(B\) is strictly dominated by \(D\) (and by \(W\)) for both hunters. The reduced game on \(\{D, W\}\) is an anti-coordination game, with nothing further dominated.

## Normal-form representation

The ingredients of a static game are *actions*, *outcomes* and *utilities*. It is convenient to speak of **strategies** rather than actions: a strategy is a complete plan of action ("if these conditions hold, play $a$; otherwise $a'$"). For now we need only deterministic plans.

::: {.definition name="Pure strategy and normal form"}
A **pure strategy** is a deterministic plan of action. A game in **normal form** is
$$G = \{S_1, \dots, S_n;\ u_1, \dots, u_n\},$$
where $S_i$ is the set of pure strategies of player $i$ and $u_i : S_1 \times \dots \times S_n \to \mathbb R$ is the payoff of player $i$ for each **strategy profile** $s = (s_1, \dots, s_n)$.
:::

With two players and finite strategy sets, $G$ is a **bi-matrix**: rows are player 1's strategies, columns player 2's, and each cell contains the pair $(u_1, u_2)$.

## Pareto efficiency

::: {.definition name="Pareto dominance and efficiency"}
A profile $s$ is **Pareto dominated** by $s'$ if $u_i(s') \geq u_i(s)$ for every player $i$ and $u_i(s') > u_i(s)$ for at least one. A profile not Pareto dominated by any other is **Pareto efficient** (Pareto optimal).
:::

There are usually several Pareto-efficient profiles, none dominating the others. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, $(M,M)$, $(M,F)$ and $(F,M)$ are Pareto efficient while $(F,F)$ is not — it is dominated by $(M,M)$.

## Strict dominance and IESDS

::: {.definition name="Strictly dominated strategy"}
In $G = \{S_1, \dots, S_n; u_1, \dots, u_n\}$, strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is **strictly dominated** by $s_i' \in S_i$ if
$$u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i} = \textstyle\prod_{j \neq i} S_j .$$
:::

A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy: whatever the others do, there is something strictly better. If rationality is common knowledge, everybody can delete everybody's dominated strategies, and the deletion can be iterated on the smaller game.

::: {.definition name="IESDS"}
**Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies** repeatedly removes, for every player, the strategies that are strictly dominated in the game that survives the previous rounds, until no more can be removed.
:::

::: {.example name="IESDS in Example 1"}
$D$ is strictly dominated by $M$ ($1 > 0$, $4 > 2$). With $D$ gone, $L$ is strictly dominated by $R$ for player B ($5 > 0$, $3 > 0$). With $L$ gone, $U$ is strictly dominated by $M$ ($4 > 0$). The unique survivor is $(M, R)$ with payoffs $(4, 3)$.
:::

::: {.example name="The Prisoner's Dilemma"}
$F$ strictly dominates $M$ for both players ($0 > -1$ and $-6 > -9$). IESDS leaves only $(F,F)$, which is Pareto dominated by $(M,M)$. Individually rational behaviour produces a collectively bad outcome — hence the "dilemma".
:::

::: {.remark name="Order irrelevance"}
For strict dominance in finite games, the final result of IESDS does not depend on the order in which strategies are removed. (This fails for weak dominance, see Chapter 3.)
:::

IESDS often fails to say anything. In Example 2 nothing is dominated, yet $(D,R)$ looks compelling. In Odds & Evens neither strategy dominates the other. In the Battle of the Sexes both $(R,R)$ and $(S,S)$ look sensible but nothing can be removed. We need a sharper concept.

## Best responses and beliefs

For single-agent problems, the solution follows from the setup. In games it depends on what the others do — so we make the dependence explicit.

::: {.definition name="Best response"}
Strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is a **best response** to $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ if
$$u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, s_i' \in S_i .$$
:::

There may be several best responses, all with the same payoff. In the game

| | L | C | R |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **U** | 3, 3 | 5, 1 | 6, 2 |
| **M** | 4, 1 | 8, 4 | 3, 6 |
| **D** | 4, 0 | 9, 6 | 6, 8 |

both $U$ and $D$ are best responses of player A to $R$ (payoff $6$), and both $M$ and $D$ to $L$ (payoff $4$).

::: {.definition name="Belief and best-response correspondence"}
A **belief** of player $i$ is a profile of opponents' strategies $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ that $i$ conjectures will be played. The **best-response correspondence** $BR_i : S_{-i} \rightrightarrows S_i$ maps each belief to the set of best responses to it. It is single-valued when the best response is unique.
:::

A rational player who holds belief $s_{-i}$ always picks some $s_i \in BR_i(s_{-i})$.

::: {.proposition name="Dominated strategies are never best responses"}
If $s_i$ is strictly dominated, it is not a best response to any $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$.
:::

*Proof.* If $s_i'$ strictly dominates $s_i$, then $u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ for every $s_{-i}$, violating the best-response inequality. $\square$

Iterating "never a best response" instead of "strictly dominated" gives **rationalizability**: the strategies that survive are those a rational player could play for *some* belief consistent with common knowledge of rationality. For two players the two procedures coincide once mixed strategies are allowed (Chapter 5).

::: {.callout-note}
Dominance arguments only use the first half of what we will need: that each player best-responds to *some* belief. Nash equilibrium adds the second half — that beliefs are *correct*.
:::

## Rationalizability and common knowledge

The solution concepts of this chapter rest on two assumptions with different strength.

::: {.definition name="Common knowledge"}
A fact $E$ is **common knowledge** if everybody knows $E$, everybody knows that everybody knows $E$, and so on *ad infinitum*.
:::

A game of complete information requires the players, strategy sets and payoffs to be common knowledge. *Rationality* alone justifies deleting strictly dominated strategies **once**; each further round of IESDS requires one more level of "I know that you know that I am rational". IESDS to the end needs **common knowledge of rationality**.

::: {.definition name="Dominant-strategy equilibrium"}
$s_i^D$ is a **strictly dominant** strategy if it strictly dominates every other $s_i \in S_i$. If every player has one, $s^D = (s_1^D, \dots, s_n^D)$ is a **strictly dominant strategy equilibrium**; it is unique, and it is the only profile surviving IESDS.
:::

The Prisoner's Dilemma has one; most games do not. It needs only rationality, not common knowledge — which makes it the most robust prediction, when it exists.

### Beliefs as probabilities

A player who is unsure what others will do holds a **probabilistic belief** $\pi_i \in \Delta(S_{-i})$ and maximises expected payoff $\sum_{s_{-i}}\pi_i(s_{-i})\,u_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. A strategy is a **never-best response** if it is not optimal for *any* such belief. Strict dominance by a pure strategy is sufficient, but not necessary:

| | L | R |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **U** | 3 | 0 |
| **M** | 0 | 3 |
| **D** | 1 | 1 |

$D$ (row player's payoffs shown) is dominated by neither $U$ nor $M$, yet it is never a best response: against belief $\Pr(L) = q$ it earns $1 < \max(3q, 3 - 3q)$, which is at least $1.5$. Equivalently, $D$ is strictly dominated by the *mixed* strategy $\tfrac12U + \tfrac12M$, which earns $1.5$ against both columns.

::: {.theorem name="Pearce (1984)"}
In a finite two-player game, a strategy is a never-best response to all beliefs if and only if it is strictly dominated by some (possibly mixed) strategy.
:::

**Rationalizability** iterates the deletion of never-best responses. By the theorem, in two-player games it coincides with IESDS when mixed dominators are allowed; with more players (correlated beliefs aside) it can be finer. Either way, it is the set of strategies a player can justify by an infinite chain of beliefs, each consistent with the rationality of the others.

### IESDS on a continuum: two classic examples

*Cournot duopoly* (studied in Chapter 4): firms choose $q_i \geq 0$, price $a - q_1 - q_2$, unit cost $c$; the best response is $q_i = \frac{a - c - q_j}{2}$. Quantities above the monopoly output $\frac{a-c}{2}$ are dominated. Given that, quantities below $\frac{a-c}{4}$ are dominated, and so on. The surviving intervals shrink geometrically around $\frac{a-c}{3}$, so IESDS alone pins down the Cournot outcome.

*The $p$-beauty contest:* $n$ players pick a number in $[0, 100]$; whoever is closest to $p = 2/3$ of the average wins. No number above $66.\overline{6}$ can be a best response; given that, nothing above $44.\overline{4}$; after $k$ rounds, nothing above $100(2/3)^k$. Only $0$ survives. In experiments the average is around $20$–$35$: subjects behave like **level-$k$ thinkers**, performing one to three rounds of reasoning. Common knowledge of rationality is a strong assumption, and the "right" play depends on how rational you believe the *others* are.

## Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), *Game Theory: A Handbook of Problems and Exercises*, Chapters 1 and 4. The equilibrium parts of these problems are in Chapters 3 and 5.

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.2)** Mr. Square (S) and Ms. Triangle (T) each pick a point. Both get revenue $10$ if the points differ and $0$ if they coincide, minus a cost $x + 3y$ for the chosen point $(x, y)$. S chooses among A $(0,0)$, B $(0,4)$, C $(4,4)$, D $(4,0)$; T among A $(0,0)$, E $(2,4)$, D $(4,0)$. Write the normal form and remove the dominated strategies.
:::

::: {.solution}
| | A | D | E |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **A** | 0, 0 | 10, 6 | 10, −4 |
| **B** | −2, 10 | −2, 6 | −2, −4 |
| **C** | −6, 10 | −6, 6 | −6, −4 |
| **D** | 6, 10 | −4, −4 | 6, −4 |

Points with high $y$ are expensive and never worth it. $B$ and $C$ are strictly dominated by $A$ for S, and $E$ by $A$ for T. What remains is the $2\times2$ anti-coordination game on $\{A, D\}$, where nothing more is dominated.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.5)** Alice and Bob each create a character: Human (H), Elf (E), Dwarf (D) or Orc (O), costing $0$, $5$, $4$, $3$. Humans give nothing to the partner; Orcs give $-3$; Dwarves give $+5$; Elves give $+15$, but only to an Elf partner. Alice gets an extra $+5$ if she is an Orc and Bob is not. Write the normal form and apply IESDS.
:::

::: {.solution}
| | H | E | D | O |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **H** | 0, 0 | 0, −5 | 5, −4 | −3, −3 |
| **E** | −5, 0 | 10, 10 | 0, −4 | −8, −3 |
| **D** | −4, 5 | −4, 0 | 1, 1 | −7, 2 |
| **O** | 2, −3 | 2, −8 | 2, −7 | −6, −6 |

$D$ is strictly dominated by $H$ for both players (a Dwarf pays $4$ to help the *other*). Then $O$ is dominated by $H$ for Bob ($0 > -3$, $0 > -8$, $-3 > -6$). Against $\{H, E\}$, Alice's $H$ is dominated by $O$ ($2 > 0$, $2 > 0$). The survivors are $\{E, O\} \times \{H, E\}$, where nothing else is dominated.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.6)** Two teams choose Aggressive, Spectacular or Relaxed. Aggressive always gives $1$; Spectacular gives $3$ and adds $1$ to the opponent; Relaxed gives $0$, unless both relax, in which case both get $5$. Write the normal form and simplify it.
:::

::: {.solution}
| | A | S | R |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **A** | 1, 1 | 2, 3 | 1, 0 |
| **S** | 3, 2 | 4, 4 | 3, 1 |
| **R** | 0, 1 | 1, 3 | 5, 5 |

$A$ is strictly dominated by $S$ ($3 > 1$, $4 > 2$, $3 > 1$). The reduced game on $\{S, R\}$ is a coordination game (a Stag Hunt: $(R, R)$ is Pareto dominant but risky), so no further elimination is possible.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.5)** Two knights choose Axe, Mace or Sword. Same weapon: White wins with probability $0.6$. Axe against Mace: Axe wins with $0.9$. Black with the Sword (White without): Black wins with $0.8$. White with the Sword (Black without): White wins with $0.7$. Winning is worth $10$, dying $-100$. Write the expected payoffs and solve by IESDS.
:::

::: {.solution}
With winning probability $\pi$ the expected utility is $10\pi - 100(1 - \pi) = 110\pi - 100$ (White's payoff first, White chooses the row):

| | A | M | S |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **A** | −34, −56 | −1, −89 | −78, −12 |
| **M** | −89, −1 | −34, −56 | −78, −12 |
| **S** | −23, −67 | −23, −67 | −34, −56 |

Every cell sums to $-90$: a constant-sum game. $M$ is strictly dominated by $S$ for both. In the remaining $\{A, S\}$ game $A$ is dominated by $S$ for both. IESDS leaves $(S, S)$, the unique NE.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.4)** Two friends choose a course, G, H or I. If they choose different courses both get $0$. If they choose the same course $j$, it turns out good with probability $\rho_G = 0.4$, $\rho_H = 0.8$, $\rho_I = 0.5$. A good course gives Anastasia $1$ and Bruno $3$, $2$, $-1$ for G, H, I; a bad course gives $0$. Write the normal form and apply IESDS, allowing domination by mixed strategies.
:::

::: {.solution}
| | G | H | I |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **G** | 0.4, 1.2 | 0, 0 | 0, 0 |
| **H** | 0, 0 | 0.8, 1.6 | 0, 0 |
| **I** | 0, 0 | 0, 0 | 0.5, −0.5 |

No pure strategy strictly dominates Bruno's $I$: its payoffs $(0, 0, -0.5)$ are only weakly below those of $G$ and $H$. The mixture $\tfrac12G + \tfrac12H$ gives $(0.6, 0.8, 0) > (0, 0, -0.5)$, so $I$ is strictly dominated. With $I$ gone, Anastasia's $I$ earns $(0, 0)$ against $(G, H)$, strictly below $\tfrac12G + \tfrac12H = (0.2, 0.4)$. The survivors are $\{G, H\}^2$: a coordination game. This is Pearce's theorem at work: these strategies are never best responses.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.7)** Two firms assign $0, 1, 2, \dots$ employees ($100$ k€ each) to a project. Equal staffing pays each $140$; otherwise the larger team gets $350$ and the smaller $160$. (a) Show that nobody assigns more than 2. (b) Write the $3\times3$ game. (c) Is $2$ strictly dominated by a mixture of $0$ and $1$?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Assigning $0$ guarantees at least $140$, while $k \geq 3$ employees yield at most $350 - 300 = 50$. (b) Row firm's payoff (the game is symmetric):

| | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **0** | 140 | 160 | 160 |
| **1** | 250 | 40 | 60 |
| **2** | 150 | 150 | −60 |

(c) No. A mixture $p\cdot0 + (1-p)\cdot1$ beats $2$ against column $0$ iff $140p + 250(1 - p) > 150$, i.e. $p < 0.909$, and against column $1$ iff $160p + 40(1 - p) > 150$, i.e. $p > 0.917$: incompatible. So $2$ survives IESDS. (The printed solution claims $0.95\cdot0 + 0.05\cdot1$ dominates $2$; it fails against column $0$, $145.5 < 150$.) This matters for Chapter 5: $2$ appears in the support of a mixed equilibrium.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.2)** Two hunters each choose a Bear, Deer or Wolf. Choosing the same prey scares it away (both get $0$). A lone hunter succeeds with probability $\rho_B = 0.4$, $\rho_D = 0.9$, $\rho_W = 0.6$, earning $+1$, and is otherwise killed ($-1$). Write the normal form and eliminate dominated strategies.
:::

::: {.solution}
A lone hunt of $j$ is worth $2\rho_j - 1$: $-0.2$, $0.8$, $0.2$.

| | B | D | W |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **B** | 0, 0 | −0.2, 0.8 | −0.2, 0.2 |
| **D** | 0.8, −0.2 | 0, 0 | 0.8, 0.2 |
| **W** | 0.2, −0.2 | 0.2, 0.8 | 0, 0 |

$B$ is strictly dominated by $D$ (and by $W$) for both hunters. The reduced game on $\{D, W\}$ is an anti-coordination game, with nothing further dominated.
:::
