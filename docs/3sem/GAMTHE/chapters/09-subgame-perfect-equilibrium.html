9  Credibility and subgame perfection

Nash equilibrium can be applied to dynamic games through their normal form, but it misses something essential: it treats a strategy as a commitment made before the game starts, so it accepts threats that the threatening player would never carry out when the moment comes. This chapter identifies the problem, solves perfect-information games by backward induction, and introduces Selten’s refinement — subgame-perfect equilibrium — which rules out non-credible threats. Stackelberg leader–follower games close the chapter.

9.1 Nash equilibria of dynamic games

The normal form of the sequential Battle of the Sexes (Chapter 8) has three pure Nash equilibria:

  • \((R, rr)\): Ann goes to R; Brian “always goes to R”;
  • \((R, rs)\): Ann goes to R; Brian “does what Ann does”;
  • \((S, ss)\): Ann goes to S; Brian “always goes to S”.

These strategies are chosen as if Brian moved first. \((R, rr)\) and \((R, rs)\) produce the same outcome: they coincide on the equilibrium path but differ off it. Out of equilibrium, \(rs\) is smarter; \(rr\) prescribes the irrational reply \(r\) to \(S\) — which never comes into play.

Given a Nash equilibrium in behavioural strategies, its equilibrium path is the set of nodes reached with positive probability.

Why specify behaviour off the path at all? Because equilibria are sustained by beliefs about what others would do both on and off the path. \((S, ss)\) is a NE because Ann’s deviation to \(R\) is “punished”: Brian threatens to go to S anyway. But is the threat credible? Once Ann is at the romance movie, Brian’s best reply is \(r\).

A proposer P splits 10 candies: \(D\) (9 for P, 1 for R), \(F\) (5–5) or \(G\) (2–8). The responder R accepts (\(Y\)) or refuses (\(N\)), in which case both get nothing. A strategy of R is a triple \((x_D, x_F, x_G)\), e.g. “accept \(D\) and \(F\) but not \(G\)”. Every pair “P offers \(x/10 - x\)” with “R refuses whenever P’s share exceeds \(x\)” is a Nash equilibrium — the responder’s threat makes many splits stable.

9.2 Backward induction

In a dynamic game of perfect information every information set is a singleton. Take two players: player 1 chooses \(a_1 \in A_1\); player 2 observes \(a_1\) and chooses \(a_2 \in A_2(a_1)\); payoffs are \(u_1(a_1, a_2)\), \(u_2(a_1, a_2)\). When it is their turn, player 2 knows exactly where they are and solves \[R_2(a_1) = \arg\max_{a_2 \in A_2(a_1)} u_2(a_1, a_2).\] Thanks to complete information, player 1 anticipates this reaction and solves \(\max_{a_1} u_1(a_1, R_2(a_1))\). The resulting outcome \((a_1^*, R_2(a_1^*))\) is a Nash equilibrium in pure strategies.

Every finite game of perfect information has a backward-induction solution, which is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium and is sequentially rational. If all terminal payoffs are different for each player, it is unique.

A consequence: every finite game of perfect information — tic-tac-toe, chess, go — has an optimal way to be played, and its value (win, draw, loss) is determined.

A chooses \(L\) (ending the game with payoffs \((2,0)\)) or \(R\). Then B chooses \(L'\) (payoffs \((1,1)\)) or \(R'\). Then A chooses \(L''\) (payoffs \((5,0)\)) or \(R''\) (payoffs \((3,2)\)).

Figure: the trust game; payoffs are (A, B).

At the last node A prefers \(L''\) (\(5 > 3\)). Anticipating this, B prefers \(L'\) (\(1 > 0\)). Anticipating that, A plays \(L\) (\(2 > 1\)). The outcome \((2,0)\) is Pareto dominated by \((3,2)\): rational players do not trust each other. A could promise to play \(R''\), but nothing makes the promise binding.

Imperfect information, two stages. If players 1 and 2 move simultaneously, then players 3 and 4 observe \((a_1, a_2)\) and move simultaneously, apply the same logic: for each \((a_1, a_2)\), let players 3 and 4 play a NE \((a_3^*(a_1, a_2), a_4^*(a_1, a_2))\) of the second-stage game; players 1 and 2 then play a NE of the first-stage game with payoffs \(u_j(a_1, a_2, a_3^*(\cdot), a_4^*(\cdot))\).

9.3 Subgame-perfect equilibrium

A (proper) subgame is a part of the tree that starts at a single node and contains all its successors, such that no information set is cut: if \(x\) belongs to the subgame and \(x' \in h_i(x)\), then \(x'\) belongs to the subgame too. The whole game is a subgame of itself.

A node inside a non-singleton information set cannot start a subgame — the player there does not know they are there.

A strategy profile is a subgame-perfect (Nash) equilibrium (SPE) if it induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame.

An SPE is a NE that passes an additional test, so SPE is a refinement of NE. Every finite extensive-form game has an SPE (apply Nash’s theorem to the smallest subgames, replace them with their equilibrium payoffs, and proceed backwards). For finite games of perfect information, the SPE are exactly the backward-induction solutions.

If player 1 knows that action \(a_1\) triggers the response \(R_2(a_1)\), then a strategy of player 2 of the form “if \(a_1\) then \(a_2 \neq R_2(a_1)\)” is a non-credible threat. SPE eliminates them.

Brian’s strategy \(ss\) threatens to go to S even if Ann chooses R. In the subgame after \(R\), playing \(s\) is not a best response; so \((S, ss)\) is not an SPE. The unique SPE is \((R, rs)\). An extreme version: there is no sci-fi movie, \(S\) means “stay at home”, and after \(R\) Brian can comply (\(r\)) or harm himself (\(k\), payoffs \((-10, -99)\)). Threatening \(k\) might scare Ann into staying home, but carrying it out would be irrational: a non-credible threat.

Whatever the offer, accepting gives the responder something rather than nothing, so in every subgame R accepts. Anticipating this, P offers \(D\). Among the many NE, the unique SPE is “P offers \(D\); R accepts everything”. Refusing a low offer is a non-credible threat.

Experimental ultimatum games do not look like this: humans routinely refuse “insulting” offers. The SPE prediction is correct for the stated payoffs; the discrepancy says that real payoffs include fairness or spite, not that backward induction is wrong.

9.4 Stackelberg games

Von Stackelberg (1934) modelled an incumbent (leader) and an outsider (follower). Take a static bi-matrix game and let player 1 move first, observed by player 2. The backward-induction outcome is the Stackelberg equilibrium.

  • In the Battle of the Sexes with Ann as leader, the Stackelberg equilibrium is \((R,R)\); Brian gets his minimax \(1\).
  • In Odds & Evens moving first is a disadvantage, unless the leader commits to a mixture: the Stackelberg outcome is both players mixing \(50/50\), each getting the minimax \(0\).
Carl \ Joe F G H
R 2, 2 3, 1 0, 0
S 1, 6 5, 4 6, 4
T 0, 1 4, 3 6, 2

\((R, F)\) is the pure NE, with payoffs \((2, 2)\). Carl as leader: Joe’s best responses are \(F\) to \(R\) and to \(S\), \(G\) to \(T\); Carl’s payoffs are then \(2\), \(1\), \(4\), so he leads with \(T\): outcome \((T, G)\), payoffs \((4,3)\). Joe gets \(3\), above his minimax \(2\). Joe as leader: Carl’s best replies are \(R\) to \(F\), \(S\) to \(G\), and \(\{S, T\}\) to \(H\). Ties need a convention; with a generous follower (who breaks ties in the leader’s favour) and a generous leader (who breaks ties in the follower’s favour), Joe leads with \(H\) and Carl answers \(S\): outcome \((S, H)\), payoffs \((6, 4)\). Carl, the follower, gets \(6\), far above his minimax \(2\).

With ties broken in the leader’s favour, the leader’s Stackelberg payoff is at least their payoff in any pure Nash equilibrium of the static game (they could always lead with their NE strategy, to which the follower’s NE strategy is a best reply). The follower’s payoff is at least their minimax.

The follower is not necessarily worse off in general games. In zero-sum games, however, the leader’s gain is the follower’s loss — strange at first sight, since the follower has more information. In single-agent optimisation knowledge is power; in games ignorance can be bliss: the leader anticipates the follower’s informed reaction and restricts the follower’s useful options. This “first-mover advantage” returns in the Stackelberg duopoly of Chapter 11.

9.5 Centipede games and the limits of backward induction

In a centipede game two players alternately choose to stop, taking the larger share of a growing pot, or continue, letting the pot grow and passing the move. Each stop is arranged so that the mover gains more by stopping now than by being stopped by the opponent at the next node. Backward induction then unravels from the last node: the last mover stops, so the one before stops, and so on. The unique SPE stops at the root, although continuing would make both far better off.

Experiments find that most subjects continue for several rounds. The prediction requires common knowledge of rationality at every node, including nodes that rationality says are never reached. A player who observes a continuation (an irrational move, by hypothesis) may reasonably doubt that the opponent will be rational later. Backward induction is most convincing in short games and for automated agents. Several exercises (house hunting, project hand-over, parties, joint projects) are centipede-like: whether the game unravels depends on whether stopping beats waiting at each node.

9.6 Commitment and the order of moves

Turning a simultaneous game into a sequential one changes what is credible.

  • The leader can always do at least as well as in some Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous game: by playing that equilibrium strategy, the follower’s best response reproduces it. Often the leader does strictly better (first-mover advantage, as in Stackelberg).
  • Moving first can also hurt: in Matching Pennies, being observed is fatal. Information about the leader’s move is valuable to the follower whenever the game has a competitive flavour.
  • Commitment is the power to make a threat credible by removing your own options: burning bridges, contracts, reputations, automated policies. Schelling’s paradox: limiting your own choices can raise your payoff, because it changes the opponent’s best response.

In a game with ties at terminal nodes, backward induction needs a tie-breaking rule. The usual conventions are a generous follower, who breaks ties in the leader’s favour, and a generous leader. Without such a rule the SPE need not be unique.

9.7 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapters 3 and 4.

(BM 3.1, continued) Find all SPE of the student–professor game. Which NE involve non-credible threats?

Solution. Every answer \(S\) gives P a positive payoff (\(3\), \(9\), \(15 > 0\)), so in every subgame P accepts: P plays \(SSS\). Anticipating this, U compares \(17\), \(20\), \(14\) and chooses \(x = 1\). The unique SPE is \((1, SSS)\). The NE \((1, SSN)\), \((1, NSS)\), \((1, NSN)\) have the same outcome but irrational plans off the path. \((0, SNS)\), \((0, SNN)\) and \((2, NNS)\) change the outcome through the non-credible threat of rejecting project \(1\) (the “strict professor”).

(BM 3.2, continued) Solve the Miss 1 / Mister 2 game by backward induction and give the SPE strategies. Is \(((A, A, A), (D, D))\) a NE?

Solution. At both final nodes Miss 1 plays A (\(9 > 6\) and \(6 > 4\)). Mister 2 then compares: after A, \(D\) gives \(-3\) and \(C\) gives \(-9\), so he drops; after B, \(D\) gives \(3\) and \(C\) gives \(9\), so he continues. Miss 1 compares \(3\) (A) with \(6\) (B). SPE: Miss 1 \((B; A, A)\) (root, after-A node, after-B node), Mister 2 \((D, C)\), with outcome \((6, 9)\). \(((A, A, A), (D, D))\) is a NE (payoffs \((3, -3)\); Miss 1 switching to B gets \(2\) under D), but not an SPE: dropping after B is a non-credible threat.

(BM 3.3) Lou asks Karen to go shopping on day 1. If she says No, Lou either Gives up or Requests again on day 2, and likewise on day 3, after which the game ends. Shopping on day \(d\) gives Karen \(d\) and Lou \(5 - 2d\); not shopping gives \(0\). Find the SPE and a NE that is not subgame perfect.

Solution. Day 3: Karen says Yes (\(3 > 0\)), giving Lou \(-1\). Day 2: Lou Gives up (\(0 > -1\)). Karen on day 2 says Yes (\(2 > 0\)). Day 1: Karen compares Yes (\(1\)) with No, after which Lou Requests (\(1 > 0\)) and she gets \(2\): she says No. The SPE is Karen \((N, Y, Y)\), Lou \((R, G)\), outcome day 2 with \((2, 1)\). Non-SPE NE: Lou \((G, G)\) and Karen \((Y, \cdot, \cdot)\). The threat of giving up immediately makes Karen accept on day 1, but Lou would not carry it out.

(BM 3.4, continued) Find the SPE of the house-buying game.

Solution. November: J buys F (\(40\)). October: K compares B (\(28\)), C (\(12\)), and waiting (\(10\), since J then buys F): she buys B. September: J compares A (\(10\)) with waiting (\(15\)). SPE \((XF, B)\), outcome Bellevue, \((15, 28)\). \((XD, B)\) has the same outcome but J’s plan to buy D in November is not optimal: it is a NE, not an SPE.

(BM 3.5) Yvonne (with a car) and Zoe (by bus) choose Beach or Disco simultaneously. Yvonne gets \(4\) at the beach and \(1\) at the disco; Zoe \(1\) and \(7\); each gets \(+5\) if together. If they are apart, Yvonne alone may Change location (costing her \(3\)) or Stay. Find the SPE outcome and Yvonne’s strategy.

Solution. After \((B, D)\): Change gives Yvonne \(1 + 5 - 3 = 3\), Stay gives \(4\), so she stays, \((4, 7)\). After \((D, B)\): Change gives \(4 + 5 - 3 = 6\), Stay gives \(1\), so she changes, \((6, 6)\). The reduced simultaneous game is \((B, B) = (9, 6)\), \((B, D) = (4, 7)\), \((D, B) = (6, 6)\), \((D, D) = (6, 12)\). \(D\) is dominant for Zoe, and Yvonne’s best response is \(D\). Outcome \((D, D)\) with \((6, 12)\); Yvonne’s SPE strategy is \((D;\ S \text{ after } (B, D),\ C \text{ after } (D, B))\). Yvonne’s flexibility hurts her: because she can always fix a mismatch, Zoe never needs to compromise.

(BM 3.6) Wife (7am and 7pm) and Husband (2pm) can each, at their turn, fix Food (benefit \(30\) to both, cost \(10\) to the doer), Clean (benefit \(50\), cost \(20\)) or do Nothing (\(+10\) to themselves). A chore cannot be repeated. The Wife’s 7am chores have \(10\%\) larger cost and benefit. Find the SPE.

Solution. Backward induction over the three-level tree gives the path: Wife does Nothing at 7am, Husband fixes Food, Wife Cleans at 7pm, with payoffs \((10 + 30 + 50 - 20,\ 30 + 50 - 10) = (70, 70)\). At 7pm the Wife does whichever chore remains if its benefit net of cost beats \(10\) (clean: \(30 > 10\); food: \(20 > 10\)). Anticipating this, the Husband prefers food to cleaning, and the Wife prefers to wait rather than pay the premium at 7am. The full SPE also specifies the Husband’s and the Wife’s moves in the 8 unreached subgames.

(BM 3.7) Anita and Ben simultaneously rush to the closet (5 candies) or the dining room (10 candies). In different rooms each keeps their candies; in the same room Anita proposes a split, giving Ben at least one candy, and Ben accepts or cries (both get \(0\)). Reduce the game by backward induction and find the SPE and a non-subgame-perfect NE.

Solution. In each ultimatum subgame Ben accepts anything positive, so Anita offers \(1\): \((4, 1)\) in the closet and \((9, 1)\) in the dining room. Reduced game: \((C, C) = (4, 1)\), \((C, D) = (5, 10)\), \((D, C) = (10, 5)\), \((D, D) = (9, 1)\). \(D\) is dominant for Anita and Ben answers \(C\). SPE: Anita plays \(D\) and offers \(1\) in either room; Ben plays \(C\) and accepts every offer. Non-SPE NE: Anita plays \(C\), Ben plays \(D\) and, in either bargaining subgame, accepts only splits leaving Anita a single candy. Outcome \((C, D)\) with \((5, 10)\). Anita does not deviate to \(D\), since she would then get at most \(1\). It is a NE because the threat is never tested, but crying when offered a positive amount is not credible.

(BM 3.8) Jeremy (odd days) and Kayla (even days) work on a project worth \(2^t\) on day \(t\). At the end of each day the worker Submits, getting \(2^t\) (the other gets \(60\%\)), or Hands over. After day \(10\) the project is worth \(0\). Find the SPE and a non-subgame-perfect NE.

Solution. On day \(10\) Kayla submits (\(1024 > 0\)). On day \(9\) Jeremy compares \(512\) (submit) with \(0.6 \times 1024 = 614.4\): he hands over. Inductively, at every \(t < 10\) handing over gives \(0.6 \cdot 2^{t+1} = 1.2\cdot2^t > 2^t\). SPE: always hand over before day \(10\); Kayla submits on day \(10\). Payoffs: Kayla \(1024\), Jeremy \(614.4\). This centipede does not unravel, because the pot grows fast enough. A non-SPE NE: Jeremy submits on day \(9\), and Kayla’s plan is to hand over even on day \(10\) (letting the deadline expire). Jeremy then rationally stops at \(9\); Kayla’s irrational plan is never tested.

(BM 3.9) In weeks \(0, 1, \dots, 5\) the Sorority (even weeks) and Fraternity (odd weeks) alternately decide to throw a Party or stop (N). A party costs the organiser \(10\) and gives both sections fun \(25\cdot0.6^j\) in week \(j\). Find the SPE. What if there is no deadline?

Solution. Cumulative payoffs (S, F) if the game stops after \(k\) parties: \(k = 0\): \((0, 0)\); \(1\): \((15, 25)\); \(2\): \((30, 30)\); \(3\): \((29, 39)\); \(4\): \((34.4, 34.4)\); \(5\): \((27.64, 37.64)\); \(6\): \((29.58, 29.58)\). Week 5: F stops (\(37.64 > 29.58\)). Week 4: S stops (\(34.4 > 27.64\)). Week 3: F stops (\(39 > 34.4\)). Week 2: S stops (\(30 > 29\)). Week 1: F parties (\(30 > 25\)). Week 0: S parties (\(30 > 0\)). SPE: S plays (P, N, N) and F plays (P, N, N) at their three nodes. Two parties are thrown, S stops in week 2, and the payoffs are \((30, 30)\). (The printed solution misreads its table and ends in week 4.) Without a deadline the same stationary profile, stopping from week 2 on, remains an SPE. From week 2 the organiser’s fun \(25\cdot0.6^j \leq 9\) is below the cost \(10\), so if the other will stop next, organising never pays (one-shot deviation principle).

(BM 3.11) Pirates of ranks \(1, \dots, n\): pirate 1 may take a pearl; whenever pirate \(k\) holds it, pirate \(k+1\) may kill him and take it. Each prefers (alive with pearl) \(\succ\) (alive without) \(\succ\) (dead). Solve for \(n = 5\) and \(n = 8\).

Solution. The captain \(n\) always takes the pearl (no one above him). So pirate \(n - 1\) never takes it (he would be killed), so pirate \(n - 2\) can take it safely, and so on: pirate \(j\) takes the pearl iff \(n - j\) is even. For \(n = 5\) (odd), pirate 1 takes the pearl and pirate 2 looks away. For \(n = 8\) (even), pirate 1 leaves the pearl. The SPE specifies the rule for every pirate, though only one or two ever move. The parity argument is typical of backward induction in long games.

(BM 4.6, continued) Which NE of the duel game are subgame perfect?

Solution. After S the Duke prefers F (\(-2 > -34\)); after P he prefers F (\(-22 > -78\)). So the Duke plays FF, and the Count compares \(-8\) (S) with \(-7\) (P). The unique SPE is \((P, FF)\). \((S, FL)\) relies on the threat of a duel to the death with pistols, which the Duke would not carry out. \((P, LF)\) has the right outcome but an irrational plan after S.

(BM 4.7, continued) Which NE of the cake game are subgame perfect?

Solution. Emily answers each cake with the shop that maximises its success probability: F after A and after B, G after C. David then compares \(1.5\) (A), \(1.6\) (B), \(0.9\) (C). The SPE is David B and Emily (F, F, G), outcome \((1.6, 0.8)\). Of the sequential NE \((B, FF)\), \((B, FG)\), \((C, GG)\) (strategies for B and C), only \((B, FG)\) is subgame perfect.

From the course material:

In the entry game, an entrant chooses Out (payoffs \((0, 2)\)) or Enter; after entry the incumbent chooses Accept (\((1,1)\)) or Fight (\((-1,-1)\)). Find all pure NE and the SPE.

Solution. Normal form: \((\text{Out}, \text{Fight})\) gives \((0, 2)\) and is a NE (entering against Fight gives \(-1\); the incumbent is indifferent since its move is off-path). \((\text{Enter}, \text{Accept})\) is a NE with \((1,1)\). Backward induction: after entry Accept beats Fight (\(1 > -1\)), so the entrant enters. The unique SPE is \((\text{Enter}, \text{Accept})\); \((\text{Out}, \text{Fight})\) rests on a non-credible threat.

Two investors each put 5000 € in a fund that pays \(x\) € each after 4 years if untouched. At the end of years \(1, 2, 3\) each may independently withdraw, getting \(6000 + 500t\) € for themselves (the other gets nothing); simultaneous withdrawal splits that amount; any withdrawal ends the game. Find the SPE for \(x = 7200\) and \(x = 8000\).

Solution. Solve backwards. Year 3: withdrawing gives \(7500\) (alone) or \(3750\) (both); keeping gives \(x\) if the other keeps, \(0\) if the other withdraws. For \(x = 7200\), Withdraw strictly dominates (\(7500 > 7200\), \(3750 > 0\)): both withdraw, \(3750\) each. For \(x = 8000\) the year-3 stage is a coordination game with NE (Keep, Keep) → \(8000\) and (Withdraw, Withdraw) → \(3750\). Year 2: withdrawing alone gives \(7000\). With continuation value \(3750\) (case \(x = 7200\), or \(x = 8000\) with the bad year-3 NE) Withdraw dominates; with continuation \(8000\), Keep–Keep is a NE (and so is Withdraw–Withdraw at \(3500\)). Year 1 repeats the logic with \(6500\). Hence for \(x = 7200\) the unique SPE has both withdrawing at year 1 (\(3250\) each), while for \(x = 8000\) there are SPE where both keep until the end (payoff \(8000\)) as well as SPE where both withdraw at the first opportunity.

## Backward induction

In a dynamic game of **perfect information** every information set is a singleton. Take two players: player 1 chooses $a_1 \in A_1$; player 2 observes $a_1$ and chooses $a_2 \in A_2(a_1)$; payoffs are $u_1(a_1, a_2)$, $u_2(a_1, a_2)$. When it is their turn, player 2 knows exactly where they are and solves
$$R_2(a_1) = \arg\max_{a_2 \in A_2(a_1)} u_2(a_1, a_2).$$
Thanks to complete information, player 1 anticipates this reaction and solves $\max_{a_1} u_1(a_1, R_2(a_1))$. The resulting outcome $(a_1^*, R_2(a_1^*))$ is a Nash equilibrium in pure strategies.

::: {.theorem name="Zermelo / Kuhn"}
Every finite game of perfect information has a backward-induction solution, which is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium and is sequentially rational. If all terminal payoffs are different for each player, it is unique.
:::

A consequence: every finite game of perfect information — tic-tac-toe, chess, go — has an optimal way to be played, and its value (win, draw, loss) is determined.

A chooses $L$ (ending the game with payoffs $(2,0)$) or $R$. Then B chooses $L'$ (payoffs $(1,1)$) or $R'$. Then A chooses $L''$ (payoffs $(5,0)$) or $R''$ (payoffs $(3,2)$).

```{=latex}
    \fill (\x,0) circle (1.6pt);
    \node[above] at (\x,0) {\p};
  }
  \draw (0,0) -- node[above] {$R$} (1,0);
  \draw (1,0) -- node[above] {$R'$} (2,0);
  \draw (2,0) -- node[above] {$R''$} (3,0) node[right] {$(3,2)$};
  \draw (0,0) -- node[left] {$L$} (0,-1) node[below] {$(2,0)$};
  \draw (1,0) -- node[left] {$L'$} (1,-1) node[below] {$(1,1)$};
  \draw (2,0) -- node[left] {$L''$} (2,-1) node[below] {$(5,0)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
```

*Figure: the trust game; payoffs are (A, B).*

At the last node A prefers $L''$ ($5 > 3$). Anticipating this, B prefers $L'$ ($1 > 0$). Anticipating that, A plays $L$ ($2 > 1$). The outcome $(2,0)$ is Pareto dominated by $(3,2)$: rational players do not trust each other. A could promise to play $R''$, but nothing makes the promise binding.
:::

**Imperfect information, two stages.** If players 1 and 2 move simultaneously, then players 3 and 4 observe $(a_1, a_2)$ and move simultaneously, apply the same logic: for each $(a_1, a_2)$, let players 3 and 4 play a NE $(a_3^*(a_1, a_2), a_4^*(a_1, a_2))$ of the second-stage game; players 1 and 2 then play a NE of the first-stage game with payoffs $u_j(a_1, a_2, a_3^*(\cdot), a_4^*(\cdot))$.

## Subgame-perfect equilibrium

::: {.definition name="Subgame"}
A (proper) **subgame** is a part of the tree that starts at a single node and contains all its successors, such that no information set is cut: if $x$ belongs to the subgame and $x' \in h_i(x)$, then $x'$ belongs to the subgame too. The whole game is a subgame of itself.
:::

A node inside a non-singleton information set cannot start a subgame — the player there does not know they are there.

::: {.definition name="Subgame-perfect equilibrium (Selten, 1965)"}
A strategy profile is a **subgame-perfect (Nash) equilibrium** (SPE) if it induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame.
:::

An SPE is a NE that passes an additional test, so SPE is a **refinement** of NE. Every finite extensive-form game has an SPE (apply Nash's theorem to the smallest subgames, replace them with their equilibrium payoffs, and proceed backwards). For finite games of perfect information, the SPE are exactly the backward-induction solutions.

::: {.definition name="Credibility"}
If player 1 knows that action $a_1$ triggers the response $R_2(a_1)$, then a strategy of player 2 of the form "if $a_1$ then $a_2 \neq R_2(a_1)$" is a **non-credible threat**. SPE eliminates them.
:::

::: {.example name="Credibility in the Battle of the Sexes"}
Brian's strategy $ss$ threatens to go to S even if Ann chooses R. In the subgame after $R$, playing $s$ is not a best response; so $(S, ss)$ is not an SPE. The unique SPE is $(R, rs)$. An extreme version: there is no sci-fi movie, $S$ means "stay at home", and after $R$ Brian can comply ($r$) or harm himself ($k$, payoffs $(-10, -99)$). Threatening $k$ might scare Ann into staying home, but carrying it out would be irrational: a non-credible threat.
:::

::: {.example name="Ultimatum game, solved"}
Whatever the offer, accepting gives the responder something rather than nothing, so in every subgame R accepts. Anticipating this, P offers $D$. Among the many NE, the unique SPE is "P offers $D$; R accepts everything". Refusing a low offer is a non-credible threat.
:::

::: {.callout-warning}
Experimental ultimatum games do not look like this: humans routinely refuse "insulting" offers. The SPE prediction is correct for the stated payoffs; the discrepancy says that real payoffs include fairness or spite, not that backward induction is wrong.
:::

## Stackelberg games

Von Stackelberg (1934) modelled an incumbent (leader) and an outsider (follower). Take a static bi-matrix game and let player 1 move first, observed by player 2. The backward-induction outcome is the **Stackelberg equilibrium**.

- In the Battle of the Sexes with Ann as leader, the Stackelberg equilibrium is $(R,R)$; Brian gets his minimax $1$.
- In Odds & Evens moving first is a disadvantage, unless the leader commits to a mixture: the Stackelberg outcome is both players mixing $50/50$, each getting the minimax $0$.

::: {.example name="Leader advantage"}
| Carl \\ Joe | F | G | H |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **R** | 2, 2 | 3, 1 | 0, 0 |
| **S** | 1, 6 | 5, 4 | 6, 4 |
| **T** | 0, 1 | 4, 3 | 6, 2 |

$(R, F)$ is the pure NE, with payoffs $(2, 2)$. **Carl as leader:** Joe's best responses are $F$ to $R$ and to $S$, $G$ to $T$; Carl's payoffs are then $2$, $1$, $4$, so he leads with $T$: outcome $(T, G)$, payoffs $(4,3)$. Joe gets $3$, above his minimax $2$. **Joe as leader:** Carl's best replies are $R$ to $F$, $S$ to $G$, and $\{S, T\}$ to $H$. Ties need a convention; with a *generous follower* (who breaks ties in the leader's favour) and a *generous leader* (who breaks ties in the follower's favour), Joe leads with $H$ and Carl answers $S$: outcome $(S, H)$, payoffs $(6, 4)$. Carl, the follower, gets $6$, far above his minimax $2$.
:::

::: {.proposition name="Leader and follower payoffs"}
With ties broken in the leader's favour, the leader's Stackelberg payoff is at least their payoff in any pure Nash equilibrium of the static game (they could always lead with their NE strategy, to which the follower's NE strategy is a best reply). The follower's payoff is at least their minimax.
:::

The follower is not necessarily worse off in general games. In **zero-sum** games, however, the leader's gain is the follower's loss — strange at first sight, since the follower has *more* information. In single-agent optimisation knowledge is power; in games **ignorance can be bliss**: the leader anticipates the follower's informed reaction and restricts the follower's useful options. This "first-mover advantage" returns in the Stackelberg duopoly of Chapter 11.

## Centipede games and the limits of backward induction

In a **centipede game** two players alternately choose to *stop*, taking the larger share of a growing pot, or *continue*, letting the pot grow and passing the move. Each stop is arranged so that the mover gains more by stopping now than by being stopped by the opponent at the next node. Backward induction then unravels from the last node: the last mover stops, so the one before stops, and so on. The unique SPE stops at the root, although continuing would make both far better off.

Experiments find that most subjects continue for several rounds. The prediction requires *common knowledge of rationality at every node*, including nodes that rationality says are never reached. A player who observes a continuation (an irrational move, by hypothesis) may reasonably doubt that the opponent will be rational later. Backward induction is most convincing in short games and for automated agents. Several exercises (house hunting, project hand-over, parties, joint projects) are centipede-like: whether the game unravels depends on whether stopping beats waiting at each node.

## Commitment and the order of moves

Turning a simultaneous game into a sequential one changes what is credible.

- The **leader** can always do at least as well as in some Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous game: by playing that equilibrium strategy, the follower's best response reproduces it. Often the leader does strictly better (first-mover advantage, as in Stackelberg).
- Moving first can also **hurt**: in Matching Pennies, being observed is fatal. Information about the leader's move is valuable to the follower whenever the game has a competitive flavour.
- **Commitment** is the power to make a threat credible by removing your own options: burning bridges, contracts, reputations, automated policies. Schelling's paradox: limiting your own choices can raise your payoff, because it changes the opponent's best response.

In a game with ties at terminal nodes, backward induction needs a **tie-breaking** rule. The usual conventions are a *generous follower*, who breaks ties in the leader's favour, and a *generous leader*. Without such a rule the SPE need not be unique.

## Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapters 3 and 4.

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.1, continued)** Find all SPE of the student–professor game. Which NE involve non-credible threats?
:::

::: {.solution}
Every answer $S$ gives P a positive payoff ($3$, $9$, $15 > 0$), so in every subgame P accepts: P plays $SSS$. Anticipating this, U compares $17$, $20$, $14$ and chooses $x = 1$. The unique SPE is $(1, SSS)$. The NE $(1, SSN)$, $(1, NSS)$, $(1, NSN)$ have the same outcome but irrational plans off the path. $(0, SNS)$, $(0, SNN)$ and $(2, NNS)$ change the outcome through the non-credible threat of rejecting project $1$ (the "strict professor").
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.2, continued)** Solve the Miss 1 / Mister 2 game by backward induction and give the SPE strategies. Is $((A, A, A), (D, D))$ a NE?
:::

::: {.solution}
At both final nodes Miss 1 plays A ($9 > 6$ and $6 > 4$). Mister 2 then compares: after A, $D$ gives $-3$ and $C$ gives $-9$, so he drops; after B, $D$ gives $3$ and $C$ gives $9$, so he continues. Miss 1 compares $3$ (A) with $6$ (B). SPE: Miss 1 $(B; A, A)$ (root, after-A node, after-B node), Mister 2 $(D, C)$, with outcome $(6, 9)$. $((A, A, A), (D, D))$ is a NE (payoffs $(3, -3)$; Miss 1 switching to B gets $2$ under D), but not an SPE: dropping after B is a non-credible threat.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.3)** Lou asks Karen to go shopping on day 1. If she says No, Lou either Gives up or Requests again on day 2, and likewise on day 3, after which the game ends. Shopping on day $d$ gives Karen $d$ and Lou $5 - 2d$; not shopping gives $0$. Find the SPE and a NE that is not subgame perfect.
:::

::: {.solution}
Day 3: Karen says Yes ($3 > 0$), giving Lou $-1$. Day 2: Lou Gives up ($0 > -1$). Karen on day 2 says Yes ($2 > 0$). Day 1: Karen compares Yes ($1$) with No, after which Lou Requests ($1 > 0$) and she gets $2$: she says No. The SPE is Karen $(N, Y, Y)$, Lou $(R, G)$, outcome day 2 with $(2, 1)$. Non-SPE NE: Lou $(G, G)$ and Karen $(Y, \cdot, \cdot)$. The threat of giving up immediately makes Karen accept on day 1, but Lou would not carry it out.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.4, continued)** Find the SPE of the house-buying game.
:::

::: {.solution}
November: J buys F ($40$). October: K compares B ($28$), C ($12$), and waiting ($10$, since J then buys F): she buys B. September: J compares A ($10$) with waiting ($15$). SPE $(XF, B)$, outcome Bellevue, $(15, 28)$. $(XD, B)$ has the same outcome but J's plan to buy D in November is not optimal: it is a NE, not an SPE.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.5)** Yvonne (with a car) and Zoe (by bus) choose Beach or Disco simultaneously. Yvonne gets $4$ at the beach and $1$ at the disco; Zoe $1$ and $7$; each gets $+5$ if together. If they are apart, Yvonne alone may Change location (costing her $3$) or Stay. Find the SPE outcome and Yvonne's strategy.
:::

::: {.solution}
After $(B, D)$: Change gives Yvonne $1 + 5 - 3 = 3$, Stay gives $4$, so she stays, $(4, 7)$. After $(D, B)$: Change gives $4 + 5 - 3 = 6$, Stay gives $1$, so she changes, $(6, 6)$. The reduced simultaneous game is $(B, B) = (9, 6)$, $(B, D) = (4, 7)$, $(D, B) = (6, 6)$, $(D, D) = (6, 12)$. $D$ is dominant for Zoe, and Yvonne's best response is $D$. Outcome $(D, D)$ with $(6, 12)$; Yvonne's SPE strategy is $(D;\ S \text{ after } (B, D),\ C \text{ after } (D, B))$. Yvonne's flexibility hurts her: because she can always fix a mismatch, Zoe never needs to compromise.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.6)** Wife (7am and 7pm) and Husband (2pm) can each, at their turn, fix Food (benefit $30$ to both, cost $10$ to the doer), Clean (benefit $50$, cost $20$) or do Nothing ($+10$ to themselves). A chore cannot be repeated. The Wife's 7am chores have $10\%$ larger cost and benefit. Find the SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Backward induction over the three-level tree gives the path: Wife does **Nothing** at 7am, Husband fixes **Food**, Wife **Cleans** at 7pm, with payoffs $(10 + 30 + 50 - 20,\ 30 + 50 - 10) = (70, 70)$. At 7pm the Wife does whichever chore remains if its benefit net of cost beats $10$ (clean: $30 > 10$; food: $20 > 10$). Anticipating this, the Husband prefers food to cleaning, and the Wife prefers to wait rather than pay the premium at 7am. The full SPE also specifies the Husband's and the Wife's moves in the 8 unreached subgames.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.7)** Anita and Ben simultaneously rush to the closet (5 candies) or the dining room (10 candies). In different rooms each keeps their candies; in the same room Anita proposes a split, giving Ben at least one candy, and Ben accepts or cries (both get $0$). Reduce the game by backward induction and find the SPE and a non-subgame-perfect NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
In each ultimatum subgame Ben accepts anything positive, so Anita offers $1$: $(4, 1)$ in the closet and $(9, 1)$ in the dining room. Reduced game: $(C, C) = (4, 1)$, $(C, D) = (5, 10)$, $(D, C) = (10, 5)$, $(D, D) = (9, 1)$. $D$ is dominant for Anita and Ben answers $C$. SPE: Anita plays $D$ and offers $1$ in either room; Ben plays $C$ and accepts every offer. Non-SPE NE: Anita plays $C$, Ben plays $D$ and, in either bargaining subgame, accepts only splits leaving Anita a single candy. Outcome $(C, D)$ with $(5, 10)$. Anita does not deviate to $D$, since she would then get at most $1$. It is a NE because the threat is never tested, but crying when offered a positive amount is not credible.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.8)** Jeremy (odd days) and Kayla (even days) work on a project worth $2^t$ on day $t$. At the end of each day the worker Submits, getting $2^t$ (the other gets $60\%$), or Hands over. After day $10$ the project is worth $0$. Find the SPE and a non-subgame-perfect NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
On day $10$ Kayla submits ($1024 > 0$). On day $9$ Jeremy compares $512$ (submit) with $0.6 \times 1024 = 614.4$: he hands over. Inductively, at every $t < 10$ handing over gives $0.6 \cdot 2^{t+1} = 1.2\cdot2^t > 2^t$. SPE: always hand over before day $10$; Kayla submits on day $10$. Payoffs: Kayla $1024$, Jeremy $614.4$. This centipede does *not* unravel, because the pot grows fast enough. A non-SPE NE: Jeremy submits on day $9$, and Kayla's plan is to hand over even on day $10$ (letting the deadline expire). Jeremy then rationally stops at $9$; Kayla's irrational plan is never tested.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.9)** In weeks $0, 1, \dots, 5$ the Sorority (even weeks) and Fraternity (odd weeks) alternately decide to throw a Party or stop (N). A party costs the organiser $10$ and gives both sections fun $25\cdot0.6^j$ in week $j$. Find the SPE. What if there is no deadline?
:::

::: {.solution}
Cumulative payoffs (S, F) if the game stops after $k$ parties: $k = 0$: $(0, 0)$; $1$: $(15, 25)$; $2$: $(30, 30)$; $3$: $(29, 39)$; $4$: $(34.4, 34.4)$; $5$: $(27.64, 37.64)$; $6$: $(29.58, 29.58)$. Week 5: F stops ($37.64 > 29.58$). Week 4: S stops ($34.4 > 27.64$). Week 3: F stops ($39 > 34.4$). Week 2: S stops ($30 > 29$). Week 1: F parties ($30 > 25$). Week 0: S parties ($30 > 0$). SPE: S plays (P, N, N) and F plays (P, N, N) at their three nodes. Two parties are thrown, S stops in week 2, and the payoffs are $(30, 30)$. (The printed solution misreads its table and ends in week 4.) Without a deadline the same stationary profile, stopping from week 2 on, remains an SPE. From week 2 the organiser's fun $25\cdot0.6^j \leq 9$ is below the cost $10$, so if the other will stop next, organising never pays (one-shot deviation principle).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 3.11)** Pirates of ranks $1, \dots, n$: pirate 1 may take a pearl; whenever pirate $k$ holds it, pirate $k+1$ may kill him and take it. Each prefers (alive with pearl) $\succ$ (alive without) $\succ$ (dead). Solve for $n = 5$ and $n = 8$.
:::

::: {.solution}
The captain $n$ always takes the pearl (no one above him). So pirate $n - 1$ never takes it (he would be killed), so pirate $n - 2$ can take it safely, and so on: pirate $j$ takes the pearl iff $n - j$ is even. For $n = 5$ (odd), pirate 1 takes the pearl and pirate 2 looks away. For $n = 8$ (even), pirate 1 leaves the pearl. The SPE specifies the rule for every pirate, though only one or two ever move. The parity argument is typical of backward induction in long games.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.6, continued)** Which NE of the duel game are subgame perfect?
:::

::: {.solution}
After S the Duke prefers F ($-2 > -34$); after P he prefers F ($-22 > -78$). So the Duke plays FF, and the Count compares $-8$ (S) with $-7$ (P). The unique SPE is $(P, FF)$. $(S, FL)$ relies on the threat of a duel to the death with pistols, which the Duke would not carry out. $(P, LF)$ has the right outcome but an irrational plan after S.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.7, continued)** Which NE of the cake game are subgame perfect?
:::

::: {.solution}
Emily answers each cake with the shop that maximises its success probability: F after A and after B, G after C. David then compares $1.5$ (A), $1.6$ (B), $0.9$ (C). The SPE is David B and Emily (F, F, G), outcome $(1.6, 0.8)$. Of the sequential NE $(B, FF)$, $(B, FG)$, $(C, GG)$ (strategies for B and C), only $(B, FG)$ is subgame perfect.
:::

From the course material:

::: {.exercise}
In the entry game, an entrant chooses Out (payoffs $(0, 2)$) or Enter; after entry the incumbent chooses Accept ($(1,1)$) or Fight ($(-1,-1)$). Find all pure NE and the SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Normal form: $(\text{Out}, \text{Fight})$ gives $(0, 2)$ and is a NE (entering against Fight gives $-1$; the incumbent is indifferent since its move is off-path). $(\text{Enter}, \text{Accept})$ is a NE with $(1,1)$. Backward induction: after entry Accept beats Fight ($1 > -1$), so the entrant enters. The unique SPE is $(\text{Enter}, \text{Accept})$; $(\text{Out}, \text{Fight})$ rests on a non-credible threat.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Two investors each put 5000 € in a fund that pays $x$ € each after 4 years if untouched. At the end of years $1, 2, 3$ each may independently withdraw, getting $6000 + 500t$ € for themselves (the other gets nothing); simultaneous withdrawal splits that amount; any withdrawal ends the game. Find the SPE for $x = 7200$ and $x = 8000$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Solve backwards. **Year 3:** withdrawing gives $7500$ (alone) or $3750$ (both); keeping gives $x$ if the other keeps, $0$ if the other withdraws. For $x = 7200$, Withdraw strictly dominates ($7500 > 7200$, $3750 > 0$): both withdraw, $3750$ each. For $x = 8000$ the year-3 stage is a coordination game with NE (Keep, Keep) → $8000$ and (Withdraw, Withdraw) → $3750$. **Year 2:** withdrawing alone gives $7000$. With continuation value $3750$ (case $x = 7200$, or $x = 8000$ with the bad year-3 NE) Withdraw dominates; with continuation $8000$, Keep–Keep is a NE (and so is Withdraw–Withdraw at $3500$). **Year 1** repeats the logic with $6500$. Hence for $x = 7200$ the unique SPE has both withdrawing at year 1 ($3250$ each), while for $x = 8000$ there are SPE where both keep until the end (payoff $8000$) as well as SPE where both withdraw at the first opportunity.
:::
