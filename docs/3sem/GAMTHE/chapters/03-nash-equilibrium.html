3  Nash equilibrium

Dominance arguments ask only that players best-respond to some belief. They often leave many outcomes standing. Nash equilibrium sharpens the prediction by demanding that beliefs also be correct: every player best-responds to what the others actually do. This chapter defines the concept, shows how to find equilibria in bi-matrices, relates it to dominance, and introduces the tools — Pareto efficiency, price of anarchy — used to judge the quality of an equilibrium.

3.1 Definition

The guiding idea: game theory should predict the outcome of play among rational players, and a prediction is credible only if, once announced, nobody wants to deviate from it. Such a prediction is self-enforcing (or strategically stable).

In \(G = \{S_1, \dots, S_n; u_1, \dots, u_n\}\) the profile \(s^* = (s_1^*, \dots, s_n^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium (NE) if, for every player \(i\), \(s_i^*\) is a best response to \(s_{-i}^*\): \[u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \qquad \forall\, s_i \in S_i,\] equivalently \(s_i^* \in \arg\max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)\) for all \(i\).

Motivation by contradiction. Take a profile \(s'\) that is not a NE. Then some player \(i\) has \(s_i'' \in S_i\) with \(u_i(s_i'', s_{-i}') > u_i(s_i', s_{-i}')\): an incentive to deviate unilaterally. Any outcome other than a NE is therefore unstable.

Remark (A forecast, not a process). A NE is a property of a one-shot game: an outcome after which nobody has regrets about their own choice, given the others’. It is not the end point of a sequence of adjustments — a widespread but mistaken reading that dynamic and repeated games will dispel.

3.2 Finding equilibria in a bi-matrix

Brute force checks every cell. More efficiently: for each column, mark player 1’s best payoff(s); for each row, mark player 2’s best payoff(s). A cell with both entries marked is a NE.

  • Example 1: \((M, R)\) is the only NE — the same outcome IESDS produced.
  • Example 2: \((D, R)\) with payoffs \((9,9)\) is the only NE, although IESDS removed nothing.
  • Odds & Evens: no cell has both entries marked; there is no pure-strategy NE (Chapter 5 restores one by allowing randomisation).
  • Battle of the Sexes: both \((R,R)\) and \((S,S)\) are NE. The concept confirms the intuition but cannot predict which will occur.
  • Prisoner’s Dilemma: \((F,F)\) is the unique NE.

3.3 Relation with dominance

In a finite game:

  1. every NE survives IESDS;
  2. if IESDS leaves a single profile \(s^*\), then \(s^*\) is the unique NE.

The same holds with “rationalizable” in place of “survives IESDS”.

Proof sketch. (1) A strategy played in a NE is a best response to the others’ equilibrium strategies, and a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response; so no equilibrium strategy is removed at the first round, and by induction at any round. (2) By (1) any NE must be the survivor; the survivor is a NE because each component is the only remaining — hence best — response once all others are removed. \(\square\)

Nash equilibrium is thus a refinement of IESDS: whenever IESDS makes a prediction, NE makes the same one; NE often predicts where IESDS cannot.

Two requirements define a NE: (1) everyone best-responds to their beliefs; (2) everyone’s beliefs are correct. The first follows from rationality. The second is demanding — it requires some external reason for beliefs to coincide with actual play: convention, communication, learning, or an influential player that coordinates expectations.

3.4 Weak dominance

Write \(s_{-i} = (s_j)_{j \neq i}\).

\(s_i'\) weakly dominates \(s_i\) if \(u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\) for every \(s_{-i}\), with strict inequality for at least one \(s_{-i}\). Without the strictness requirement we just say \(s_i'\) dominates \(s_i\). A strategy that (strictly or weakly) dominates every other strategy of a player is (strictly or weakly) dominant.

If every player \(i\) has a dominant strategy \(s_i^*\), then \((s_1^*, \dots, s_n^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium. The converse is false.

Do not iteratively eliminate weakly dominated strategies. Add to Odds & Evens (with stakes \(5\)) a third strategy “Punch the opponent” (P), after which both get \(-5\):

0 1 P
0 −5, 5 5, −5 −5, −5
1 5, −5 −5, 5 −5, −5
P −5, −5 −5, −5 −5, −5

\(P\) is weakly dominated for both, yet \((P,P)\) is the only pure-strategy NE: no unilateral deviation helps. Eliminating \(P\) loses it. Weak elimination is also order-dependent.

3.5 Equilibrium vs. efficiency

Pareto efficiency and Nash equilibrium answer different questions:

  • Pareto efficiency: no way, in the whole game, to make someone better off without making someone worse off.
  • Nash equilibrium: no way for a single player to improve by a unilateral change.

The Prisoner’s Dilemma shows they can be disjoint: the unique NE \((F,F)\) is the only Pareto-inefficient profile. Inefficient equilibria arise because players are driven by self-interest alone. To quantify the cost of decentralised, selfish behaviour, assign each profile a social cost \(K(s)\), for instance the negative total welfare \(K(s) = -\sum_j u_j(s)\) or the min–max fairness cost \(K(s) = \max_j (-u_j(s))\).

The price of anarchy is the ratio between the social cost of the worst Nash equilibrium and the social optimum: \[\mathrm{PoA} = \frac{\max_{s^* \in \mathrm{NE}} K(s^*)}{\min_{s \in S} K(s)} .\] Using the best Nash equilibrium in the numerator gives the price of stability.

\(\mathrm{PoA} = 1\) means selfishness costs nothing; large values mean that a central planner would do much better. For important classes of games (selfish routing with linear latencies, Chapter 4) the PoA admits tight theoretical bounds.

An eccentric professor lets a game decide exam grades. You and an unknown classmate secretly pick an integer between \(18\) and \(30\). If the numbers match, both get that grade. Otherwise let \(L\) be the lower number: whoever proposed \(L\) gets \(L + R\), the other \(L - R\) (below \(18\) fails, above \(30\) is honours).

For any \(R > 1\) the unique NE is \((18, 18)\). At any common \(x > 18\), undercutting to \(x - 1\) yields \(x - 1 + R > x\); at \((18,18)\), raising yields \(18 - R\). Experimentally, with \(R = 2\) many students cooperate at high numbers — a well-known criticism of the rationality assumption — while a large \(R\) such as \(10\) dampens cooperation.

3.6 A field guide to small games

Most exercises reduce, after IESDS, to a \(2\times2\) game of one of a few types. Recognising the type gives the equilibria at once.

type structure pure NE mixed NE
Prisoner’s Dilemma a dominant strategy for each player, outcome Pareto dominated 1 none
Coordination (Battle of the Sexes, Stag Hunt) both want to match 2 (diagonal) 1
Anti-coordination (Chicken, Hawk–Dove, volunteer) both want to mismatch 2 (off-diagonal) 1
Discoordination (Matching Pennies) one wants to match, the other to mismatch 0 1

Larger games often hide one of these after dominated rows and columns are removed. With three or more players, the normal form is a table of tables: one matrix per action of the third player, and each cell lists three payoffs. A cell is a NE if each player’s payoff is maximal along their own direction: the column within the row (player 2), the row within the column (player 1), and the same cell across the matrices (player 3). Games with \(n\) symmetric players, such as public goods and volunteer’s dilemmas, are best described by a formula, \(u_i(s_i, k)\) depending on how many others choose each action.

3.7 Selecting among equilibria

When a game has several equilibria, Nash’s requirement that “beliefs are correct” does not say which beliefs. Several criteria are used in practice; none is part of the definition.

  • Focal points (Schelling, 1960). Asked to meet in New York without communicating, most people pick Grand Central at noon. Culture, labels and history make one equilibrium salient, and the salience itself makes it self-fulfilling.
  • Pareto dominance. If one equilibrium is better for everyone, players who can talk beforehand will agree on it, and the agreement is self-enforcing because it is a NE.
  • Risk dominance (Harsanyi and Selten, 1988). Pre-play talk may not convince when the efficient equilibrium is risky. Consider the Stag Hunt:
S H
S 4, 4 0, 3
H 3, 0 3, 3

\((S, S)\) is Pareto dominant, but \(S\) is a best response only if \(\Pr(\text{other plays } S) \geq 3/4\), while \(H\) guarantees \(3\). In a \(2\times2\) symmetric game, an equilibrium risk-dominates the other if the product of the players’ losses from unilateral deviation is larger: here \((4 - 3)^2 = 1 < 9 = (3 - 0)^2\), so \((H, H)\) is risk dominant. Experiments and learning dynamics often converge to the risk-dominant equilibrium. - Stability and learning. An equilibrium reached by best-response or fictitious-play dynamics from many starting points (Chapter 5) is more plausible as a long-run convention. Evolutionary game theory makes this precise.

Multiplicity is not a defect of the theory: many real situations (technology standards, driving on the left or right, bank runs) do have several self-fulfilling outcomes, and what selects among them is outside the game.

3.8 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapters 1 and 4. The mixed equilibria of these games are found in Chapter 5.

(BM 1.1) Jorah (J) and Khaleesi (K) each attack Meeren (M, value \(5\)) or Yunkai (Y, value \(3\)). An attacked city is always conquered. K’s utility is the total value conquered; J’s is the same plus \(10\) if they attack the same city. Write the normal form and find the pure NE.

Solution.

M Y
M 15, 5 8, 8
Y 8, 8 13, 3

J wants to match K (\(15 > 8\), \(13 > 8\)); K wants to mismatch (\(8 > 5\), \(8 > 3\)). Every cell has a player who regrets, so there is no pure-strategy NE: a discoordination game like Matching Pennies.

(BM 1.4) Two students choose a supervisor among Xavier, Yuan and Zingberry, worth \(40\), \(60\), \(50\). Choosing the same supervisor gives each only \(70\%\) of the value. Write the normal form and find the pure NE.

Solution.

X Y Z
X 28, 28 40, 60 40, 50
Y 60, 40 42, 42 60, 50
Z 50, 40 50, 60 35, 35

\(X\) is strictly dominated by \(Y\). In the reduced game the best response to \(Y\) is \(Z\) (\(50 > 42\)) and to \(Z\) is \(Y\) (\(60 > 35\)). The pure NE are \((Y, Z)\) and \((Z, Y)\): the students spread out.

(BM 1.8) Andrea books the first week and Brianna the second, each choosing Florence, Rome or Venice. One week in Florence and one in Rome (in either order) lets them tour Tuscany: utility \(3\) each. Both in Florence: Andrea \(1\), Brianna \(0\); both in Rome: Andrea \(0\), Brianna \(1\). Each week in Venice adds \(1\) to both. Find the pure NE.

Solution.

F R V
F 1, 0 3, 3 2, 1
R 3, 3 0, 1 1, 2
V 2, 1 1, 2 2, 2

Marking best responses gives three pure NE: \((F, R)\) and \((R, F)\) with payoffs \((3, 3)\), and \((V, V)\) with \((2, 2)\). The last is Pareto dominated but still self-enforcing: if the other goes to Venice, Tuscany is out of reach.

(BM 1.11) Siblings Agatha and Bruno each play with the old Train (T) or the New toy (N), while Mom simultaneously buys a Dollhouse (D) or Soldiers (S). If the kids do not play together, everyone gets \(0\). Together with T, each kid gets \(3\). Together with N, Agatha gets \(6\) (D) or \(1\) (S) and Bruno \(1\) (D) or \(4\) (S). Mom’s utility is the sum of the kids’. Find the pure NE.

Solution. Payoffs (Agatha, Bruno, Mom):

Mom D N T
N 6, 1, 7 0, 0, 0
T 0, 0, 0 3, 3, 6
Mom S N T
N 1, 4, 5 0, 0, 0
T 0, 0, 0 3, 3, 6

Mismatched profiles are never NE (each kid would rather join the other). \((T, T, D)\) and \((T, T, S)\) are NE: Mom’s choice is then irrelevant. \((N, N, D)\) is a NE, but \((N, N, S)\) is not, since Mom would switch to \(D\) (\(7 > 5\)). Three pure NE.

(BM 4.3) Jean (value \(600\)) and Kate (value \(450\)) each go to the boutique R (\(20\%\) off the \(250\) price) or the mall store S. Each shop has one bag; if both go to the same shop, a fair coin decides who gets it. Write the expected-utility normal form and find the pure NE.

Solution. Alone at R: Jean \(400\), Kate \(250\); alone at S: \(350\), \(200\); together, half of that.

R S
R 200, 125 400, 200
S 350, 250 175, 100

The pure NE are \((R, S)\) and \((S, R)\), an anti-coordination game. Note that the coin toss enters only through expected utility: a \(50\%\) chance at \(V - P\) is equivalent, for a vN-M player, to a sure \((V - P)/2\).

(BM 4.10, part 1) Find all NE of

X Y Z
A 2, 2 3, 1 0, 0
B 1, 6 7, 4 9, 4
C 0, 1 5, 3 9, 2

Solution. M’s best responses: \(A\) to \(X\), \(B\) to \(Y\), \(B\) and \(C\) to \(Z\). N’s: \(X\) to \(A\), \(X\) to \(B\), \(Y\) to \(C\). Only \((A, X)\) is marked twice, with payoffs \((2, 2)\). A support check confirms it is the only NE, mixed ones included, even though \((B, Y)\) and \((B, Z)\) are better for both. The sequential (Stackelberg) versions of this game are solved in Chapter 11.

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.11)** Siblings Agatha and Bruno each play with the old Train (T) or the New toy (N), while Mom simultaneously buys a Dollhouse (D) or Soldiers (S). If the kids do not play together, everyone gets $0$. Together with T, each kid gets $3$. Together with N, Agatha gets $6$ (D) or $1$ (S) and Bruno $1$ (D) or $4$ (S). Mom's utility is the sum of the kids'. Find the pure NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Payoffs (Agatha, Bruno, Mom):

| Mom D | N | T |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **N** | 6, 1, 7 | 0, 0, 0 |
| **T** | 0, 0, 0 | 3, 3, 6 |

| Mom S | N | T |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **N** | 1, 4, 5 | 0, 0, 0 |
| **T** | 0, 0, 0 | 3, 3, 6 |

Mismatched profiles are never NE (each kid would rather join the other). $(T, T, D)$ and $(T, T, S)$ are NE: Mom's choice is then irrelevant. $(N, N, D)$ is a NE, but $(N, N, S)$ is not, since Mom would switch to $D$ ($7 > 5$). Three pure NE.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.3)** Jean (value $600$) and Kate (value $450$) each go to the boutique R ($20\%$ off the $250$ price) or the mall store S. Each shop has one bag; if both go to the same shop, a fair coin decides who gets it. Write the expected-utility normal form and find the pure NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
Alone at R: Jean $400$, Kate $250$; alone at S: $350$, $200$; together, half of that.

| | R | S |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **R** | 200, 125 | 400, 200 |
| **S** | 350, 250 | 175, 100 |

The pure NE are $(R, S)$ and $(S, R)$, an anti-coordination game. Note that the coin toss enters only through expected utility: a $50\%$ chance at $V - P$ is equivalent, for a vN-M player, to a sure $(V - P)/2$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 4.10, part 1)** Find all NE of

| | X | Y | Z |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **A** | 2, 2 | 3, 1 | 0, 0 |
| **B** | 1, 6 | 7, 4 | 9, 4 |
| **C** | 0, 1 | 5, 3 | 9, 2 |
:::

::: {.solution}
M's best responses: $A$ to $X$, $B$ to $Y$, $B$ and $C$ to $Z$. N's: $X$ to $A$, $X$ to $B$, $Y$ to $C$. Only $(A, X)$ is marked twice, with payoffs $(2, 2)$. A support check confirms it is the *only* NE, mixed ones included, even though $(B, Y)$ and $(B, Z)$ are better for both. The sequential (Stackelberg) versions of this game are solved in Chapter 11.
:::
