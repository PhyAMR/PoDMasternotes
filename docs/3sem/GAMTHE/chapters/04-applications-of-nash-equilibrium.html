4  Applications of Nash equilibrium

Nash equilibrium is useful in two ways. As a prediction instrument, it tells us where a system of self-interested agents will settle (with care when equilibria are multiple). As a critique of competition, it shows that self-interest need not produce efficient outcomes. This chapter works out the classic models — Cournot and Bertrand duopolies, Hotelling’s location game, the tragedy of the commons — and closes with selfish routing, where the price of anarchy can be computed exactly.

4.1 Cournot duopoly

Cournot (1838) anticipated Nash’s idea a century early. Two firms produce quantities \(q_1, q_2 \geq 0\) of a homogeneous good, \(Q = q_1 + q_2\). Production costs \(C(q) = c\,q\) for both; the market-clearing price is \[P(Q) = \max(a - Q, 0), \qquad a > c .\] Each firm’s strategy set is \(S_i = [0, \infty)\) (quantities above \(a\) are pointless) and its payoff is its profit \[u_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i\bigl(P(q_i + q_j) - c\bigr) = q_i(a - q_i - q_j - c).\]

The unique Nash equilibrium is \[q_1^* = q_2^* = \frac{a - c}{3}, \qquad u_1^* = u_2^* = \frac{(a - c)^2}{9}.\]

Proof. Firm \(i\)’s best response maximises a concave parabola in \(q_i\): the first-order condition \(a - 2q_i - q_j - c = 0\) gives \[BR_i(q_j) = \frac{a - q_j - c}{2}.\] A NE is an intersection of the two best-response lines; solving the symmetric system \(q = (a - q - c)/2\) gives \(q = (a-c)/3\). \(\square\)

Monopoly benchmark. A single firm solves \(\max_q q(a - q - c)\), giving \(q^m = (a - c)/2\) and profit \(u^m = (a-c)^2/4\). The monopolist produces less than the two duopolists together (\(q^m < 2(a-c)/3\)); lower production means a higher price and higher total profit.

Why the firms do not collude. If each produced \(q^m/2 = (a-c)/4\) they would share \(u^m\), i.e. \((a-c)^2/8 > (a-c)^2/9\) each. But \(q^m/2\) is not a best response to \(q^m/2\): \(BR_i((a-c)/4) = 3(a-c)/8\). With a high price, unilaterally raising output increases one’s own revenue at the other’s expense, and the process continues until no gain is left — at the Cournot point. (Chapter 11 shows how repetition can sustain collusion.)

Asymmetric costs. If firm \(i\) has unit cost \(c_i\), the best responses become \(q_i = (a - c_i - q_j)/2\), and solving the pair gives \[q_i^* = \frac{a - 2c_i + c_j}{3}, \qquad P^* = \frac{a + c_1 + c_2}{3},\] valid while both quantities are positive. If \(a - 2c_i + c_j \leq 0\), the inefficient firm produces nothing and the other is a monopolist. The cheaper firm produces more and earns more, and a cost advantage of one firm lowers the rival’s output — strategic substitutes at work.

4.2 Bertrand duopoly

Bertrand (1883) objected that firms set prices, not quantities. Strategies are now \(p_i \in [0, \infty)\); consumers buy \(q = a - p\) from the cheaper firm (splitting equally at a tie); cost is \(c\,q\).

The unique Nash equilibrium is \(p_1^* = p_2^* = c\): both firms price at cost and earn zero profit.

Undercutting is always profitable while \(p > c\): a firm stealing the whole market by charging \(p - \varepsilon\) nearly doubles its profit. At \(p = c\) nobody can gain. Note that \((c + \varepsilon, c)\) is not a NE even though firm 1’s profit is \(0\) either way: firm 2, earning \(0\), could raise its price slightly and earn a positive profit.

The Bertrand outcome is great for consumers but unrealistic. Two classical fixes:

  • Imperfect substitutes. If \(q_i = a - p_i + b\,p_j\) with \(0 < b < 2\), profits are smooth in prices and the first-order conditions give the NE \(p_1^* = p_2^* = (a + c)/(2 - b) > c\).
  • Cost asymmetry. With \(c_1 = 1\), \(c_2 = 2\) and prices on a grid of step \(\varepsilon = 0.01\), firm 1 prices at \(1.99\) and takes the market: \((1.99, 2.00)\) is a NE. As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) the equilibrium disappears because payoffs are discontinuous — discretising the strategy space is a standard trick to avoid such pathologies.

4.3 Hotelling’s location game

Two ice-cream vendors choose a location on a 1 km boulevard, discretised into \(101\) points \(0, 1, \dots, 100\). Customers are uniformly spread and buy from the nearer vendor; prices are equal.

If A stands at \(22\) and B at \(35\), A serves everyone up to \(28\). A has an incentive to move right — best of all to \(36\), just beyond B, which captures every customer from \(36\) to \(100\). Iterating this reasoning, the unique NE is both vendors at \(50\). The result is the classical median voter theorem: in a two-party system, platforms converge to the political centre.

4.4 The tragedy of the commons

Since at least Hume (1739) it has been understood that citizens moved by private incentives misuse public resources (Hardin, 1968: “the tragedy of the commons”). An ICT version: \(n\) users share a Wi-Fi spectrum; user \(i\) activates \(g_i\) processes, \(G = \sum_i g_i\). Each process costs \(c\) and earns a throughput value \(v(G)\) that decreases with congestion: \(v'(G) < 0\), \(v''(G) < 0\), \(v(G_{\max}) = 0\). User \(i\)’s payoff is \(g_i\,(v(G) - c)\).

At the symmetric NE \(g_i^* = G^*/n\), \[v(G^*) + \frac{G^*}{n}\,v'(G^*) - c = 0,\] whereas the social optimum \(G^m\), maximising \(G\,(v(G) - c)\), satisfies \[v(G^m) + G^m\,v'(G^m) - c = 0 .\] Since \(v\) is decreasing and concave, \(G^* > G^m\): the resource is overused.

Interpretation. A user considering one more process compares its cost \(c\) with the marginal loss of value of its own \(g_i\) processes, \(v'(G)\,g_i = v'(G)\,G/n\). The planner counts the loss suffered by all processes, \(v'(G)\,G\). The factor \(1/n\) is the externality each user ignores.

4.5 Selfish routing

Pigou’s example (1920). One unit of traffic travels from \(s\) to \(d\) over two parallel links. The upper link’s cost per unit equals its load \(x\); the lower link has constant cost \(1\).

Figure: Pigou’s network.

The upper link is a dominant choice (its cost never exceeds \(1\)), so at equilibrium all traffic uses it and the total cost is \(1\). Splitting the traffic \(\tfrac12\)–\(\tfrac12\) gives total cost \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac34\), which is optimal — but every user on the lower link would deviate. Hence \[\mathrm{PoA} = \frac{1}{3/4} = \frac43 .\]

Nonlinear latencies. Replace the upper cost by \(x^a\) with \(a\) large. The equilibrium is unchanged (total cost \(1\)), while routing a small fraction \(\varepsilon\) on the lower link costs \((1 - \varepsilon)^{a+1} + \varepsilon \to 0\) as \(a \to \infty\) and \(\varepsilon \to 0\) suitably. The price of anarchy is unbounded.

Braess’s paradox. Adding capacity can make the equilibrium worse.

Figure: Braess’s network; the dashed link is the costless shortcut.

Without the dashed link, traffic splits evenly over the two symmetric routes and each unit pays \(\tfrac12 + 1 = \tfrac32\). With a costless shortcut \(r_1 \to r_2\), the route \(s \to r_1 \to r_2 \to d\) becomes dominant: everyone takes it and each unit pays \(1 + 0 + 1 = 2\). A “better” network has a worse Nash equilibrium.

For selfish routing with affine latencies on any network, the cost of a Nash flow is at most \(\tfrac43\) times the cost of the optimal flow. Pigou’s two-link network attains the bound.

The intuition: confronted with two choices, all selfish users take the momentarily better one and overload it. Remarkably, this inefficiency is intrinsic — the worst case does not depend on the topology.

Selfish routing is a template for many ICT problems: users choosing access points, channels or servers by their own latency. The Pigou bound tells a network designer how much is lost by letting users choose — and Braess’s paradox warns that adding a resource can backfire.

4.6 Strategic substitutes and complements

In games with continuous strategies the slope of the best-response functions summarises the strategic interaction.

  • Strategic substitutes: \(BR_i\) is decreasing. In Cournot, \(BR_i(q_j) = (a - c - q_j)/2\): the more the rival produces, the less I want to produce. Aggressive play by one player induces restraint by the other.
  • Strategic complements: \(BR_i\) is increasing. In Bertrand with differentiated products, \(BR_i(p_j) = (a + c + bp_j)/2\): a rival’s price rise lets me raise mine. Effort in joint projects and arms races behave similarly.

Formally, with smooth payoffs, the sign of \(\partial^2u_i/\partial s_i\partial s_j\) decides which case applies. The distinction matters for (i) multiplicity: with complements, best responses can cross several times (coordination failures, as in the investment game of the exercises); (ii) comparative statics: a shock that raises one player’s action raises (complements) or lowers (substitutes) the other’s; (iii) commitment in sequential versions (Chapter 9): a leader commits to being aggressive under substitutes but to being soft under complements.

If each \(BR_i\) is a function with \(|BR_i'| \leq k < 1\), the composite map \(BR = (BR_1, BR_2)\) is a contraction, so the Nash equilibrium exists, is unique, and best-response iteration converges to it from any start.

Cournot (\(k = 1/2\)) and Bertrand with \(b < 2\) (\(k = b/2\)) both satisfy it.

4.7 Voting: the median voter theorem

Hotelling’s model also describes elections. Voters are spread on a left–right axis \([-a, a]\) with distribution \(F\), and each votes for the candidate whose platform is nearest. Each of two office-seeking candidates chooses a platform to maximise the probability of winning.

If voter preferences are single-peaked on a line, the unique Nash equilibrium is that both candidates choose the platform of the median voter \(m\), \(F(m) = 1/2\).

Proof. If \(a_j \neq m\), candidate \(i\) wins for sure by locating strictly between \(a_j\) and \(m\), close enough to \(a_j\). The voters on the far side of the midpoint then include the median, i.e. more than half. So no profile with \(a_j \neq m\) is a NE. At \((m, m)\) each wins with probability \(1/2\), and any move away from \(m\) loses. \(\square\)

Convergence to the centre is a strong prediction. It fails when candidates care about policy, when abstention punishes moderation, when there are more than two candidates (with three, no pure equilibrium exists in the uniform case), or when the policy space has more than one dimension (majority cycles, Chapter 7).

4.8 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 1, and, for oligopoly and contests (not covered by the handbook), Tadelis (T), Chapter 5.

(BM 1.3) A transmitter either concentrates on one frequency \(f_0\) (C, power cost \(2\)) or spreads over the whole spectrum (S, cost \(9\)). The receiver listens on one frequency (A) or two (B), not knowing which one is \(f_0\). If the transmitter spreads, the receiver’s utility is \(2\) plus the number of frequencies used; if it concentrates, \(2\) minus that number. The transmitter gets twice the receiver’s utility, plus the number of frequencies used by the receiver, minus its power cost. Write the normal form and find the pure NE.

Solution.

A B
C 1, 1 0, 0
S −2, 3 1, 4

E.g. \((C, A)\): receiver \(2 - 1 = 1\), transmitter \(2 + 1 - 2 = 1\); \((S, B)\): receiver \(4\), transmitter \(8 + 2 - 9 = 1\). The pure NE are \((C, A)\) and \((S, B)\): a coordination problem between a cheap narrow-band and an expensive wide-band configuration. Without a protocol fixing the convention, the devices can miscoordinate.

(BM 1.7, continued) In the staffing game of Chapter 2 (row payoffs \((140, 160, 160)\), \((250, 40, 60)\), \((150, 150, -60)\) for \(0, 1, 2\) employees, symmetric), find the pure NE.

Solution. The best response to \(0\) is \(1\) (\(250\)); to \(1\) it is \(0\) (\(160\)); to \(2\) it is \(0\) (\(160\)). The pure NE are \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), with payoffs \(250\) and \(160\). Exactly one firm commits a worker, a “chicken” structure. The symmetric outcome \((0, 0)\), which would be fair, is not stable.

(BM 1.9) Two TV channels each choose to invest in the Morning, Daytime or Evening news. Same slot: \(0\) each. Otherwise the earlier slot wins (Evening counts as earlier than the next Morning), the loser gets \(-1\), and the winner gets \(1\), \(4\), \(2\) for M, D, E. Write the normal form. Is there a pure NE?

Solution.

M D E
M 0, 0 1, −1 −1, 2
D −1, 1 0, 0 4, −1
E 2, −1 −1, 4 0, 0

A weighted rock–paper–scissors: M beats D, D beats E, E beats M. Every pure profile has a player who would switch to the slot that beats the rival’s, so there is no pure NE. Nash’s theorem guarantees a mixed one (Chapter 5).

(BM 1.10) Grandma needs eggs. Each of three grandchildren decides whether to go to the store (G, cost \(1\)) or not (N). If at least one goes, everybody gets \(4\) from the cake; otherwise everybody gets \(0\). Write the normal form and find the pure NE.

Solution. \(u_i = 4\cdot\mathbb 1\{\text{someone goes}\} - \mathbb 1\{i \text{ goes}\}\). Going gives \(3\) for sure; staying gives \(4\) if someone else goes and \(0\) otherwise. The pure NE are the three profiles where exactly one grandchild goes: anyone else going would waste \(1\), and the one who goes would get \(0 < 3\) by staying. This is the volunteer’s dilemma. The equilibria are asymmetric, and who volunteers is not determined by the game.

(T 5.8, \(n\)-firm Cournot) \(n\) firms with cost \(cq_i\) face \(P(Q) = a - Q\). Find the Cournot equilibrium and the limit of the price as \(n \to \infty\).

Solution. Firm \(i\) maximises \(q_i(a - Q_{-i} - q_i - c)\): \(q_i = (a - c - Q_{-i})/2\). In the symmetric equilibrium \(q = (a - c - (n-1)q)/2\), so \(q^* = \frac{a - c}{n + 1}\), \(Q^* = \frac{n(a - c)}{n + 1}\), \(P^* = \frac{a + nc}{n + 1}\) and each profit is \(\frac{(a - c)^2}{(n + 1)^2}\). As \(n \to \infty\), \(P^* \to c\): the competitive outcome. Cournot spans the range from monopoly (\(n = 1\)) to perfect competition.

(T 5.11, Wasteful Shipping Costs) Two countries each have demand \(p = 90 - q\) and one coal firm with unit cost \(10\). (a) Without trade, find the outputs. (b) With free trade and shipping cost \(10\) per unit exported, find the NE. (c) Firm 1 can trigger a regulation raising shipping costs to \(40\). Will it?

Solution.

  1. Monopoly in each country: \(q = 40\), \(p = 50\), profit \(1600\). (b) The markets are separate Cournot games with costs \(10\) (home) and \(20\) (foreign). With asymmetric costs \(q_i = (a - 2c_i + c_j)/3\): home sells \(30\), foreign \(20\), \(p = 40\). Profits are \(900 + 400 = 1300\) per firm, and the equilibrium is unique. Consumers gain, firms lose, and \(20 \times 10\) per country is wasted on shipping goods that cross in opposite directions. (c) With foreign cost \(50\), the foreign firm’s best response to the monopoly output \(40\) is \((90 - 50 - 40)/2 = 0\). Trade dies and each firm is back to \(1600 > 1300\). Firm 1 releases the report.

(T 5.18, Political Campaigning) Candidate \(i\) spends \(s_i \geq 0\) and wins with probability \(s_i/(s_1 + s_2)\); winning is worth \(v\). Find the best responses and the NE. What if candidate 2 values winning at \(kv\), \(k > 1\)?

Solution. \(u_i = v\frac{s_i}{s_1 + s_2} - s_i\); the FOC \(v\frac{s_j}{(s_1 + s_2)^2} = 1\) gives \(BR_i(s_j) = \sqrt{vs_j} - s_j\). Symmetric NE: \(s^* = v/4\), increasing linearly in \(v\). Total spending dissipates half the prize. With values \(v\) and \(kv\) the FOCs give \(vs_2 = kvs_1\), so \(s_2 = ks_1\), and \(s_1^* = \frac{kv}{(1 + k)^2}\), \(s_2^* = \frac{k^2v}{(1 + k)^2}\). The candidate who cares more spends \(k\) times more and wins with probability \(k/(1 + k)\). This is the Tullock contest, a model of lobbying and rent-seeking.

Nash equilibrium is useful in two ways. As a **prediction instrument**, it tells us where a system of self-interested agents will settle (with care when equilibria are multiple). As a **critique of competition**, it shows that self-interest need not produce efficient outcomes. This chapter works out the classic models — Cournot and Bertrand duopolies, Hotelling's location game, the tragedy of the commons — and closes with selfish routing, where the price of anarchy can be computed exactly.

## Cournot duopoly

Cournot (1838) anticipated Nash's idea a century early. Two firms produce quantities $q_1, q_2 \geq 0$ of a homogeneous good, $Q = q_1 + q_2$. Production costs $C(q) = c\,q$ for both; the market-clearing price is
$$P(Q) = \max(a - Q, 0), \qquad a > c .$$
Each firm's strategy set is $S_i = [0, \infty)$ (quantities above $a$ are pointless) and its payoff is its profit
$$u_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i\bigl(P(q_i + q_j) - c\bigr) = q_i(a - q_i - q_j - c).$$

::: {.proposition name="Cournot–Nash equilibrium"}
The unique Nash equilibrium is
$$q_1^* = q_2^* = \frac{a - c}{3}, \qquad u_1^* = u_2^* = \frac{(a - c)^2}{9}.$$
:::

*Proof.* Firm $i$'s best response maximises a concave parabola in $q_i$: the first-order condition $a - 2q_i - q_j - c = 0$ gives
$$BR_i(q_j) = \frac{a - q_j - c}{2}.$$
A NE is an intersection of the two best-response lines; solving the symmetric system $q = (a - q - c)/2$ gives $q = (a-c)/3$. $\square$

**Monopoly benchmark.** A single firm solves $\max_q q(a - q - c)$, giving $q^m = (a - c)/2$ and profit $u^m = (a-c)^2/4$. The monopolist produces *less* than the two duopolists together ($q^m < 2(a-c)/3$); lower production means a higher price and higher total profit.

**Why the firms do not collude.** If each produced $q^m/2 = (a-c)/4$ they would share $u^m$, i.e. $(a-c)^2/8 > (a-c)^2/9$ each. But $q^m/2$ is not a best response to $q^m/2$: $BR_i((a-c)/4) = 3(a-c)/8$. With a high price, unilaterally raising output increases one's own revenue at the other's expense, and the process continues until no gain is left — at the Cournot point. (Chapter 11 shows how repetition can sustain collusion.)

**Asymmetric costs.** If firm $i$ has unit cost $c_i$, the best responses become $q_i = (a - c_i - q_j)/2$, and solving the pair gives
$$q_i^* = \frac{a - 2c_i + c_j}{3}, \qquad P^* = \frac{a + c_1 + c_2}{3},$$
valid while both quantities are positive. If $a - 2c_i + c_j \leq 0$, the inefficient firm produces nothing and the other is a monopolist. The cheaper firm produces more and earns more, and a cost advantage of one firm *lowers* the rival's output — strategic substitutes at work.

## Bertrand duopoly

Bertrand (1883) objected that firms set *prices*, not quantities. Strategies are now $p_i \in [0, \infty)$; consumers buy $q = a - p$ from the cheaper firm (splitting equally at a tie); cost is $c\,q$.

::: {.proposition name="Bertrand paradox"}
The unique Nash equilibrium is $p_1^* = p_2^* = c$: both firms price at cost and earn zero profit.
:::

Undercutting is always profitable while $p > c$: a firm stealing the whole market by charging $p - \varepsilon$ nearly doubles its profit. At $p = c$ nobody can gain. Note that $(c + \varepsilon, c)$ is *not* a NE even though firm 1's profit is $0$ either way: firm 2, earning $0$, could raise its price slightly and earn a positive profit.

The Bertrand outcome is great for consumers but unrealistic. Two classical fixes:

- **Imperfect substitutes.** If $q_i = a - p_i + b\,p_j$ with $0 < b < 2$, profits are smooth in prices and the first-order conditions give the NE $p_1^* = p_2^* = (a + c)/(2 - b) > c$.
- **Cost asymmetry.** With $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = 2$ and prices on a grid of step $\varepsilon = 0.01$, firm 1 prices at $1.99$ and takes the market: $(1.99, 2.00)$ is a NE. As $\varepsilon \to 0$ the equilibrium disappears because payoffs are discontinuous — discretising the strategy space is a standard trick to avoid such pathologies.

## Hotelling's location game

Two ice-cream vendors choose a location on a 1 km boulevard, discretised into $101$ points $0, 1, \dots, 100$. Customers are uniformly spread and buy from the nearer vendor; prices are equal.

If A stands at $22$ and B at $35$, A serves everyone up to $28$. A has an incentive to move right — best of all to $36$, just beyond B, which captures every customer from $36$ to $100$. Iterating this reasoning, the unique NE is **both vendors at $50$**. The result is the classical *median voter theorem*: in a two-party system, platforms converge to the political centre.

## The tragedy of the commons

Since at least Hume (1739) it has been understood that citizens moved by private incentives misuse public resources (Hardin, 1968: "the tragedy of the commons"). An ICT version: $n$ users share a Wi-Fi spectrum; user $i$ activates $g_i$ processes, $G = \sum_i g_i$. Each process costs $c$ and earns a throughput value $v(G)$ that decreases with congestion: $v'(G) < 0$, $v''(G) < 0$, $v(G_{\max}) = 0$. User $i$'s payoff is $g_i\,(v(G) - c)$.

::: {.proposition name="Overuse in equilibrium"}
At the symmetric NE $g_i^* = G^*/n$,
$$v(G^*) + \frac{G^*}{n}\,v'(G^*) - c = 0,$$
whereas the social optimum $G^m$, maximising $G\,(v(G) - c)$, satisfies
$$v(G^m) + G^m\,v'(G^m) - c = 0 .$$
Since $v$ is decreasing and concave, $G^* > G^m$: the resource is overused.
:::

*Interpretation.* A user considering one more process compares its cost $c$ with the marginal loss of value of *its own* $g_i$ processes, $v'(G)\,g_i = v'(G)\,G/n$. The planner counts the loss suffered by *all* processes, $v'(G)\,G$. The factor $1/n$ is the externality each user ignores.

## Selfish routing

**Pigou's example (1920).** One unit of traffic travels from $s$ to $d$ over two parallel links. The upper link's cost per unit equals its load $x$; the lower link has constant cost $1$.

```{=latex}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small, line width=0.4pt, >=stealth]
  \node[circle, draw, inner sep=1.5pt] (s) at (0,0) {$s$};
  \node[circle, draw, inner sep=1.5pt] (d) at (3.2,0) {$d$};
  \draw[->] (s) to[bend left=40]  node[above] {$c(x) = x$} (d);
  \draw[->] (s) to[bend right=40] node[below] {$c(x) = 1$} (d);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
```

*Figure: Pigou's network.*

The upper link is a dominant choice (its cost never exceeds $1$), so at equilibrium all traffic uses it and the total cost is $1$. Splitting the traffic $\tfrac12$–$\tfrac12$ gives total cost $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac34$, which is optimal — but every user on the lower link would deviate. Hence
$$\mathrm{PoA} = \frac{1}{3/4} = \frac43 .$$

**Nonlinear latencies.** Replace the upper cost by $x^a$ with $a$ large. The equilibrium is unchanged (total cost $1$), while routing a small fraction $\varepsilon$ on the lower link costs $(1 - \varepsilon)^{a+1} + \varepsilon \to 0$ as $a \to \infty$ and $\varepsilon \to 0$ suitably. The price of anarchy is **unbounded**.

**Braess's paradox.** Adding capacity can make the equilibrium *worse*.

```{=latex}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small, line width=0.4pt, >=stealth]
  \node[circle, draw, inner sep=1.5pt] (s) at (0,0) {$s$};
  \node[circle, draw, inner sep=1.2pt] (a) at (1.8,1.1) {$r_1$};
  \node[circle, draw, inner sep=1.2pt] (b) at (1.8,-1.1) {$r_2$};
  \node[circle, draw, inner sep=1.5pt] (d) at (3.6,0) {$d$};
  \draw[->] (s) -- node[above left]  {$x$} (a);
  \draw[->] (a) -- node[above right] {$1$} (d);
  \draw[->] (s) -- node[below left]  {$1$} (b);
  \draw[->] (b) -- node[below right] {$x$} (d);
  \draw[->, dashed] (a) -- node[right] {$0$} (b);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
```

*Figure: Braess's network; the dashed link is the costless shortcut.*

Without the dashed link, traffic splits evenly over the two symmetric routes and each unit pays $\tfrac12 + 1 = \tfrac32$. With a costless shortcut $r_1 \to r_2$, the route $s \to r_1 \to r_2 \to d$ becomes dominant: everyone takes it and each unit pays $1 + 0 + 1 = 2$. A "better" network has a worse Nash equilibrium.

::: {.theorem name="Roughgarden–Tardos"}
For selfish routing with **affine latencies** on any network, the cost of a Nash flow is at most $\tfrac43$ times the cost of the optimal flow. Pigou's two-link network attains the bound.
:::

The intuition: confronted with two choices, all selfish users take the momentarily better one and overload it. Remarkably, this inefficiency is intrinsic — the worst case does not depend on the topology.

::: {.callout-tip}
Selfish routing is a template for many ICT problems: users choosing access points, channels or servers by their own latency. The Pigou bound tells a network designer how much is lost by letting users choose — and Braess's paradox warns that adding a resource can backfire.
:::

## Strategic substitutes and complements

In games with continuous strategies the slope of the best-response functions summarises the strategic interaction.

- **Strategic substitutes:** $BR_i$ is *decreasing*. In Cournot, $BR_i(q_j) = (a - c - q_j)/2$: the more the rival produces, the less I want to produce. Aggressive play by one player induces restraint by the other.
- **Strategic complements:** $BR_i$ is *increasing*. In Bertrand with differentiated products, $BR_i(p_j) = (a + c + bp_j)/2$: a rival's price rise lets me raise mine. Effort in joint projects and arms races behave similarly.

Formally, with smooth payoffs, the sign of $\partial^2u_i/\partial s_i\partial s_j$ decides which case applies. The distinction matters for (i) **multiplicity**: with complements, best responses can cross several times (coordination failures, as in the investment game of the exercises); (ii) **comparative statics**: a shock that raises one player's action raises (complements) or lowers (substitutes) the other's; (iii) **commitment** in sequential versions (Chapter 9): a leader commits to being aggressive under substitutes but to being soft under complements.

::: {.proposition name="Uniqueness by contraction"}
If each $BR_i$ is a function with $|BR_i'| \leq k < 1$, the composite map $BR = (BR_1, BR_2)$ is a contraction, so the Nash equilibrium exists, is unique, and best-response iteration converges to it from any start.
:::

Cournot ($k = 1/2$) and Bertrand with $b < 2$ ($k = b/2$) both satisfy it.

## Voting: the median voter theorem

Hotelling's model also describes elections. Voters are spread on a left–right axis $[-a, a]$ with distribution $F$, and each votes for the candidate whose platform is nearest. Each of two office-seeking candidates chooses a platform to maximise the probability of winning.

::: {.theorem name="Median voter theorem"}
If voter preferences are single-peaked on a line, the unique Nash equilibrium is that both candidates choose the platform of the **median voter** $m$, $F(m) = 1/2$.
:::

*Proof.* If $a_j \neq m$, candidate $i$ wins for sure by locating strictly between $a_j$ and $m$, close enough to $a_j$. The voters on the far side of the midpoint then include the median, i.e. more than half. So no profile with $a_j \neq m$ is a NE. At $(m, m)$ each wins with probability $1/2$, and any move away from $m$ loses. $\square$

Convergence to the centre is a strong prediction. It fails when candidates care about policy, when abstention punishes moderation, when there are more than two candidates (with three, no pure equilibrium exists in the uniform case), or when the policy space has more than one dimension (majority cycles, Chapter 7).

## Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapter 1, and, for oligopoly and contests (not covered by the handbook), Tadelis (T), Chapter 5.

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.3)** A transmitter either concentrates on one frequency $f_0$ (C, power cost $2$) or spreads over the whole spectrum (S, cost $9$). The receiver listens on one frequency (A) or two (B), not knowing which one is $f_0$. If the transmitter spreads, the receiver's utility is $2$ plus the number of frequencies used; if it concentrates, $2$ minus that number. The transmitter gets twice the receiver's utility, plus the number of frequencies used by the receiver, minus its power cost. Write the normal form and find the pure NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
| | A | B |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **C** | 1, 1 | 0, 0 |
| **S** | −2, 3 | 1, 4 |

E.g. $(C, A)$: receiver $2 - 1 = 1$, transmitter $2 + 1 - 2 = 1$; $(S, B)$: receiver $4$, transmitter $8 + 2 - 9 = 1$. The pure NE are $(C, A)$ and $(S, B)$: a coordination problem between a cheap narrow-band and an expensive wide-band configuration. Without a protocol fixing the convention, the devices can miscoordinate.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.7, continued)** In the staffing game of Chapter 2 (row payoffs $(140, 160, 160)$, $(250, 40, 60)$, $(150, 150, -60)$ for $0, 1, 2$ employees, symmetric), find the pure NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
The best response to $0$ is $1$ ($250$); to $1$ it is $0$ ($160$); to $2$ it is $0$ ($160$). The pure NE are $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, with payoffs $250$ and $160$. Exactly one firm commits a worker, a "chicken" structure. The symmetric outcome $(0, 0)$, which would be fair, is not stable.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.9)** Two TV channels each choose to invest in the Morning, Daytime or Evening news. Same slot: $0$ each. Otherwise the earlier slot wins (Evening counts as earlier than the next Morning), the loser gets $-1$, and the winner gets $1$, $4$, $2$ for M, D, E. Write the normal form. Is there a pure NE?
:::

::: {.solution}
| | M | D | E |
|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| **M** | 0, 0 | 1, −1 | −1, 2 |
| **D** | −1, 1 | 0, 0 | 4, −1 |
| **E** | 2, −1 | −1, 4 | 0, 0 |

A weighted rock–paper–scissors: M beats D, D beats E, E beats M. Every pure profile has a player who would switch to the slot that beats the rival's, so there is no pure NE. Nash's theorem guarantees a mixed one (Chapter 5).
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(BM 1.10)** Grandma needs eggs. Each of three grandchildren decides whether to go to the store (G, cost $1$) or not (N). If at least one goes, everybody gets $4$ from the cake; otherwise everybody gets $0$. Write the normal form and find the pure NE.
:::

::: {.solution}
$u_i = 4\cdot\mathbb 1\{\text{someone goes}\} - \mathbb 1\{i \text{ goes}\}$. Going gives $3$ for sure; staying gives $4$ if someone else goes and $0$ otherwise. The pure NE are the three profiles where **exactly one** grandchild goes: anyone else going would waste $1$, and the one who goes would get $0 < 3$ by staying. This is the *volunteer's dilemma*. The equilibria are asymmetric, and who volunteers is not determined by the game.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 5.8, $n$-firm Cournot)** $n$ firms with cost $cq_i$ face $P(Q) = a - Q$. Find the Cournot equilibrium and the limit of the price as $n \to \infty$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Firm $i$ maximises $q_i(a - Q_{-i} - q_i - c)$: $q_i = (a - c - Q_{-i})/2$. In the symmetric equilibrium $q = (a - c - (n-1)q)/2$, so $q^* = \frac{a - c}{n + 1}$, $Q^* = \frac{n(a - c)}{n + 1}$, $P^* = \frac{a + nc}{n + 1}$ and each profit is $\frac{(a - c)^2}{(n + 1)^2}$. As $n \to \infty$, $P^* \to c$: the competitive outcome. Cournot spans the range from monopoly ($n = 1$) to perfect competition.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 5.11, Wasteful Shipping Costs)** Two countries each have demand $p = 90 - q$ and one coal firm with unit cost $10$. (a) Without trade, find the outputs. (b) With free trade and shipping cost $10$ per unit exported, find the NE. (c) Firm 1 can trigger a regulation raising shipping costs to $40$. Will it?
:::

::: {.solution}
(a) Monopoly in each country: $q = 40$, $p = 50$, profit $1600$. (b) The markets are separate Cournot games with costs $10$ (home) and $20$ (foreign). With asymmetric costs $q_i = (a - 2c_i + c_j)/3$: home sells $30$, foreign $20$, $p = 40$. Profits are $900 + 400 = 1300$ per firm, and the equilibrium is unique. Consumers gain, firms lose, and $20 \times 10$ per country is wasted on shipping goods that cross in opposite directions. (c) With foreign cost $50$, the foreign firm's best response to the monopoly output $40$ is $(90 - 50 - 40)/2 = 0$. Trade dies and each firm is back to $1600 > 1300$. Firm 1 releases the report.
:::

::: {.exercise}
**(T 5.18, Political Campaigning)** Candidate $i$ spends $s_i \geq 0$ and wins with probability $s_i/(s_1 + s_2)$; winning is worth $v$. Find the best responses and the NE. What if candidate 2 values winning at $kv$, $k > 1$?
:::

::: {.solution}
$u_i = v\frac{s_i}{s_1 + s_2} - s_i$; the FOC $v\frac{s_j}{(s_1 + s_2)^2} = 1$ gives $BR_i(s_j) = \sqrt{vs_j} - s_j$. Symmetric NE: $s^* = v/4$, increasing linearly in $v$. Total spending dissipates half the prize. With values $v$ and $kv$ the FOCs give $vs_2 = kvs_1$, so $s_2 = ks_1$, and $s_1^* = \frac{kv}{(1 + k)^2}$, $s_2^* = \frac{k^2v}{(1 + k)^2}$. The candidate who cares more spends $k$ times more and wins with probability $k/(1 + k)$. This is the Tullock contest, a model of lobbying and rent-seeking.
:::
