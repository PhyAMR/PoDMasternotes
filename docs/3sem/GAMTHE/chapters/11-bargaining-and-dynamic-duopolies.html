11  Bargaining and dynamic duopolies

Two important economic interactions unfold over time. In bargaining, players alternate offers to split a surplus that shrinks while they argue. In dynamic oligopoly, firms either move in sequence (Stackelberg) or compete repeatedly, which opens the door to tacit collusion. Both are solved with the tools of Chapters 9–10: backward induction, subgame perfection, and trigger strategies.

11.1 Alternating-offer bargaining

Two players must split a pie of size \(1\): player 1 gets \(x\), player 2 gets \(1 - x\). There are two approaches: the axiomatic Nash bargaining solution (Chapter 15) and the strategic model below (Ståhl, Rubinstein).

At stage \(t = 1, 2, \dots\) a proposer offers a split and a responder accepts (the game ends) or refuses (the game moves to stage \(t+1\)). Player 1 proposes at odd stages, player 2 at even stages. If agreement on \((x, 1-x)\) is reached at stage \(t\), payoffs are \((\delta^{t-1} x, \delta^{t-1}(1-x))\) with \(0 < \delta < 1\): each round of delay wastes a fraction \(1 - \delta\) of the pie. If no agreement is reached by the deadline \(T\), both get \(0\).

This is not a repeated game: an acceptance terminates it.

Deadline \(T = 1\) is the ultimatum game: many NE, but the unique SPE has the proposer keeping everything (\(x = 1\)) and the responder accepting.

Odd deadline \(T\). Player 1 makes the last offer; at stage \(T\) player 2 accepts anything, so player 1 takes the whole pie. At stage \(T-1\), player 2 knows that refusing gives player 1 \(\delta\) (in stage-\((T-1)\) units), so offers exactly \(x = \delta\), which is accepted. Iterating backwards, the SPE has immediate agreement with \[u_1 = \frac{1 + \delta^T}{1 + \delta}, \qquad u_2 = \frac{\delta - \delta^T}{1 + \delta}.\]

In every SPE of the finite-horizon alternating-offer game, agreement is reached in the first round.

Delay is doubly costly: it shrinks the pie and merely leads to another, symmetric round of proposer–responder interaction, which rational players anticipate.

With an infinite horizon the alternating-offer game has a unique SPE: player 1 offers and player 2 immediately accepts \[u_1 = \frac{1}{1 + \delta}, \qquad u_2 = \frac{\delta}{1 + \delta}.\]

The limit \(T \to \infty\) of the finite formulas gives the same numbers, but uniqueness must be proved without backward induction. Proof sketch (Shaked–Sutton). Every subgame starting with a proposal is a copy of the whole game with the roles possibly swapped, so let \(M\) and \(m\) be the supremum and infimum of the proposer’s SPE payoff. A responder who refuses becomes the next proposer, worth between \(\delta m\) and \(\delta M\) today. Hence the responder accepts any offer above \(\delta M\), so the proposer gets at least \(1 - \delta M\): \(m \geq 1 - \delta M\). And the responder never accepts less than \(\delta m\), so \(M \leq 1 - \delta m\). Combining, \(M \leq 1 - \delta m \leq 1 - \delta(1 - \delta M)\), i.e. \(M(1 - \delta^2) \leq 1 - \delta\), so \(M \leq 1/(1+\delta)\); symmetrically \(m \geq 1/(1+\delta)\). Thus \(M = m = 1/(1+\delta)\). \(\square\)

The first-mover advantage \(1/(1+\delta) - \delta/(1+\delta) = (1 - \delta)/(1 + \delta)\) vanishes as \(\delta \to 1\): with patient players the split tends to \(50/50\).

11.2 Bargaining power: patience and proposal rights

The Rubinstein argument generalises when players discount differently, with factors \(\delta_1, \delta_2\). Let \(x\) be what player 1 gets when proposing and \(y\) what player 2 gets when proposing. In the unique SPE each proposer offers the responder exactly the discounted value of becoming proposer next round: \[1 - x = \delta_2\,y, \qquad 1 - y = \delta_1\,x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{1 - \delta_2}{1 - \delta_1\delta_2}, \qquad 1 - x = \frac{\delta_2(1 - \delta_1)}{1 - \delta_1\delta_2}.\] Patience is power: as \(\delta_2 \to 1\) with \(\delta_1\) fixed, player 2 takes almost everything, even though player 1 proposes first. If both \(\delta_i = e^{-r_i\Delta}\) and the time between offers \(\Delta \to 0\), player 1’s share tends to \(\frac{r_2}{r_1 + r_2}\): the asymmetric Nash bargaining solution of Chapter 15 with weights inversely proportional to the impatience rates.

Legislative bargaining (Baron and Ferejohn, 1989). \(n\) legislators (odd) divide a budget by majority rule. Each round a randomly chosen member proposes a division; if a majority accepts, it is implemented; otherwise a new proposer is drawn and the budget is discounted by \(\delta\). In the stationary SPE the proposer buys the cheapest majority: offers \(\delta/n\) (the continuation value) to \((n-1)/2\) others, gives nothing to the rest, and keeps \[1 - \frac{n-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\delta}{n}.\] Agreement is immediate, the proposer captures much more than an equal share, and minimum winning coalitions form — features observed in real legislatures and committees.

Stationary strategies and wars of attrition. In infinite-horizon games whose subgames are scaled copies of the whole game (all Rubinstein-like problems), one looks for stationary equilibria: the action depends only on the current role (proposer or responder, mover or waiter), not on the date. The value of each role then solves a pair of equations like those above. When ending the game benefits the other player more than yourself, the game is a war of attrition: each wants the other to concede. Then there are typically two asymmetric pure equilibria (one side always concedes) and a mixed one in which each concedes with a constant probability per period, so delay is wasteful but possible.

11.3 Stackelberg duopoly

Return to the Cournot setting (Chapter 4: price \(a - Q\), unit cost \(c\)), but now a leader (firm 1) chooses \(q_1\) first and a follower (firm 2) observes it before choosing \(q_2\).

By backward induction the follower plays its best response \[R_2(q_1) = \frac{a - q_1 - c}{2},\] and the leader maximises \(u_1(q_1, R_2(q_1)) = q_1\bigl(a - q_1 - R_2(q_1) - c\bigr) = q_1 (a - q_1 - c)/2\), giving \[q_1^* = \frac{a-c}{2}, \qquad q_2^* = \frac{a-c}{4}, \qquad u_1^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{8}, \qquad u_2^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{16}.\] Compared with Cournot (\(q = (a-c)/3\) each, profit \((a-c)^2/9\)), the leader produces more and earns more; the follower produces and earns less. Total output \(3(a-c)/4\) exceeds Cournot’s \(2(a-c)/3\).

Remark (Threats and information). The follower might threaten “choose the Cournot quantity or I will flood the market”. The threat is non-credible: flooding hurts the follower too. More interesting is the role of information. If the follower moved without observing \(q_1\), a chain of anticipations (“the leader will play \((a-c)/2\), so I play \((a-c)/4\), so the leader should play \(3(a-c)/8\), …”) leads back to Cournot. It is precisely the follower’s observation of \(q_1\) — common knowledge to both — that hands the leader the first-mover advantage. In multi-agent problems more information can make a player worse off.

11.4 Collusion in a repeated Cournot duopoly

At the Cournot equilibrium each firm earns \(u^C = (a-c)^2/9\), while sharing the monopoly output would give each \(u^m/2 = (a-c)^2/8\). The one-shot game cannot sustain this, but by the folk theorem an infinitely repeated one can.

Consider the strategy: produce \(q^m/2 = (a-c)/4\) at \(t = 1\) and as long as both firms have always done so; after any deviation produce the Cournot quantity \(q^C = (a-c)/3\) forever. Both firms using it is an SPE iff \(\delta \geq 9/17\).

Proof. After a deviation, \((q^C, q^C)\) forever is a NE of every subgame. On the cooperative path, the most profitable one-shot deviation against \(q^m/2\) is \(\arg\max_q q(a - q - (a-c)/4 - c) = 3(a-c)/8\), earning \(u^D = 9(a-c)^2/64\). Cooperation is optimal iff \[\frac{(a-c)^2/8}{1 - \delta} \geq \frac{9(a-c)^2}{64} + \frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\,\frac{(a-c)^2}{9} \iff \delta \geq \frac{9}{17}. \qquad \square\]

Less ambitious collusion. For \(\delta < 9/17\) the firms can still beat Cournot by targeting some \(q^* \in [q^m/2, q^C]\) with a grim trigger. Against \(q^*\), the best deviation \(q^D = (a - q^* - c)/2\) earns \(u^D = (a - q^* - c)^2/4\), while cooperation earns \(u^* = q^*(a - 2q^* - c)\) per period. The smallest sustainable \(q^*\) makes the incentive constraint \(u^*/(1-\delta) \geq u^D + \delta u^C/(1-\delta)\) binding: \[q^*(\delta) = \frac{(a - c)(9 - 5\delta)}{3(9 - \delta)},\] which moves continuously from \(q^C\) at \(\delta = 0\) to \(q^m/2\) at \(\delta = 9/17\).

Carrot and stick. Unlike the Prisoner’s Dilemma, the Cournot game offers punishments harsher than reverting to the NE. Abreu’s two-phase strategy uses a reward action \(R\) (produce \(q^m/2\)) and a punishment action \(P\) (produce some \(x > q^C\)):

  • start with \(R\);
  • play \(R\) if both played \(R\) in the previous period, or if both played \(P\);
  • otherwise play \(P\).

A deviator is punished for one period with low prices, and the punishment itself is enforced because deviating from it restarts it. One can check that with \(\delta = \tfrac12\) and \(x = 2(a-c)/5\) the strategy is an SPE: the monopoly split is sustained even though \(\tfrac12 < 9/17\).

Competition authorities fine firms that meet to fix prices or quantities. The repeated-game analysis shows why this is not enough: a grim-trigger agreement needs no meeting and no communication. It can be reached “in the CEO’s head”, as a mutually understood equilibrium of the repeated game — tacit collusion.

11.5 Exercises

Problems from Badia and Marchioro (BM), Chapters 3 and 4.

(BM 3.10) Amelie (odd days) and Bernard (even days) work on a project worth \(v_1\) on day 1 and \(v_{j+1} = \delta v_j\) afterwards. On their day, the mover either Calls the end (C), getting \(0.4v_j\) while the other gets \(0.6v_j\), or Keeps working (K). Show that in any stationary SPE the project ends by day 2, and find for which \(\delta\) Amelie ends it.

Solution. Every subgame starting on an odd (even) day is the day-1 game scaled by \(\delta^{j-1}\), with roles swapped on even days. So a stationary strategy depends only on whether one is the mover. If the mover’s rule were “keep” for both, payoffs would be \(0 < 0.4\), so somebody calls by day 2. Amelie compares \(0.4v_1\) (call now) with \(0.6\delta v_1\) (Bernard calls tomorrow). For \(\delta < 2/3\) calling is better for every mover, and the unique SPE is that Amelie ends the project on day 1. For \(\delta > 2/3\) being the non-caller is worth waiting for, and there are two pure stationary SPE: Amelie always keeps and Bernard always calls (the project ends on day 2), or Bernard always keeps and Amelie always calls (it ends on day 1). This is a war of attrition, in which each wants the other to concede. (The printed solution states the inequality the wrong way round.)

(BM 4.10, parts 2–3) In the game below (static NE \((A, X)\), Chapter 3), find the Stackelberg outcome when M leads, and when N leads (break ties generously: a follower favours the leader, and a leader favours the follower).

X Y Z
A 2, 2 3, 1 0, 0
B 1, 6 7, 4 9, 4
C 0, 1 5, 3 9, 2

Solution. M leads. N answers \(A \to X\), \(B \to X\), \(C \to Y\), so M compares \(2\), \(1\), \(5\) and plays \(C\). Outcome \((C, Y)\) with \((5, 3)\). Both gain over the static NE \((2, 2)\): the leader never does worse than a NE (it could replay \(A\)), and here the follower also gets more than its pure minmax \(2\). N leads. M answers \(X \to A\), \(Y \to B\), and \(Z \to B\) or \(C\) (tie at \(9\); the generous follower picks \(B\), which gives N \(4\) rather than \(2\)). N then compares \(2\) (\(X\)), \(4\) (\(Y\)), \(4\) (\(Z\)). The tie is broken generously toward \(Z\), which gives M \(9\) instead of \(7\). Outcome \((B, Z)\) with \((9, 4)\). Sequential play removes the coordination failure of the static game.

From the course material:

In the alternating-offer game with deadline \(T = 2\) (player 1 proposes first, player 2 proposes last), find the SPE.

Solution. At stage 2 player 2 proposes and keeps everything: payoffs \((0, \delta)\). At stage 1 player 1 offers player 2 exactly \(\delta\) and keeps \(1 - \delta\); player 2 accepts. SPE payoffs \((1 - \delta, \delta)\): with an even deadline the last proposer has the advantage when \(\delta > \tfrac12\).

Verify the carrot-and-stick strategy for \(a - c = 1\), \(\delta = \tfrac12\), \(x = \tfrac25\).

Solution. Reward profit \(\tfrac18\); punishment profit (both at \(0.4\), price \(0.2\)) is \(0.08\). Value on the reward path \(V_R = \tfrac18 / (1 - \tfrac12) = 0.25\). Value at the start of a punishment phase with compliance \(V_P = 0.08 + \tfrac12 V_R = 0.205\). Reward phase: best deviation \(q = \tfrac38\) earns \(\tfrac{9}{64} \approx 0.1406\), then punishment: \(0.1406 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.205 = 0.243 < 0.25\). Punishment phase: best deviation against \(0.4\) is \(q = 0.3\), earning \(0.09\), then punishment restarts: \(0.09 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.205 = 0.1925 < 0.205\). No profitable one-shot deviation exists, so by the one-stage deviation principle the strategy is an SPE.

::: {.solution}
**M leads.** N answers $A \to X$, $B \to X$, $C \to Y$, so M compares $2$, $1$, $5$ and plays $C$. Outcome $(C, Y)$ with $(5, 3)$. Both gain over the static NE $(2, 2)$: the leader never does worse than a NE (it could replay $A$), and here the follower also gets more than its pure minmax $2$. **N leads.** M answers $X \to A$, $Y \to B$, and $Z \to B$ or $C$ (tie at $9$; the generous follower picks $B$, which gives N $4$ rather than $2$). N then compares $2$ ($X$), $4$ ($Y$), $4$ ($Z$). The tie is broken generously toward $Z$, which gives M $9$ instead of $7$. Outcome $(B, Z)$ with $(9, 4)$. Sequential play removes the coordination failure of the static game.
:::

From the course material:

::: {.exercise}
In the alternating-offer game with deadline $T = 2$ (player 1 proposes first, player 2 proposes last), find the SPE.
:::

::: {.solution}
At stage 2 player 2 proposes and keeps everything: payoffs $(0, \delta)$. At stage 1 player 1 offers player 2 exactly $\delta$ and keeps $1 - \delta$; player 2 accepts. SPE payoffs $(1 - \delta, \delta)$: with an even deadline the last proposer has the advantage when $\delta > \tfrac12$.
:::

::: {.exercise}
Verify the carrot-and-stick strategy for $a - c = 1$, $\delta = \tfrac12$, $x = \tfrac25$.
:::

::: {.solution}
Reward profit $\tfrac18$; punishment profit (both at $0.4$, price $0.2$) is $0.08$. Value on the reward path $V_R = \tfrac18 / (1 - \tfrac12) = 0.25$. Value at the start of a punishment phase with compliance $V_P = 0.08 + \tfrac12 V_R = 0.205$. **Reward phase:** best deviation $q = \tfrac38$ earns $\tfrac{9}{64} \approx 0.1406$, then punishment: $0.1406 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.205 = 0.243 < 0.25$. **Punishment phase:** best deviation against $0.4$ is $q = 0.3$, earning $0.09$, then punishment restarts: $0.09 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.205 = 0.1925 < 0.205$. No profitable one-shot deviation exists, so by the one-stage deviation principle the strategy is an SPE.
:::
